Pilot chapter

Where to Draw the Boundaries: Davis's Philosophy of Categorization

From It's pointless to argue about the definitions of words to the laws that govern what you have when you take ●●-many plus another ●●-many, 2012–2025

About this chapter. This is a pilot chapter for a book-length study of the blogging of Zack M. Davis: An Algorithmic Lucidity (AAL, at zackmdavis.net/blog), The Scintillating But Ultimately Untrue Thought (UUT, at unremediatedgender.space), and his posts on Less Wrong (LW). The reader is not assumed to have read any of it. Quotations are exact. Corpus citations link to the live posts; unpublished drafts and unlisted ("hidden") pages are identified as such. External sources were fetched and read for this chapter except where noted. A note on method and verification, and the list of works cited, come at the end.

1. Two sentences, seven years apart

In August 2012 Davis, taking a summer course on contemporary sexuality to satisfy a general-education requirement, reported how he had answered a workbook question asking how to define virginity:

It's pointless to argue about the definitions of words; once you know what behavior someone has engaged in, then calling it 'virginity' or 'non-virginity' doesn't give you more information

— "Contemporary," AAL, 7 August 2012

In April 2019 the same writer ended a 3,900-word essay on the philosophy of language with this instruction:

Do not ask whether there's a rule of rationality saying that you shouldn't call dolphins fish. Ask whether dolphins are fish.

— "Where to Draw the Boundaries?," AAL and LW, 13 April 2019

The two sentences are not quite contradictory. The 2012 remark makes the move Eliezer Yudkowsky had called dissolving a "disguised query": once you know the facts, the label adds nothing. The phrase comes from "A Human's Guide to Words," the sequence Yudkowsky published on Overcoming Bias in 2008, from which, by his own account, Davis learned most of his philosophy. The 2019 sentence comes out of the same tradition. But they are opposite in emphasis. The first treats arguments about category membership as a confusion to be dissolved. The second treats them as substantive questions with right answers. This chapter is about how Davis got from the first to the second, and whether the second is correct.

In outline, the story runs as follows. Starting in 2016, people in Davis's intellectual community increasingly answered his claims about transsexuality with some version of an argument popularized by Scott Alexander's 2014 essay "The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories": category boundaries are not facts, so there is nothing wrong with drawing woman to include trans women if doing so helps people. Davis spent roughly the next seven years answering it. The answer appeared first in polemical posts on his pseudonymous gender blog (2018). It became a run of deliberately depoliticized philosophy essays on Less Wrong (2019–2021), which drew in information theory, signaling games, and causal graphs. Finally it returned to its gender-political occasion in a long memoir (2023–2024) and a few late polemics (2024–2025). Davis came to call this the "Category War." In a 2021 comment he described it as the major project of my life for the previous forty months.

I will argue that, despite its sprawl, the project defends one distinction, stated more precisely each time: values may legitimately determine which features a category must help predict; given that choice, where the boundary falls is determined by the distribution of things in the world, not by what we wish. I call this the distinction between two kinds of value-dependence, after the title of one of Davis's unpublished drafts, which sketches the same point with the remark that if putting the blue points in a category doesn't reduce your entropy, then it's useless ("Two Kinds of Value-Dependence," UUT draft, UUT_content/drafts/two-kinds-of-value-dependence.md). It is correct. It is more important than most of his critics allowed, and his formal statement of it in 2021 is a genuine contribution, whose arithmetic I have checked. Davis attached a stronger thesis to it: that any departure from predictively optimal categories is "optimized for deception," and that appeals to consequences are therefore invalid in arguments about definitions. His mathematics does not establish this. His own most careful writing, much of it buried in comment threads and in admissions to his strongest critics, retreats from it toward a position that can be defended. That position is a norm of accounting rather than a law of thought: if you want a category that predicts worse, you owe your listeners an argument, made openly, for why the cost is worth paying.

The chapter follows the argument roughly in order: its inheritance (§2), its first polemical statement (§3), the core distinction and its game-theoretic complication (§4), the information-theoretic case that bad categories are deceptive (§5), and the late turn from clusters to causes (§6). An assessment comes next (§7). The last sections take up the argument as conduct, meaning the strategy of the "stealth reply" and the dispute with Yudkowsky that it served (§8), and conclude (§9). The empirical sexology on which Davis's gender applications depend, chiefly the Blanchard–Bailey–Lawrence two-type taxonomy of male-to-female transsexualism, is contested. A full study would examine it on its own terms. Here I treat it only where the logic of the categorization argument depends on it, which turns out to be less often than either Davis or his critics usually implied.

2. The inheritance: Yudkowsky, Alexander, and the academic counterpart

Davis's philosophy of categorization starts as exegesis. His canonical text is "A Human's Guide to Words." Its central technical claim is that a word is useful insofar as it compresses: having a short word for a conjunction of properties pays only when those properties occur together more often than chance would predict. Yudkowsky's toy example is the "wiggin," a made-up word for green-eyed black-haired people, and his conclusion is blunter than its later reception suggested:

If green-eyes and black hair have no greater than default probability to be found together, nor does any other property occur at greater than default probability along with them, then the word “wiggin” is a lie: The word claims that certain people are worth distinguishing as a group, but they’re not.

— Eliezer Yudkowsky, "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace" (2008)

The same essay concludes: the way to carve reality at its joints is to draw your boundaries around concentrations of unusually high probability density in Thingspace. In "Where to Draw the Boundary?" Yudkowsky uses the example that would become the emblem of Davis's whole campaign. In the original Less Wrong text, a speaker who insists his extensional list of "fish" cannot be wrong because it is "just a list" is told: Or you could stop playing nitwit games and admit that dolphins don't belong on the fish list.

Two features of this inheritance matter later. First, Yudkowsky had already described a bad category as a kind of lie. Davis's later claim that gerrymandered categories are "optimized for deception" therefore extends the source text rather than departing from it. Second, the dolphin example rests on a bare assertion, not an argument. Davis would eventually concede the point, calling Yudkowsky's original dismissal sloppy and flawed, and supplying the argument himself.

The antagonist text is Alexander's 2014 essay. It deserves a fairer summary than its opponents usually gave it, because it is two arguments, not one. Sections I–III argue that classification is relative to purpose. King Solomon, who cares whether a creature is hunted by boat or on horseback, is not wrong to file whales under dag rather than behemah, and category boundaries, like national borders, can be bad at fulfilling your goals in drawing them, but not bad in an absolute sense or factually incorrect. Alexander does not say anything goes: A category “fish” containing herring, dragonflies, and asteroids is going to be stupid. Section IV then turns to transgender people, and here the purposes in question change kind. Alexander has just admitted to hospital a trans man who, distressed at being biologically female, alternates between trying to kill himself and trying to cut off parts of his body. He concludes:

If I’m willing to accept an unexpected chunk of Turkey deep inside Syrian territory to honor some random dead guy – and I better, or else a platoon of Turkish special forces will want to have a word with me – then I ought to accept an unexpected man or two deep inside the conceptual boundaries of what would normally be considered female if it’ll save someone’s life. There’s no rule of rationality saying that I shouldn’t, and there are plenty of rules of human decency saying that I should.

— Scott Alexander, "The Categories Were Made For Man, Not Man For The Categories," Slate Star Codex, 21 November 2014

Everything in Davis's response can be read as an attempt to show that §IV does not follow from §§I–III. Solomon's "purpose" is a choice about which features to predict. The purpose of saving a patient's life is not a choice of that kind. It concerns the effect of a classification on the person classified. Alexander himself later conceded part of the point. After a long argument with Davis over Christmas 2019, he added a bracketed edit to the essay: In most cases plausible definitions will be limited to a few possibilities suggested by the territory, with worsening penalties as you deviate from these further.

Davis rarely situates his work in academic philosophy, so it is worth noting that the debate has an academic counterpart which states the opposing position more explicitly than Alexander does. Sally Haslanger's influential paper "Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We Want Them To Be?" (2000) distinguishes "conceptual," "descriptive," and "analytical" inquiries into a concept. It pursues the last, which asks what concept would best serve one's legitimate purposes, here feminist and antiracist ones. On that approach, she writes, the world by itself can’t tell us what gender is, or what race is; it is up to us to decide what in the world, if anything, they are. Haslanger is also candid about why one would attach a revised concept to the old word rather than coin a new one:

I think there are rhetorical advantages to using the terms ‘gender’, ‘man’ and ‘woman,’ and ‘race’ for the concepts I’ve defined, but if someone else is determined to have those terms, I’ll use different ones.

— Sally Haslanger, Noûs 34 (2000), p. 52

Davis did not engage this literature. A search of the whole corpus finds no mention of Haslanger or of "ameliorative" analysis. The academic philosophers of gender he does name, Kathleen Stock and Alex Byrne, both defenders of the definition he favors, appear only in passing. But Haslanger's frankness bears on his argument, as §7 will show. What he calls "piggybacking" on an existing word's meaning is exactly what she acknowledges, and her open avowal of the normative purpose is what he demands of the writers he criticizes.

3. The first statement (2018): subspaces, compression, and a conditional

Davis's earliest writings on the subject are complaints rather than theory. In 2016 he wrote a dialogue whose speaker confesses to going mad because the world does not understand that words mean things. The speaker is unsettled because I wasn't expecting people to redefine a top-20 noun out from under my feet. ("Prescription," AAL, 28 August 2016.) His account of the psychotic episodes of early 2017, written that June, places categorization at the center of the crisis. He had lost sleep over an alarming fraction of the smartest people I know being seemingly unwilling to publicly affirm the conjunction biological sex is a predictively useful category and categories should be predictively useful. The failure felt like the loss of a shared world: I thought I was safe; I thought that words meant the same thing to other people that they meant to me ("Memoirs of My Recent Madness, Part I," UUT, 19 June 2017). The theory that follows was written, in a fairly literal sense, to restore that world.

The first theoretical statement is a parable. "Blegg Mode" (UUT, 1 February 2018) retells Yudkowsky's factory of "bleggs" (blue, egg-shaped, furry, vanadium-cored objects) and "rubes" (red, cubical, palladium-cored ones). Into it Davis introduces red cubes that someone has sanded and dressed in blue fur. The narrator, bored on the sorting line, works out that a new category is worth having when there are cheap-to-detect features that correlate with more-expensive-to-detect features that are decision-relevant with respect to the agent's goals. A hand-drawn 2×2 table makes the point. Purple bleggs differ visibly but not in anything that matters. Palladium-bearing ordinary bleggs differ in something that matters but cannot be told apart. Only the "adapted" objects are both easy to spot and decision-relevant, and so a distinction that makes a difference.

Three weeks later came the long reply to Alexander, "The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions" (UUT, 23 February 2018). Davis first grants Alexander's relativity point, then denies that it supports Alexander's conclusion: The map is not the territory, and many very different kinds of maps can correspond to the territory in different ways—we have geographical maps, political maps, road maps, globes, &c.—but that doesn't mean no map is in error. The technical device that makes this possible, and that remains Davis's signature, is the subspace. Whales cluster with mammals in the subspace of dimensions on which mammals resemble one another, and with water-dwellers in another subspace. There is no contradiction: a vector can be close to one point in the x₁–x₂ plane and close to a different point in the x₃–x₄ plane. Choosing a subspace reflects interests. Once the subspace is chosen, though, clustering is not up to us. From this Davis draws a modest program for disputes over words:

Before shrugging and saying, "Well, this is a difference in values; nothing more to be said about it," we can talk about the detailed consequences of what is gained or lost by paying attention to some differences and ignoring others. That there exists an element of subjectivity in what you choose to pay attention to, doesn't negate the fact that there is a structured empirical reality to be described—and not all descriptions of it are equally compact.

Its application to sex and gender is more careful than its tone suggests, and it is explicitly conditional. Davis grants that a trans man who is perceived as a man without incongruity might be said to be a man in the sense of social gender, because perception is what such a category tracks. That, he says, is an empirical argument and not a redefinition: It works because and to the extent that transitioning actually works. His further claim is that most trans women in Western countries belong to a "late-onset," non-androphilic type that is psychologically male-typical, so that a self-identification criterion picks out a cluster that is specifically part of male cluster along most dimensions. That claim is avowedly conditional on the two-types hypothesis. Strip out the sexology and the philosophical claim that remains is narrower: whether trans women form a natural category with women depends on facts about trans women, and cannot be settled by appeal to the arbitrariness of definitions. That narrower claim is the one Davis spent the next five years defending.

The companion "Reply to The Unit of Caring on Adult Human Females" (UUT, 19 April 2018) adds an argument from circularity. A self-identification definition of woman has no base case: If all I know about women is that women are defined as people who identify as women, I can't use that definition to figure out which people are women. It is worth noting what Davis does not claim. He offers a trans-inclusive sense of the word that is non-circular, "adult human females, and also adult human males who have undergone interventions to resemble adult human females sufficiently closely so that they can be taken as such socially." And on the thought experiment of perfect bodily transition without psychological change, he concedes: Honestly, I lean towards Yes, but I can at least see the argument of someone who preferred not to use the word that way. His demand is for the narrower sense to remain sayable: the point is that it should be socially acceptable to describe reality using words.

The 2018 statement has an evident weakness, which its first serious critics found at once. The "cheap-to-detect features" heuristic of "Blegg Mode" looks like a criterion tailored to its application. When the parable was cross-posted to Less Wrong in March 2019, the commenter gjm said so plainly: It has the look, to me, of a principle that's constructed in order to reach a particular conclusion. He supplied counterexamples (isotopes, enantiomers, supernova types) in which categories were refined because the difference mattered, whether or not it was cheap to detect. Jessica Taylor added that an ideal Bayesian learner isn't going to assign special importance to observed features, as it treats the observations as causally downstream of the ontological reality rather than causally upstream of it. Davis conceded: Yeah, I might have been on the wrong track there. The concession matters for what came next, because the later theory drops observational cost from the definition of a good category and keeps it only as a reason for coordinating on simple membership tests (§4). Ozy Brennan's 2018 reply raised an objection that recurs throughout the reception: if differing trait distributions disqualify trans women from woman, then any group of women whose interests and personality traits, on average, observably differ from that of women as a whole ought to be classified as not actually women at all. Hence: By extension, lesbians are not women. Davis's 2018 post had anticipated a version of this objection. The answer, made rigorous only later, is that subclusters inside a cluster differ from points that belong to a different cluster. His unpublished long reply to Ozy shows him struggling to make that answer persuasive (§6).

4. The core distinction (2019): two kinds of value-dependence

"Where to Draw the Boundaries?" is the hinge of the corpus. Davis wrote it after he and several allies had spent the winter of 2018–2019 trying, without success, to get Yudkowsky to clarify publicly a Twitter thread of his. (The episode is narrated in §8.) He presented it on Less Wrong as pure philosophy with examples about dolphins and job titles. The speeches of its hypothetical interlocutor, it later emerged, weave in unattributed sentences from Alexander, Yudkowsky, and Rob Bensinger. The opening objector's speech includes, for example, You're not standing in defense of truth if you insist on a word, brought explicitly into question, being used with some particular meaning. That is a sentence of Yudkowsky's from November 2018.

The post's central claim is a single sentence of reply: Everything we identify as a joint is a joint not "because we care about it", but because it helps us think about the things we care about. It is developed into the distinction this chapter is about:

Which dimensions of Thingspace you bother paying attention to might depend on your values, and the clusters returned by your brain's similarity-detection algorithms might "split" or "collapse" according to which subspace you're looking at. But in order for your map to be useful in the service of your values, it needs to reflect the statistical structure of things in the territory—which depends on the territory, not your values.

A sequence of contrasts follows. One is between leaving mountains off a political map and leaving Everest off a geographic map because one's sister died on it. Another is between not calling a pill poison because poison is defined relative to human metabolism and not calling it poison for fear of a lawsuit. The third is between having a utility function defined over a statistical model's performance against specific real-world data and having one defined over features of the model itself. This third contrast is the deepest. It locates the confusion precisely: preferring a model because of what it does when you use it to predict is different from preferring a model because of what happens to you, or to others, when it is used.

Two arguments carry the weight. The first is a numerical example. Suppose there is a cluster of "Foos" near [1, 2, 3] and a cluster of "Quuxes" near [2, 4, 6], and someone redefines Foo to cover both while insisting that on seeing [2, _, _] they will still predict 4 and 6. Then, Davis observes, they are not really using the new concept. Someone who was would predict the new category's means, 3 and 4.5. The knowledge that "entities for which x₁≈2, also tend to have x₂≈4 and x₃≈6" needs to be represented somewhere in the one's mind in order to get the right answer. Redefinition therefore either changes your predictions, making them worse, or is merely verbal. That is a real dilemma, and it reappears in every later essay. The second is an information-theoretic gloss on "title inflation." If some low-paid jobs get the title "Vice President," the mutual information between title and pay falls from about 0.918 bits to about 0.252, and so all that's actually been accomplished is making language less useful. I have reproduced both figures. They are correct.

The post also contains the first version of an argument that Davis repeats more than any other, the argument from arbitrariness to indifference: If the choice were really arbitrary, you would be satisfied with the choice being made arbitrarily: by flipping a coin, or calling a random number generator. It is a good argument against a particular rhetorical posture, the one that appeals to arbitrariness to block an objection and then insists on a particular choice. As gjm had pointed out in the "Blegg Mode" thread a month earlier, it is a weaker argument against someone who says, without contradiction, that a choice is underdetermined by the data and is being made for stated non-epistemic reasons. Davis eventually accepted that framing. His complaint in 2019 was that the reasons were not being stated.

The Less Wrong reception split in a way that foreshadowed the next two years. Said Achmiz, in the thread's top-rated comment, praised the post's rare quality, like much of the Sequences, of the ideas it describes being utterly obvious—in retrospect. Wei Dai pressed the hardest constructive question: Since it doesn't seem to make sense to never use a word to point to a cluster in a "thin" subspace, what is your advice for when it's ok to do this or accept others doing this? Davis's answer was candid about the limit of his account: watch for clusters that hold only in a thin subspace and break apart in the dimensions relevant to the context, adding, I wish I had a rigorous reduction of what "relevant in the context" means, but I don't. Much depends on this admission. The distinction between the two kinds of value-dependence is clean in the abstract. In application, deciding which dimensions are "relevant" to a context is where the substantive disagreements lie, and that decision belongs to the first kind of value-dependence, the legitimate kind.

The Schelling complication

Davis's next essay complicates his own position, and does so to the advantage of his opponents. That is one reason to doubt gjm's suspicion that his principles were reverse-engineered from their conclusions. In "Schelling Categories, and Simple Membership Tests" (AAL and LW, 25 August 2019) he takes two distributions that are cleanly separable in forty dimensions but overlap heavily on each one alone. (This is the "univariate fallacy" of his June 2019 post: groups can overlap on every single measured trait and still be almost perfectly distinguishable when many traits are taken together.) He then adds a forty-first variable that is individually more diagnostic but has no bearing on the decisions at stake. Agents who must coordinate but can observe only one coordinate each may do best to define their shared category by that variable. So the right categories to use given constraints on communication and observation, might be different from the category boundaries you would draw from a "God's eye view". Legal adulthood is the example: everyone can compute an age cutoff, while "maturity" is illegible. The same post notes the cost of delegating classification to an authority, including the person classified. Such a test is furthermore subject to adversarial Goodhart: once membership confers benefits, people have reason to pass the test without having the properties it was meant to track.

Davis then turned this against his own side, in a post he introduced with evident self-awareness. "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point" (UUT, 6 October 2019) argues that when observers disagree about someone's sex and social life requires a single answer, the natural Schelling point is, "Just ask the person what gender they are, and that's what they are." He calls this an apparently novel argument in favor of the self-identity criterion. He also notes that only the extremes, pure chromosomal sex and pure self-report, are stable, because any line not drawn on a sufficiently sticky Schelling point will face constant attempts to push it up or down the slippery slope. In "More Schelling" (UUT, 22 December 2019) he uses Thomas Schelling's daylight-saving example to explain why the fight is about words rather than policies. Changing what a common word picks out imposes itself on all the downstream predictions and decisions people were already using that category/word for, decisions no one has catalogued in advance.

These essays are the most philosophically generous in the corpus, and the most consequential for evaluating it. They concede that something other than predictive fit, namely coordination, can legitimately shape category boundaries. They also identify a respectable reason to prefer self-identification that has nothing to do with sparing feelings. Davis did not always remember them. When critics later complained that his theory had a pretty gaping hole in your arguments that have something-or-other-to-do-with "but, like, coordination tho" (Raymond Arnold, January 2021), he could rightly point them to this post. Elsewhere, though, the polemics seldom mention it.

5. The deception thesis (2019–2021)

The parenthesis that closes the argument of "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" anticipates the next stage: (Oh, and if you're actually trying to optimize other people's models of the world, rather than the world itself—you could just lie, rather than playing clever category-gerrymandering mind games. It would be a lot simpler!) Over the following eighteen months Davis built an account of meaning and deception intended to show that this was more than a quip.

Meaning from signaling games

The foundation is the sender–receiver game of David Lewis and Brian Skyrms, which Davis presents in "Philosophy in the Darkest Timeline" (AAL and LW, 7 June 2020) as runnable Rust code wrapped in a bleak short story. Two agents with initially random dispositions, rewarded when the receiver acts appropriately to the state the sender observed, reliably converge on one of two arbitrary conventions. Davis's conclusion is the reductionist one: The meaning of a word "lives", not in the word/symbol/signal itself, but in the self-reinforcing network of correlations between the signal, the agents who use it, and the world. The account explains why meaning needs common interest. In "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!" (AAL and LW, 22 August 2020) Davis faces the difficulty it raises. If a predator firefly imitates a mate's flash and prey adapt to the new base rates, the flash is no longer misleading at equilibrium, so where is the deception? His answer follows Skyrms and Barrett. Propositional content is fixed in the part of the game where the interests of sender and receiver coincide, so deception is parasitic on a convention: you can't have a lie without some shared map for it to be a lie in.

This sounds like a detour from categorization. It is in fact the premise of the strongest form of Davis's thesis. If meanings are conventions maintained by usage, then redefining a word is not an act of private stipulation. It is an attempt to change a convention that others rely on. Whether that attempt misleads depends on whether listeners still decode the word under the old convention. "Algorithmic Intent" (AAL and LW, 13 July 2020) supplies the other premise: purposes may be attributed to a system on the basis of what its behavior is optimized to achieve, whether or not anyone consciously intends it. Combined, the two premises let Davis speak of categories that are "optimized for" deceiving without accusing anyone of lying in the ordinary sense. They also, as §8 argues, let him attribute motives on grounds that are very hard to check.

"Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception"

The capstone essay (AAL and LW, 8 January 2021; roughly 10,000 words) is Davis's most important work on the subject, and he was right to say in 2025 that it went underappreciated. He also identified the reason himself: I sacrificed readability by optimizing for comprehensiveness. It answers an interlocutor who has absorbed Alexander's border metaphor and wants to trade a little categorizational awkwardness for a lot of morality. The reply begins by refusing the metaphor: Countries aren't Bayesian networks. They just aren't. A category, on Davis's account, is a hypothesis, a probabilistic model that factorizes a joint distribution. The drawn "boundary" is just a picture of that model, so you can't "redraw the boundaries" associated with a communication signal without messing with the model that generates them, which means messing with your predictions about the real world.

The essay then makes its main technical advance. The information-theoretic framing of 2019 cannot explain why gerrymandering is bad, because a squiggly region that splits probability mass in half carries a bit just as a compact region does. What distinguishes them is that, when answers can be nearly right, compact categories leave smaller expected error. Learning whether a number from 1 to 8 is even or odd cuts the expected squared error only from 5.25 to 5. Learning whether it is in 1–4 or 5–8 cuts it to 1.25. In Davis's factory example, the natural blegg/rube/other classification reduces the expected squared error from about 13.63 to 1.38, while a gerrymandered "blegg*" region reduces it only to about 4.12. In an email of September 2020 that he reproduces in a memoir, he noted that this choice of measure departs from Yudkowsky's preference for the logarithmic score. I have independently re-derived all of these values from the setup the post states (see the note on method). Every one reproduces, including the 0.96 posterior and the squared-error-minus-revenue figures of −142.62 and −151.57. The one slip I found is a minor comparison of model sizes. Davis sets the joint table's 127 free parameters against the naive-Bayes model's 57 table entries, of which only 47 are free. The like-for-like comparison favors his point slightly more than the one he made.

The squared-error argument establishes this: given a metric on a feature space, a category system's predictive value is fixed by the data, and gerrymandered systems do worse. Davis is explicit about where values come in: How you should define words depends on what you want, but that's not the same as defining words any way you want. The poison example shows that value-laden categories (substances dangerous to human metabolism) are compact in one space and squiggly in another. That is fine, because the values chose the space.

From here Davis argues that the only plausible motive for a category that predicts worse in the relevant space is to make someone else's map worse to one's own advantage, or to make one's own map flattering. He calls these deception and wireheading. His example is a factory engineer who owns a stake in the supplier, which is paid more for each object the classifier labels a blegg. That engineer has a scoring function under which the squiggly boundary is optimal. The key sentence asserts an equivalence: there's no functional difference between saying "I reserve the right to lie p% of the time about whether something belongs to category C", and adopting a new, less-accurate category system that misclassifies p% of instances with respect to the old system. A section on mimicry follows, the most original part of the essay. A Batesian mimic is optimized to match the original in a subspace (wing markings) while differing elsewhere (toxicity). Mimicry is deceptive in contexts where the unmatched features matter and harmless where they do not, which gives the "duck test" its scope. Davis adds a caveat: Nondeceptive mimicry is fragile. The essay's real-world example is not gender but meat substitutes and the nineteenth-century laws that dyed margarine pink.

The essay ends, though, with a concession its rhetoric tends to bury. Of the animal-rights advocate who wants plant-based substitutes called meat to reduce suffering, Davis writes: I can't prove that that's not the right the answer to the decision problem of what verbal behavior to perform. The stakes are genuinely high. What he can say is that the hidden Bayesian structure of language and cognition makes no reference to the stakes, and departing from the structure extracts a price that isn't up to us. Read carefully, this is a weaker thesis than the title states. The mathematics shows that a less natural category has a price, measured in predictive error. It does not show that the price is never worth paying, and on reflection Davis does not claim that it does. The title's "optimized for deception" describes what such categories do, not what anyone intends. Even so understood, it holds only relative to a convention that listeners still use, a qualification §7 takes up.

The reception was small but substantive. John S. Wentworth, whose "natural abstraction" research program aims at the same target from AI theory, objected that "clusters in thingspace" is a metaphor. There isn't really a canonical "thingspace" with pre-defined features along the axes; figuring out what features to use is most of the problem from the start. The deeper notion, he argued, is conditional independence given summary statistics. Davis took this up. It becomes the causal turn of §6. Years later Wei Dai offered the most pointed counterexample: a "neural fireplace" that induces a hallucination of a fire, and that people might reasonably call a fireplace because the experience is what they care about. He then suggested that "trans women are women" isn't necessarily motivated by deception of which sex cluster someone belongs to, but instead a signal of local values. Davis answered that people who think neural fireplaces are fireplaces should be arguing for that on the merits. That is the disclosure norm again, not the deception thesis.

The equivalence between lying and recategorizing drew a precise objection from the commenter tslarm in 2025: If I'm honest about the boundaries of my new category system, how is this deceptive? Davis's reply concedes the structure of the problem. Whether recategorizing deceives depends on what a label is construed to mean canonically, which is the puzzle of "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!". His most rigorous engagement with the formal literature, the 2021 "Comment on 'Deception as Cooperation'," sets out the strongest version of the opposing view and then holds his ground in a narrowed form. Following Manolo Martínez, Davis treats signaling as the joint construction of a rate–distortion-optimal channel whose shape reflects both parties' payoffs. Seen through that frame, he observes, a public health agency that shades its advice for the sake of outcomes is just doing straightforwardly correct consequentialist optimization of the information channel between states of the world, and actions. The frame also makes it look antagonistic to single out one codeword in a shared code and call it deceptive, as if a word could be objectively "wrong." Davis does not retreat from that claim: I am, ultimately, willing to bite this bullet. But what he keeps is more modest than the 2021 title. He grants that agents have different interests in where boundaries fall and drops the insistence that the result be called "lying." What is left is a demand to discriminate: we can still strive to notice the difference between maps optimized to reflect decision-relevant aspects of territory, and maps optimized to control other agents' decisions.

6. From clusters to causes (2021–2025)

The third phase begins with a public retraction. In 2016 Davis had written, against Ozy Brennan, that Summarizing correlations is the entire point of making a taxonomy. When the survey researcher and blogger known as tailcalled objected in 2021, Davis replied in a post titled "You Are Right and I Was Wrong" (UUT, 1 February 2021): But as Tailcalled's response points out, this is just wrong! Different causal theories can generate the same correlations in a particular set of observations, while still making meaningfully and drastically different claims about the world. His example is a Chicago-only taxonomy of "basketball/red" and "football/blue" athletes. It compresses the observations, but it is fragile because jersey color does not cause the sport. A category, on this revised view, earns its keep by tracking causal structure that generalizes.

"Blood Is Thicker Than Water 🐬" (AAL and LW, 27 September 2021) applies the revision to dolphins. Nate Soares, MIRI's executive director, had tweeted (as Davis quotes him) that The definitional gynmastics [sic] required to believe that dolphins aren't fish are staggering. Davis had first replied with a short, mock-innocent post. Three months later he gave the argument in full. Phylogeny is not an aesthetic preference, because evolution actually happened. The genome sits at the root of the causal graph of an organism's traits, so conditioning on ancestry makes many traits conditionally independent, and that conditional independence is what makes a category useful in the first place. The cluster picture is thereby demoted from foundation to illustration: "There's conditional independence structure in the causal graph" is a meaningfully deeper explanation than "There's a cluster in configuration space". The title states the conclusion: Blood is thicker than water (that is, the similarities induced by shared blood are a thicker subspace of configuration space than the similarities induced by living in the water). The post also corrects a piece of folk history Soares had relied on. Aristotle, who knew nothing of evolution, had already set cetaceans apart from fish, as Davis shows with a quotation I have verified against the Thompson translation of the History of Animals.

The causal turn makes Davis's view a near relation of the most influential account of natural kinds in recent philosophy of science, Richard Boyd's "homeostatic property clusters." On that account, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarizes it, kinds are families of such properties that are contingently clustered in nature held together by underlying mechanisms. Davis does not cite Boyd. As far as I can tell he reached the position independently, by way of Judea Pearl's causal graphs, a suggestion from tailcalled, and Wentworth's natural abstractions (the post thanks both), which is some evidence that it is the natural destination of the reasoning. His late account of definitions converges just as closely with a classic of the philosophy of language. A dictionary definition is just a convenient pointer, he wrote in the reply to Soares, to help people pick out "the same" natural abstraction in their own world-model. This is the Kripke–Putnam view that descriptions fix the reference of a kind term without giving its meaning. When Davis defers to encyclopedists on alligators and crocodiles in "Blood Is Thicker," he is practicing what Putnam called the division of linguistic labor, under which an ordinary speaker's reference is fixed not by their own knowledge or beliefs, but rather by the knowledge of certain experts to whom that individual defers. That the parallels are unacknowledged is not to Davis's discredit. His readers would have been better served had they been drawn, if only because the academic literature has already worked through objections he meets for the first time in comment threads.

The causal turn also clarifies what Davis thinks sex is. "Beyond the Binary" (UUT, 12 December 2023) treats definition by gametes or chromosomes as a simple membership test, the analogue of a never-flipped first bit in a family of mostly-zeros and mostly-ones bitstrings: Discrete features make for better word definitions than high-dimensional statistical regularities, even if most of the everyday inferential utility of using the word comes from the high-dimensional statistical correlates. The essay is notable for turning on both camps. Against trans advocates it argues that if the discrete markers are removed, there's nothing left for the words to attach to but mere statistical tendencies—that is, stereotypes. Against the gender-critical claim that sex is just about genitals and chromosomes and gamete size, it argues that this cannot explain why anyone cares about single-sex spaces. It closes on an admission. The view seems to make masculine women less "real" women, a consequence the author's younger, antisexist self would have resisted, and he hopes the map can describe outliers rather than being shot, but: I couldn't then have explained how that would work mathematically—and I still can't. Let me know if you figure it out.

That admission is to Davis's credit. It also marks the point where Ozy's 2018 objection still bites. The long draft reply to Ozy, which survives unpublished in the blog's repository, shows Davis repeatedly reaching for the answer and leaving placeholders ([TODO: address that "non-exclusively-androphilic" does exclude lesbians]). Its most interesting passage concedes that In all philosophical strictness, I think I agree. That is, the "actual category" an event organizer wants is not sex but some function of traits. He then argues that sex wins anyway, as a cheap and agreed proxy, which is the Schelling argument again (UUT draft, "I Mean, Yes, I Agree That Man Should Allocate Some More Categories, But," UUT_content/drafts/i-mean-yes-i-agree-that-man-should-allocate-some-more-categories-but.md). The causal account supplies, in my reconstruction, the answer the draft gropes for: lesbians differ from other women in a few dimensions, through a cause (sexual orientation) that leaves the sex-linked generative structure in place. On the two-type hypothesis, the bulk of trans women differ from women across that whole structure. But that answer is only as strong as the hypothesis, and so the categorization argument, at the point where it touches the contested question, leans on the contested science.

The latest essays on the theme, written after the Category War had formally ended (§8), restate the theory against new targets. "Prediction Markets Are Not a Drop-In Replacement for Concepts" (UUT, 21 December 2024) answers Yudkowsky's suggestion, in a comment on Davis's blog, that once prediction markets supply all available information, nothing remains to be gained from laws that treat the sexes differently. Its point is that traders in such a market would themselves use categories, because there contingently do happen to be approximate conditional independence relationships in the world. "A Generic Ontological Finickiness" (UUT, 14 September 2025) rebuts Yudkowsky's suggestion that objections to misclassification are rationalizations reached for after the fact. It ends with the plainest statement of the theory's normative core: Maybe human beings don't always, inherently, consistently care about exact descriptive accuracy—but rationalists should.

7. Assessment

I can now state what Davis established, what he did not, and where his critics were right.

What holds

The distinction itself. In its general form the thesis that values pick the space and the metric while the data fix the clusters is standard in statistics and machine learning. Its application to disputes over words is valuable precisely because the popular "categories are made for man" position runs the two together. Alexander's essay goes from a premise about the first kind of value-dependence (Solomon's interests determine which features matter) to a conclusion that needs the second kind (a classification may be chosen for its effects on the classified). Davis was right that the border metaphor hides the move, because borders really are chosen for their effects on the people bordered, while concepts used for inference are not. That Alexander's own 2019 edit concedes that plausible definitions are limited by the territory shows how much of the original rhetoric could not be sustained.

The dilemma of redefinition. The Foo example's dilemma deserves to be better known. Either a redefinition changes the redefiner's predictions, and makes them worse, or it is a change in verbal behavior that leaves the old concept doing the inferential work unnamed. Many disputes over words are, on inspection, of the second kind, and naming that fact clarifies them.

The piggyback diagnosis. In "Unnatural Categories" Davis argues that precisely because words don't have intrinsic meanings, there's no reason to want to redefine an existing word, except to piggyback off the meaning people are already using that signal for. Critics took this as uncharitable mind-reading. Haslanger's frank acknowledgment of the "rhetorical advantages" of the existing terms shows that it describes, accurately, a strategy that ameliorative theorists themselves avow. And in "Challenges to Yudkowsky's Pronoun Reform Proposal" (UUT, 13 March 2022) Davis applies the diagnosis to pronouns with real force: if she and he were just two alternative third-person pronouns that could be used interchangeably with no difference in meaning, with the only function of the distinction being collision-avoidance, then there would be no reason to care which one someone used. The redefinition is desired because of the meaning it claims not to carry. That is a real inconsistency in the position he targets, and he identified it.

The mathematics. Davis's formal examples are correct. The move from bits to squared error is a real insight about why compactness matters. It resembles, so far as I recall (I have not checked the psychology literature for this chapter), measures such as "category utility" used in cognitive science. One measure he uses is idiosyncratic. In "Schelling Categories," the ≈ 1.18 bits you can get from any one observation is a mixture-weighted average of absolute log-likelihood ratios, not the standard mutual information, which is about 0.124 bits per variable against 0.189 for the special variable. The comparison he needs goes the same way under either measure, so the conclusion stands.

What does not hold

The invalidity of appeals to consequences. In "Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist" (AAL and LW, 14 October 2019) Davis argues that Appeals to consequences are invalid because they represent a map–territory confusion. In "Algorithmic Intent" he declares that as an aspiring rationalist he is not allowed to care whether a description might be socially harmful. As a rule for belief this is sound: what is true does not depend on what it would be good to believe. As a rule for definitions it is ambiguous, and on one reading it contradicts the two-kinds distinction, which concedes that the space a definition answers to is chosen by values. The commenter TAG put the problem in one line: Why are (b) and (d) not exceptions to your thesis, already? TAG's (b) and (d) were two of the answers Davis had said he could give to any proposed non-predictive use of language, "that's coordination" and "that's a self-fulfilling prophecy." TAG's point was that both are uses other than prediction. Raymond Arnold suggested that the defensible version is rule-consequentialist: the problem is with appeals to shortsighted consequences. Abram Demski, Davis's ablest defender, conceded the point in structure even while defending the norm: My argument in support of Zack's argument appeals to consequences, but does so in service of the normative question of whether a community of truth-seekers should adopt norms against appeals to consequences. Davis himself, in January 2021, privately accepted Demski's deeper objection that epistemic norms are justified by their instrumental benefits, and he recorded it in his memoir: In that light, it made sense to regard "have accurate beliefs" as merely a convergent instrumental subgoal, rather than what rationality is about—as sacrilegious as that felt to type. Once that is granted, "appeals to consequences are invalid" cannot be a law of thought. It must be a norm, justified by the consequences of having it. Such a norm can be a good one (§9), but it has exceptions, and arguments about where those exceptions lie are themselves appeals to consequences.

The identity of recategorization and lying. The equivalence holds where the listener decodes the word under the old convention, and Davis's own signaling theory shows that it fails where the convention has actually changed and everyone knows it. So the deception charge is strongest in exactly the case his pronoun essay argues is the real one: a convention still entrenched in the listeners' actual usage (Declaring a "normative" meaning on your Facebook wall doesn't rewrite the actual meaning encoded in the brains of 370 million English speakers.). It weakens as usage actually shifts. That is a defensible, empirical claim about present usage. It is not the timeless claim the title "Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception" appears to make. Davis's engagement with Martínez, which no longer insists on the word "lying," implicitly acknowledges the difference.

The words–thoughts gap. The most sustained critic, gjm, argued over several years that language is used for more than prediction, that speech and private cognition need not share an ontology, and that in shallow social interaction a gendered word may well be most informative about how someone wishes to be treated. He concluded: I see no reason why language-use should be optimized purely for prediction, because the actual uses of language are broader than that. Davis's answer was not an argument but an avowal: But—don't you want the language you speak to your friends to be the same as the language you use to organize your own thoughts? How can you accept a wall between the world you see, and the world you're allowed to talk about? Doesn't your soul die a little bit? The avowal is moving, and in my judgment it states a real value, the value of a community whose public speech one can take at face value. But it is a value, not a theorem of the Bayesian structure of cognition. Davis is at his best when he presents it as one.

Interactive kinds. Davis repeatedly flags that his theory is simplest when causation runs only from territory to map. Self-fulfilling categories are a complication he says he does not yet understand (comment of 13 December 2020 in the "Where to Draw" thread). This is a larger gap than he allowed. Ian Hacking's "interactive kinds," classifications that change the people classified, are the standard case in the philosophy of social science. Social gender is close to a paradigm instance: being treated as a woman changes how one is treated, which changes the data. Davis's reply to Ozy's money analogy ("Reply to Ozymandias on Fully Consensual Gender," UUT, 20 December 2019) is clever. Gresham's law predicts that a convention requiring defaced Canadian dollars to be honored as American ones will produce private discounting. But it addresses feasibility, not legitimacy. His own conclusion, that fully consensual gender is a coherent position. That doesn't make it feasible., is a claim about social dynamics that the theory of categorization neither supports nor refutes.

The critics who were wrong, and the one who was most nearly right

Not all criticism deserves equal weight. The 2023 r/SneerClub thread on "The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions" was mostly mockery (one commenter reported skimming most of the post), though one comment made two points worth answering. First, the cost-aggregation argument (many small costs to many speakers) could be used against any recognition claim, including racial ones. Davis's 2018 essay raises a racial analogy itself, in its discussion of women's spaces, with a remark about white people who don't feel comfortable sharing water fountains with black people. It does not answer the aggregation version beyond declining to draw a policy conclusion. Second, the analysis questions whether trans women form a homogeneous category without asking the same of cis women: caring about the utility of man or woman is predicated on believing that these categories have any use, should have any use. The first point is fair: the aggregation argument alone settles nothing, as Davis's own disclaimers suggest. The second misunderstands his position, which is precisely a claim that those categories have use, but it correctly identifies where the burden of proof falls.

The critic most nearly right, in my judgment, is the one Davis found most persuasive. In the comment thread on "Blood Is Thicker," Alexander himself (commenting as Yvain) posed a thought experiment: after a billion more years of convergent evolution whales might be more like fish in 99% of ways. Between now and then, he asked, might there be a long period in which you're allowed to categorize them however you want based on which is more useful for you. If the latter, what makes you think we're not in that period right now? Davis's answer, No, of course not: we want categories that give useful information., concedes the principle. What remains is an empirical disagreement about where the present case falls. That is the correct place for the disagreement, and it is where Davis's theory, at its best, says it belongs. It is also where Davis had wanted it to be from the start: I think assertions like "trans women are women" and similar need to be argued for on the empirical merits; you can't just define them to be true.

8. The argument as conduct: stealth replies and the Category War

The philosophy cannot be fully assessed apart from how it was deployed, because Davis himself made deployment part of the subject. Three features stand out.

The stealth reply. The Less Wrong essays were written to be politically clean, and their political occasion was concealed. On his gender blog Davis linked "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" with an arch disclaimer that it doesn't have anything to do with the topic focuses of this blog. Twenty months later, in a "containment thread" for discussing his motives, he disclosed that the essay was intended as a "stealth reply" (quoting/paraphrasing without attribution in order to reply to the philosophical substance while eliding the political context) to Alexander and Yudkowsky. In the 2023 memoir he explains the strategy. Writing about dolphins would let reputable people endorse the philosophy without being seen to take a side on gender. He also acknowledges its cost: I could see a case that it was unfair of me to include political subtext and then only expect people to engage with the politically clean text. In the "Blegg Mode" thread he had already conceded that delusionally expecting to get away with it is trying to have one's cake and eat it, too.

The strategy had a defensible rationale. The philosophical point really is general, and its truth does not depend on its occasion. In the memoir Davis puts it as a general point about how language itself works that has nothing to do with gender. Benjamin Hoffman, defending the parable, argued that treating such a post as political concedes that the Rationalist project is so doomed that an attempt to correct a politically motivated epistemic error via an otherwise entirely depoliticized fictional example in a specifically Rationalist space is construed as a political act. But the strategy also had a cost the theory itself predicts. Readers who sensed the subtext suspected exactly the motivated principle-construction gjm alleged. Readers who did not could not see why the questions mattered, and Arnold called the post's closing paragraphs, on wishing that reality were otherwise, a meandering vagueblog. An abstraction chosen to hide its motivating application is, in Davis's own terms, a map optimized for the reader's reaction rather than for the territory. The memoir's admission of unfairness, quoted above, comes close to saying so. David Hornbein's verdict on the original post gives the strategy its best defense. He calls it crushingly well-argued, and he thinks it significant that write a nigh-irrefutable argument about philosophy of language, in order to strike at the heart of the substantive disagreement which provoked the social conflict could work as a social move in that community at all.

The dispute with Yudkowsky. According to the memoir "A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning" (UUT, 15 July 2023), the immediate provocation was a November 2018 Twitter thread. In it Yudkowsky declared that asking for different pronouns Is. Not. Lying. and that you're not standing in defense of truth if you insist on a word, brought explicitly into question, being used with some particular meaning. (The tweets are quoted here as quoted by Davis; X/Twitter is login-walled and I have not read them directly.) Davis read the second sentence as a retraction, under political pressure, of the 2008 Sequence. With a small group of allies he spent the next five months writing to Yudkowsky privately, in vain. He reports the outcome of April 2019 bleakly: It seemed that the Category War was over, and we lost. According to the sequel, "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them" (UUT, 30 December 2023), the war ended in September 2020. After a pair of furious emails ("Your heartbroken student"), Yudkowsky published a Facebook clarification. Davis quotes it as including the line you are being the bad guy if you try to shut down that conversation by saying that "I can define the word 'woman' any way I want". (Facebook is also login-walled; this too is Davis's quotation.) Davis counted it, with reservations, as the ex cathedra statement he had sought.

On the narrow philosophical question, then, the dispute ended with the position Davis had defended conceded by the figure whose authority he cared about. It is worth recording what the concession did and did not cover. It conceded that categories are not exempt from accuracy because they concern gender. It did not concede any application: that trans women are not women in this or that sense, or that the two-type taxonomy is true. Davis was careful about this distinction at the time. In the September 2020 email he insisted that his syllogism (you cannot define any noun any way you want; woman is a noun; therefore you cannot define woman any way you want) is totally compatible with the claim that trans women are women, and trans men are men, and nonbinary people are nonbinary! That sentence is the best short statement of the position this chapter defends as correct. The dispute's later history, chiefly Yudkowsky's 2021 pronoun proposal and Davis's reply, belongs to a study of the Yudkowsky quarrel, but it shows the same pattern. Davis's reply grants the proposed policy and attacks only its rationale, and it insists that Policy debates should not appear one-sided.

Motive attribution. The least attractive feature of the campaign is how readily Davis attributed bad faith, and here the theory of "algorithmic intent" did him no favors. An unpublished draft reply to Soares, preserved as an unlisted page on the gender blog, goes further than anything Davis published. It demands that Soares condemn Alexander's sentences as a complete betrayal of everything we allegedly stand for, adding: And if you can't do it, then you're a fraud. ("Dolphin War!! 🐬," hidden page, unremediatedgender.space/ancillary/dolphin-war/.) The draft attributes Alexander's whale-is-fish argument to the political need to reach a predetermined conclusion, while acknowledging, in the text of a link, that we have a terrible track record in guessing Scott's motives. Soares's public reply in the Less Wrong thread is evidence against the theory. He reported that the thread was not a veiled attempt to discuss gender issues, offered the fragment of his argument he did endorse (be wary of those who say they are here to refine your concepts, for sometimes they are here to steal them), and listed object-level reasons for his change of view. Two other commenters weakened the premise of Davis's suspicion. Vaniver showed, using divergence-time estimates from timetree.org, that the 2008 fish list was itself poor phylogenetics, since guppies share a more recent common ancestor with dolphins than with sharks: it's that, as far as I can tell, it's bad phylogenetics. And cata pointed out that the political mapping might run the other way: For me, "dolphins are fish because they swim and look fishy" is associated with a "common sense clusters" classification strategy, which also gives a traditional common-sense idea of who is a man and who is a woman.

That last remark goes deep. Davis's theory licenses surface-cluster categories (social gender as perceived, the "duck test" for mimics) wherever the unmatched features are irrelevant to the context. In different essays he invokes both the perceptual category (where a passing trans woman is a woman) and the causal one (where she is not). The theory is consistent about this, because it lets context select the space. That is exactly why it cannot, by itself, decide disputes in which the relevant context is what is in dispute. Davis apologized in the thread for the escalation that followed (I regret letting my emotions get the better of me. I apologize.), and the published reply to Soares is ironic rather than accusatory. But the pattern recurs in the memoirs. The attributions of "algorithmic" dishonesty may well be right in particular cases. They are not, in practice, the kind of claim his theory of evidence would let him hold with the confidence he expresses.

One further case tests whether Davis applies his principles to himself. His 2024 essay "'Deep Learning' Is Function Approximation" (AAL, 21 March 2024) systematically renames machine-learning terms to strip their connotations: (Some authors call these "deep neural networks" for some reason, but I like my name better.) On its face this is the very move he condemns, redefining words for their effects on readers. But his theory distinguishes the cases. Renaming to remove inferences the evidence does not warrant ("training" suggesting an animal that might turn on its masters) improves the map, while renaming to import inferences it does not warrant degrades it. The test is again empirical: which inferences are warranted. That is consistent, and it confirms that what the theory supplies is a criterion and a burden of proof, not verdicts.

9. Conclusion: a law of thought, or a norm of accounting?

Davis began by holding that arguments about definitions were pointless, because the facts are what matter. He ended by holding that arguments about definitions are arguments about facts in disguise: about which features cluster, which causes generate which traits, and which inferences a word licenses in the heads of the people who use it. Seen that way, the two positions are one. Both refuse to let a verbal choice substitute for an empirical claim. What changed between 2012 and 2019 was that Davis met people using the doctrine that definitions are arbitrary to block an empirical claim rather than dissolve a verbal one. The work that followed, from the subspace move through the squared-error calculation to the causal graph, is an unusually sustained piece of amateur philosophy. Its central distinction deserves a place in any serious discussion of how to argue about words.

The ambition Davis attached to it, to show that departures from predictively optimal categories are not merely costly but illegitimate, a kind of lying, did not succeed, and his own best work shows why. The mathematics measures the price of a category. It cannot say whether the price is worth paying, and Davis says as much when he writes that he can't prove that that's not the right the answer to the decision problem of what verbal behavior to perform. What survives is a norm, and I think a good one. If you want a word to pick out something other than the cluster it now names, say so; give the reason; acknowledge what is lost, and to whom. Do not present a choice as a discovery, or a discovery as a choice. In an unpublished outline Davis put it in a single line: It helps to be explicit about when you're being normative, and when you think you're describing the territory. On the evidence of the texts, that is the lesson the Category War established. And with Yudkowsky's 2020 clarification, his community, to the extent it can be said to concede anything, conceded it.

Whether the norm favors one side of the gender dispute is a separate question. Davis himself sometimes answered it in the negative: the norm is "totally compatible" with trans-inclusive language, provided that language is argued for on the merits. The application of the norm depends on what the merits turn out to be. That is a question for sexology and social science, not for the philosophy of categorization. Davis's achievement was to show, more precisely than anyone in his community had, why it cannot be avoided.
