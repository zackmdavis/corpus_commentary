### Aiming for Convergence Is Like Discouraging Betting
- Path: `AAL_content/2023/aiming-for-convergence-is-like-discouraging-betting.md` · 2023-01-31 · philosophy (epistemology) · 3396 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2023/Jan/aiming-for-convergence-is-like-discouraging-betting/
- Summary: LW-first. Against Duncan Sabien's Fifth Guideline ("Aim for convergence on truth, and behave as if your interlocutors are also aiming for convergence on truth"). Prediction markets converge because traders profit from divergences; forums converge because commenters win status by being less wrong than the discussion. "Every trade is an expression of the belief that your counterparty is _not_ aiming to converge." "Disagreement is disrespect." Bayesian superintelligences wouldn't bet, but bounded agents become less wrong by sticking necks out. On "good faith"/"defection": "_I don't know what any of those words mean_"; discourse shouldn't be a Prisoner's Dilemma; good faith ≠ cooperativeness (honorable foe vs. fair-weather friend). Hypothetical candid reply ("raise my status at the expense of yours") is _good faith_; "_politeness is bad faith_." Collaborative vs. competitive truth-seeking: "_they're not actually different things_."
- Themes: discourse-norms, sabien, prediction-markets, good-faith, collaborative-truth-seeking, politeness, competition
- Xrefs: AAL 2023 "Physicist Motors," "Assume Bad Faith"; LW 2023 lw-only posts on Sabien/moderation (check LW_content/2023).
- Quotes:
> When market prices converge on the truth, it's _because_ traders can only make money by looking for divergences where their beliefs are more accurate than the market's.
> Every trade is an expression of the belief that your counterparty is _not_ aiming to converge on the correct probability—that there's a sucker at every table, and that _this time it isn't you_.
> clarifying real disagreements is more valuable than steering towards fake agreement. Every bet and every disagreement is the result of _someone's_ failure. But the only way out is through.
> But, normatively, rationalist discourse shouldn't be a Prisoner's Dilemma-like situation at all.
> I consider this a _good faith_ reply. It's certainly not a polite thing to say. But _politeness is bad faith_.
> In slogan form, you could perhaps say that I don't believe in collaborative truth-seeking; I believe in competitive truth-seeking. But I don't like that slogan, because in my ontology, _they're not actually different things_.

### Conflict Theory of Bounded Distrust

Pinker–Nowak–Lee indirect-speech bribery: corrupt and honest cops want different things. Regime "revenue enhancements": supporters vs. dissidents (who need common knowledge, requiring unobfuscated language). Intent irrelevant (firefly predator).
- Themes: conflict-vs-mistake, bounded-distrust, scott-alexander, indirect-speech, common-knowledge, deception-without-intent
- Quotes:

### "Rationalist Discourse" Is Like "Physicist Motors"
- Path: `AAL_content/2023/rationalist-discourse-is-like-physicist-motors.md` · 2023-02-25 · philosophy (discourse) · 2841 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2023/Feb/rationalist-discourse-is-like-physicist-motors/
- Summary: LW-first. Guideline lists (Sabien, Bensinger's "Elements") have "the wrong type signature for being '_the_ basics'"; the basics are laws of probability that explain which guidelines are good. Debate/adversarial court as a discourse algorithm countering rationalization (cf. GANs); its flaws; Aumann/Hanson unpredictable disagreement makes predictable disagreement "crazy." Yudkowsky's lament that readers got "a vague spirit of how to reason and argue." Bensinger's elements 1–8 lawful; #9 "Goodwill" is taste, not law; footnote "role-playing being on the same side" contradicts Non-Deception = "concern trolling." Rationalization is a feature for persuasion; factions warring over the shared map; a "rationalist" faction claiming only to want truth — "_anyone could just say that_." Rationalism's value is as a field of study that can "_explain why it makes sense that people don't like concern trolling_."
- Themes: discourse-norms, bensinger, sabien, debate-algorithm, concern-trolling, rationalism-as-faction, law-vs-toolbox
- Quotes:
> But there's a reason for that: a list of guidelines has the wrong type signature for being "_the_ basics".
> Other intellectual communities have a name for the behavior of role-playing being on the same side as people you disagree with: they call it ["concern trolling"](https://geekfeminism.fandom.com/wiki/Concern_troll), and they think it's a _bad_ thing.
> If persuading others is the real goal, then rationalization is _not_ irrational; it's just dishonest.
> You _do_ have interests, and if you can fool yourself into thinking that you don't, your competitors are unlikely to fall for it. Even if your claim to only want Society's shared map to reflect the territory were true—which it isn't—_anyone could just say that_.
> That if something called "rationalism" is to have any value whatsoever, it's as the _field of study_ that can do things like _explain why it makes sense that people don't like concern trolling_.

### Bayesian Networks Aren't Necessarily Causal
- Path: `AAL_content/2023/bayesian-networks-arent-necessarily-causal.md` · 2023-05-13 · mathematics (statistics, Bayes-structure of the universe) · 2477 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2023/May/bayesian-networks-arent-necessarily-causal/
- Summary: LW-first. Second-person exercise: a casual formal-epistemology fan applies Koller–Friedman Algorithm 3.2 (minimal I-map given an ordering) to a rain/sprinkler/wet/slippery joint distribution; with the ordering wet-first gets a "crazy" network (wet → rain); with rain-first, the "true" one. Both encode the same joint distribution (same predictions); they differ only under interventions (do-operator: P(rain | do(¬wet)) ≈ 0.032 vs 1/4). Ends with "slight philosophical discomfort" that intervention — "almost anthropomorphic" — is integral to causality.
- Themes: causality, bayesian-networks, interventions, philosophy-of-science, self-correction
- Xrefs: AAL 2021 "Blood Is Thicker Than Water" and UUT 2022 two-type DAG (which lean on causal readings of graphs).
- Quotes:
> The two networks represent the same predictions about the world recorded in your family archives, but starkly different predictions about nearby _possible_ worlds—about what _would_ happen if some of the factors underlying the world were to change.
> You don't like the idea of forced change, of intervention, being so integral to such a seemingly basic notion as causality. It feels almost anthropomorphic

### Assume Bad Faith
- Path: `AAL_content/2023/assume-bad-faith.md` · 2023-08-25 · philosophy (discourse) · 2250 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2023/Aug/assume-bad-faith/
- Summary: LW-first. Bad faith (Wikipedia) = apparent reasons ≠ real reasons, distinct from malign intent (uniformed soldier shooting you is in good faith). Hidden motives are ubiquitous (Elephant in the Brain); persistent "good faith disagreements" are "_deeply weird_ at best, and probably just fake" — honest disagreements converge quickly (concert date; physics problem). Belief in persistent good-faith disagreement is itself in bad faith. Bias vs. deception "_uninteresting_" for information transmission (rose-colored glasses), except deception resists harder. Two strategies: stick to the object level (motives off-topic, not presumed honest), or "full-contact psychoanalysis" (negotiation factoring pseudo-disagreements into conflicts; Examplene/wetlands example). Default object-level, psychoanalysis against stonewalling. "There's always an angle; the question is which one."
- Themes: bad-faith, hidden-motives, disagreement, bias-vs-deception, full-contact-psychoanalysis, conflict-vs-disagreement
- Xrefs: AAL 2022 "Plea Bargaining"; AAL 2023 "Aiming for Convergence," "Conflict Theory of Bounded Distrust."
- Quotes:
> That is, bad faith is when someone's apparent reasons for doing something aren't the same as the real reasons. This is distinct from malign intent.
> I claim that this supposedly ordinary state of affairs is _deeply weird_ at best, and probably just fake.
> but a more fundamental reply is that from the standpoint of [modeling information transmission](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/fmA2GJwZzYtkrAKYJ/algorithms-of-deception), the difference between bias and deception is _uninteresting_
> the main difference I'm proposing is that speakers' intentions be regarded as _off-topic_ rather than presumed to be honest.
> There's always an angle; the question is which one.
