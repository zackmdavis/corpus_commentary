# Review of Chapter 1: The Apprentice

The chapter’s strongest finding is that Davis’s early writing documents attempts to restrict his own intellectual and political activity. The passages from “Egoism as Defense,” “Speaking of Addiction,” “Clearness,” and “Retirement” establish this more securely than retrospective autobiography alone could. The qualification about small groups in “Counterfactual Social Thought” is especially valuable: it complicates an easy story of renunciation followed by relapse.

The larger claim—unchanged commitments followed by a change of “license”—needs substantial qualification. The chapter sometimes treats resemblance as intellectual descent, early formulations as the completed philosophy, and public silence as evidence of private disinterest. Its interpretation would become stronger by distinguishing what the early posts establish independently from what becomes visible only through comparison with the later corpus.

**1. Continuity is established more securely than formation**

The opening declares:

> “The commitments did not change in 2016; his willingness to fight for them did.”

Yet §2 describes the young and later Davis as drawing “opposite” morals from “Disguised Queries,” while §5 introduces a new priority between epistemic and instrumental rationality. These could be changes in commitments, depending on what the term includes. The chapter needs to distinguish persistent concerns, substantive beliefs, methods, and norms governing action. A continuing concern with accurate language does not establish an unchanged account of what accuracy requires.

The strongest overstatement comes in §7:

> “The criterion of honesty that the later corpus develops […] is present in December 2011 in a post about complex logarithms.”

The plan defines that criterion through optimization for the listener’s accurate representation, independently of deceptive intent. The logarithm passage, as quoted here, establishes that mathematical facts constrain useful terminology. It does not establish the functional account of honesty. Indeed, §2’s more cautious “something like the norm of accounting” is better than the conclusion’s confident identification.

Likewise, calling “Education and Indoctrination Feel the Same From the Inside” “the root” of the 2026 selection-effects essay asserts a genealogy where the chapter shows a shared problem. “Counterfactual Social Thought” may anticipate the use of causal graphs in the gender writing, but “a wish fulfilled by the 2022 graph of transition” makes an early methodological aspiration look destined for that particular application.

These distinctions matter to the book’s originality claim. Principles about definitions and clear communication may supply the materials of Davis’s later synthesis without already constituting it. The chapter should identify what the later work adds: mechanisms, evidential standards, a theory of misleading communication, and arguments about collective enforcement.

The title also promises an apprenticeship whose teacher and setting remain thinly described. The Introduction’s summary supplies Davis’s encounter with *Overcoming Bias* in 2007 and his institutional involvement in 2009. Thus the first AAL posts are already products of several years of participation. A brief account of that prior formation would prevent “the first posts” from becoming an unsupported proxy for intellectual origins. Here the relevant evidence includes Davis’s early LessWrong writing and explicit citations, not merely later statements that Yudkowsky rewrote his personality.

**Proposed replacement for the opening paragraph’s final two sentences**

*Problem addressed: the existing formulation identifies continuity of concerns with an absence of intellectual change.*

> The early posts establish that several concerns central to the later writing preceded the public gender dispute. They also document attempts to limit the time and emotion Davis spent pursuing them. In 2016 he began describing accommodation as a demand to affirm what he believed false. This was a change in his stated willingness to argue; whether it also changed his account of honesty requires comparison with the arguments developed afterward.

**2. The audience claim cannot support the evidential weight placed on it**

In §1, a joke about having no readers becomes:

> “There is accordingly no reception to report for this period.”

The preceding sentence reports forty comments in one year. A small audience and an absence of substantial independent reception are different findings. The chapter should report what the survey found, rather than deduce non-reception from self-deprecation. Were those comments substantive? Were relevant posts cross-posted or discussed on LessWrong? The packet does not answer these questions.

The next inference is more consequential:

> “a record kept without an audience to persuade, and so a check on what the later writing […] claims about its own origins.”

An obscure public blog can address friends, imagined readers, a future self, or members of an aspirational community. Dialogues, mock addresses, and instructional posts are themselves evidence of constructed audiences. Low readership does not remove self-presentation.

The early record nevertheless has considerable evidential value: its dated formulations constrain what can plausibly be attributed entirely to later disputes. That is a narrower and stronger argument than treating obscurity as a guarantee of unguarded sincerity.

The closing assertion is particularly unfair:

> “years before he had any stake in the dispute.”

The chapter itself describes gender-related jokes, electrolysis, a coming-out poem, and the memoir’s account of a longstanding private fantasy. Public controversy and personal stake are not interchangeable. The final paragraph can establish that relevant arguments predate public polemic. It cannot establish that Davis lacked personal investment when he formed them.

Nor does a sparse public record demonstrate that a subject was private because he “had no reason to write about” it. The poem’s closet imagery permits several explanations for silence. The chapter should distinguish corroboration of limited explicit publication from confirmation of the memoir’s explanation for that limitation.

**3. “Contemporary” is made to contradict the later philosophy too readily**

Section 2 says:

> “The 2012 answer about virginity is the position Davis would spend 2018 to 2021 arguing against when others used it to dismiss his objections.”

The quoted answer includes an essential condition: “once you know what behavior someone has engaged in.” Given complete knowledge of the relevant behavior, a category label may add no information. That is compatible with categories helping people infer facts they do not already know. The later philosophy could therefore develop a distinction implicit in the earlier answer rather than reverse it.

Davis’s possible defense deserves explicit consideration: the problem with a later opponent’s appeal to conventional definitions may be its use where the facts remain uncertain, where compressed communication is necessary, or where listeners retain expectations attached to an established term. None of those conditions is supplied by the quoted workbook answer.

The criticism available on the chapter’s evidence is narrower. “It’s pointless to argue about the definitions of words” sounds more general than the explanation following it warrants. The early formulation may understate coordination problems and the social uses of classification. But the chapter needs to show that excess of scope rather than announce opposite conclusions.

**Proposed replacement for §2, paragraph beginning “The same view appears…”: final three sentences**

*Problem addressed: the current account overlooks the qualification that the relevant behavior is already known.*

> Davis’s answer makes its strongest case under the condition it states: the relevant behavior is already known. A label need add nothing to that knowledge. His later categorization essays concern the further problem of how labels support inference when the facts are only partly known. The tension lies in the early answer’s opening generalization, which dismisses disputes about definitions more broadly than its explanation supports.

**4. The renunciation needs distinctions between intention, practice, and justification**

Section 3 provides the chapter’s best sustained reading, but some conclusions outrun its evidence.

> “The posts of early 2013 show the renunciation working and then failing.”

A report of reduced bitterness followed by hospitalization does not itself establish the failure of a renunciation. The phrase risks making the psychiatric event a verdict on the intellectual strategy. The quoted “Prodrome” passage establishes Davis’s own subsequent assessment of what he could safely pursue; that is enough.

Similarly, “a further retreat, now from thinking itself” is contradicted by the proposed turn to engineering, mathematics, and computer science. It is a retreat from specified kinds of inquiry. The phrase unnecessarily reinforces a division between technical competence and reflective thought that the chapter’s mathematical examples should complicate.

The sentence “The self before the bootcamp was the angry autodidact; the self after it was a competent engineer” turns Davis’s “non-canon” joke into the narrator’s settled periodization. It also makes anger and competence appear mutually exclusive. Treat these as identities Davis proposed for himself, then test them against what he continued publishing in 2014–2015.

The small-group exception is persuasive as a compatibility argument, but insufficient as an explanation:

> “It is the renunciation’s exception coming into force.”

The quoted exception concerns the efficacy of expressing opinions. The renunciation also concerns emotional dependence, outrage, and self-preservation. Believing that one can influence friends does not automatically cancel those considerations. Nor does geographical or social proximity establish actual influence over a movement’s leaders.

The charitable reading is that the later campaign could fit the earlier rule. The critical question is whether Davis estimated its likely effects, revised those estimates, or treated proximity as sufficient authorization. Chapter 7 owns the campaign’s conduct; this chapter need only preserve the question.

There is also an attribution problem in §3: “the same post states” follows discussion of the App Academy diary, although the plan locates “In a small group…” in “Counterfactual Social Thought.” Name the source directly.

**5. The chapter alternates between excessive charity and excessive suspicion**

Section 4 first presents substantial examples of self-criticism, then concludes from one parenthesis:

> “the question of the other side’s motives gets the essays […] and the question of his own error is acknowledged and set aside.”

This conflicts with the paragraph’s examples and with the chapter summaries. Chapters 2 and 7 describe sustained examinations of Davis’s motives, mistakes, and conduct. A parenthesis excluding one topic from one post cannot establish the proportions of the whole corpus.

There is a defensible local criticism: “Idiot or Alien? Incompetence or Evil?” acknowledges the possibility of the speaker’s error without incorporating it into the analysis that follows. Whether that omission becomes a recurrent asymmetry requires comparison of cases. Keep that criticism and remove the general verdict.

Conversely, the chapter is too accepting of the 2016 self-description. It moves from “Late-Onset” to:

> “Being a good person […] now required saying things he believed false.”

Davis’s sentence frames the alternatives that way. The narrator should not silently adopt the frame. What specific accommodation was being demanded? Did it require false assertion, silence, a conventional form of address, uncertainty, or reduced hostility? These possibilities matter because the later chapters dispute whether tact and epistemic distortion coincide.

“Concerns” and “Concerns II” also deserve a sentence of criticism. The speaker’s confidence that a causal hypothesis is implausible does not establish its implausibility. An objection about imperfect correlations may be weak or relevant depending on the actual inference. Invoking the argumentative theory of reasoning can explain disagreement prematurely. Check the full dialogues before deciding whether they expose an interlocutor’s evasion or merely stage one.

The opposing suspicion at the end of §5 is no better established:

> “Another is that the anger was the deep thing and the truth-framing the form it took…”

Truth-seeking and anger need not compete for the status of underlying cause. Anger can direct attention, sustain research, sharpen sensitivity to inconsistency, and impair judgments about particular opponents. Moreover, the record includes anger before 2016. Showing that commitments preceded anger’s *return* does little to establish their causal priority over anger.

Chapter 7’s summarized verdict—commitment first, anger shaping method—would benefit from replacing this psychological contest with questions about observable conduct.

**Proposed replacement for §5, final paragraph**

*Problem addressed: the existing alternatives invite an underdetermined judgment about Davis’s deepest motive.*

> The early record establishes both a concern with truthful description and dissatisfaction with the costs of sustained outrage. Their recurrence together in 2016 does not show which caused the other. The later campaign permits more specific questions: whether Davis applied his standards to favorable arguments, acknowledged contrary evidence, and revised judgments about other people when criticism warranted it. Chapter 7 examines those practices alongside his accounts of anger and its costs.

**6. The coordination argument is not yet demonstrated**

“A Common Misunderstanding” distinguishes instrumental from epistemic rationality through a joke. The chapter calls this:

> “the first appearance of […] the role-constitutive form of the coordination argument.”

The quotation supports a conflict between epistemic and instrumental priorities. It does not yet supply an argument about coordination, the instability of exceptions, or what a community must enforce. Even a role-based refusal to weigh consequences is not automatically a coordination argument.

This distinction is especially necessary because the chapter simultaneously says the coordination argument is absent from the early blog. Its chronological endpoint also slides: the apparent evidence for its emergence comes from February 2017, outside the title’s period.

Describe these posts as a brief coda identifying a rhetorical precursor to the later role rule. Reserve “coordination argument” for passages that actually provide the missing reasoning. “First appearance” also requires a search across the earlier LessWrong material, not just AAL.

**7. Historical and scholarly context worth adding**

The chapter needs a modest account of the intellectual environment, not a survey detached from Davis. The following are research leads from memory, not verified sources:

- **[memory]** Tom Chivers’s *The AI Does Not Hate You* (2019) offers a journalistic account of the rationalist community and its relation to Yudkowsky’s writing. It could help establish institutional context, though retrospective reporting should not substitute for contemporaneous evidence of Davis’s reading.
- **[memory]** Joseph Reagle’s *Hacking Life: Systematized Living and Its Discontents* (2019) examines practices of systematic self-improvement in technological cultures. It offers a possible comparison for Davis’s movement between intellectual ambition, emotional discipline, and practical competence. Check its actual treatment of rationalists before using it as community history.
- **[memory]** Elizabeth Sandifer’s *Neoreaction a Basilisk* (2017) offers a critical interpretation of Yudkowsky and neighboring online intellectual formations. It is a polemical secondary account, not a neutral history; its relevance would be as an alternative interpretation of the surrounding culture.
- **[memory]** E. T. Jaynes’s *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science* (2003) develops probability as an extension of reasoning under uncertainty. Comparison with the Sequences could clarify what “Bayesian” contributes here beyond a vocabulary of evidence and naturalism. The chapter should distinguish formal probabilistic commitments from maxims about honesty, identity, and useful concepts.
- **[memory]** Jill Walker Rettberg’s *Blogging* (2008; second edition 2014) discusses blogging as a form with personal, social, and audience relationships. It would help correct the inference from low readership to absence of audience-directed writing.
- **[memory]** Ann Blair’s *Too Much to Know* (2010) examines earlier traditions of collecting and organizing knowledge. It could support a restrained comparison with notebooks and commonplace practices, without claiming direct influence on Davis.

The most important missing reception evidence is local and contemporaneous: how Davis cited, adapted, or contested the Sequences before AAL and during 2011–2016. The chapter currently establishes familiarity with one Yudkowsky essay, then attributes several broader intellectual habits to the subculture. Document the transmission where possible; distinguish explicit borrowing from the study’s comparison.

**8. Structure, overlap, and prose**

The chapter follows its planned remit, but too much of §2 is a catalogue of later destinations. “Chapter 4 follows,” “Chapter 6,” “Chapter 5,” “Chapter 9,” “Chapter 3,” and “Chapter 11” arrive in rapid succession. This makes the early blog function as a collection of previews. Retain three developed examples—definitions, candor about coercion, and self-application—and compress the remaining anticipations into a short paragraph.

The opening, the end of §2, the end of §3, and §7 repeat the continuity/permission thesis. Removing two restatements would create space for the historical context and qualifications above.

Section 6 should remain brief, given Chapter 8’s fuller treatment. Its strongest contribution here would be to explain why dialogue complicates attribution: a speaker’s claim is evidence of what Davis chose to dramatize, not automatically his considered position. Apply that distinction to “Ringing a Bell,” “Hustle,” and the 2016 dialogues.

Several formulations have the brief’s disfavored compressed-verdict quality: “The resolution gave up the goodness”; “That complaint is the later one about words in small”; “The joke records a real division.” Replace them with specific claims about what the text licenses. “The verse carries things Davis could not yet say in prose” also needs qualification: the evidence establishes a chosen form, not inability.

Two concrete errors need attention. “The posts of the following weeks” introduces a parable dated two days before “Late-Onset.” More seriously, §1 says Davis was “finishing a mathematics degree,” while the supplied summaries describe later consideration of completing that degree and renewed study in 2024–2025. Verify whether “studying mathematics” is intended.

Finally, add the required unnumbered “Note on method.” It should explain the distinction between contemporaneous posts and later memoir evidence, the scope of the reception search, and the status of the reviewer’s contribution. Check apparent quotation irregularities against originals; do not silently correct Davis’s typos.

**Checks for the main agent**

1. **Degree chronology:** Check “Group Introduction Redux,” “Should I Finish My Bachelor’s Degree?,” and “College Was Not That Terrible…” against §1’s claim that Davis was finishing his degree in 2012–2013.
2. **Audience and reception:** Check “2014 Year in Reverse,” early comment threads, and LessWrong cross-posts before retaining “no reception” or “without an audience to persuade.”
3. **Definition argument:** Re-read “Contemporary,” “Disguised Queries,” and the relevant later categorization essays to test whether their conclusions conflict under the same informational conditions.
4. **Renunciation’s exception:** Verify the source and scope of “In a small group…” in “Counterfactual Social Thought”; distinguish it from the App Academy diary quotation.
5. **2016 chronology and implied beliefs:** Check “Concerns,” “Concerns II,” “Late-Onset,” and “The Parable of the Honest Man and the Thing”; correct the “following weeks” sequence and identify what accommodation the texts actually describe.
6. **Priority claim:** Check “A Common Misunderstanding” and earlier LessWrong material before calling it the first role-constitutive coordination argument.
7. **Private interest versus public dispute:** Check “October Eleventh,” “Electrolysis,” “An Idle Wish for a Future *Star Trek* Series,” and “Blanchard’s Dangerous Idea” before claiming the early commitments preceded any personal stake.
