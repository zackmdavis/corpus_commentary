## Overall

The chapter is well researched and fair in temper. Its best pages are §2 (the Ozy exchange, and the observation that Serano also discounts some testimony) and §3 (self-application), and the Sophia/"different order" material in §5 is a real find. Three structural problems stand in its way. They are ranked first below.

1. **The central claim (an asymmetry "of confidence rather than principle") is asserted but not shown, and the chapter's own §1 and §3 supply the materials that would partly defeat it.** The chapter praises Davis's record for being contemporaneous and for appealing to third-person evidence, and then in §4 treats his causal reading as resting on "the same kinds of introspective evidence" as Serano's informants. On the chapter's own account the evidential positions are different, and different evidence can justify different confidence. The chapter has to confront this, and the honest verdict is probably split: the confidence is earned for order of onset and lack of childhood effeminacy, and not earned for the step from order to cause or for claims about other people's phenomenology.
2. **The experience/cause distinction carries the whole chapter's weight and is never examined.** It is attacked from two sides in the literature, and both bear on the dispute. From the trans-philosophy side, Bettcher's *first-person authority* argument (2009, not the 2014 paper the chapter uses) holds that "I am a woman" is an avowal, neither a report nor a hypothesis. From the psychology side, reports of "felt quality" (whether a feeling is erotic) are already interpretations (misattribution of arousal; Schwitzgebel; genital/subjective arousal concordance; Hacking's looping effects). The chapter treats Davis's line as "correct" and "commonplace in psychology". That is too quick, and it makes the chapter too generous to Davis's framework at the same moment that it is too hard on his confidence.
3. **The chapter is not connected to the book's thesis.** The plan's thesis 4 makes this chapter central to self-application, and thesis 2 asks every chapter to say whether part (c) drives the practice it examines. The chapter never mentions the criterion, the norm of accounting, or the coordination argument, and never says whether the "crazy or a liar" register comes from (c) or from something else. As it stands it reads as a free-standing essay.

## A. Weak or overstated arguments (ranked)

**A1. The evidential asymmetry the chapter ignores (concession acknowledged, then dropped).** §1 says the record's value lies partly in "contemporaneous documents (the 2006 notebook, the 2017 diary) that can be compared with later retrospection", and that it is "specific enough to be checked against what Davis did". §3 calls the appeal to what others observed (effeminacy) "the strongest methodological move in the personal writing". Then §4 says Davis's materials "are also exactly the materials on which Serano and many of the trans women she cites reach the opposite conclusion." They are the same *kind* of material, but they differ in quality on exactly the dimensions the chapter praised. An onset order recorded at eighteen, about four years after onset and before any public or clinical stake, is better evidence than an onset order recalled decades later in a clinical or political setting. That is the chapter's own incentive argument (§4, para 3), turned to Davis's credit. A claim that confidence is unequal does not show that the inequality is unjustified, and the chapter never asks what confidence the evidence warrants. The autobiographical-memory literature (see C4) would let the chapter make the comparison properly. My suggested verdict: the confidence is earned for the *sequence* and for the absence of childhood femininity, and unearned for the *step from sequence to cause*, which no notebook can settle, because a common cause, separate causes that mature at different times, or a causal link all predict the same record. That is a sharper and fairer claim than "confidence rather than principle." See F2 for prose.

**A2. "Confidence rather than principle" is unclear in itself.** If the principle is that introspection does not reveal causes, then confidently holding a causal reading that rests on introspection *is* a breach of principle, not just a difference in degree. If the principle allows causal inference from introspective data plus order plus theory (which §4 says it does: "all are the right kind of evidence on Nisbett and Wilson's account"), then confidence becomes a matter of evidential weight, and the question in A1 has to be answered. The chapter takes the first reading in the lede ("he holds his reading with more confidence than he allows theirs") and the second in §4. It should pick one. The phrase also reads as a softening formula, and a reader may suspect it was chosen to be gentle.

**A3. Internal inconsistency over the Polly Pocket passage.** §3 praises the cherry-picking passage as "the strongest methodological move in the personal writing; it moves the question from what one feels to what others saw." §4 then lists the same passage as evidence of overconfidence: "Where the record raises the possibility that the identity feeling had earlier roots of its own, as in the Polly Pocket passage, it raises it in order to reject it as cherry-picking." He rejects the alternative *on the third-person criterion the chapter has just praised*. The chapter cannot have it both ways. Either the criterion is good, in which case rejecting the cherry-picked narrative is warranted, or it is not.

**A4. The "male brain" note is misdescribed.** "That is a claim about neuroscience that no introspection could support, and the 2016 principle would forbid anyone else to make it about themselves from the inside." From the quotation, Davis is not claiming to know his brain is male from the inside. He is inferring it from his sex (and perhaps from his reading of the brain-sex literature). The fair criticism is that it is a contested empirical claim stated with certainty (the trans-brain literature, e.g. Zhou et al. 1995 and Kruijver et al. 2000 on the BSTc, Guillamon et al. 2016's review, and Joel et al. 2015 on "mosaic" brains, all from memory, is disputed in both directions). It is not a breach of the introspection principle. The register should also be checked: "sufficiently-advanced soul science" reads as playful, and an editor's note may be ironic. Treating a joke as a sober claim would be unfair. (Check G4.)

**A5. "The confident register in which he holds the conclusion is what he would call, in anyone else, taking the beautiful pure sacred feeling literally."** This is a category error dressed as a paradox. In Davis's usage, "taking the feeling literally" means concluding that one *is* female. Holding a causal theory of the feeling with too much confidence is a different error. Cut the sentence or restate it plainly.

**A6. The "different order" symmetry argument proves less than §5 and §8 claim.** "If the order of one's reading can turn the same experiences into different self-understandings, then self-understanding is weak evidence about cause on either side." Agreed. But §5 concludes that "the reading cannot be what makes it right", and §8 then uses this to deny Davis his confidence. That edges toward the genetic fallacy, which §5 itself disclaims. The sentence shows that self-understanding is weak evidence, not that Davis's confidence rests on self-understanding. If it rests on the record and on third-person criteria (A1), the symmetry argument does not reach it. Also check what Sophia actually concludes (G7). If she accepts an autogynephilic account of herself and transitioned anyway, her case shows that the same causal reading can lead to different *decisions*. That is §7's economic point, not §5's point about self-understanding, and the "mirror universe" may have been misfiled.

**A7. "Exactly the materials" also misstates the content.** The chapter lists the pattern of what Davis's fantasy contains (bodies indiscriminate as to age, race, and build; other male bodies "bounce off") as introspective evidence of the same sort Serano uses. But the *content* of that pattern is evidence that discriminates between hypotheses: an erotic-target account predicts it, and an identity account does not obviously predict it. Serano's informants, as the chapter reports them, describe different content (earlier, non-sexual onset). The situation is therefore same evidence type, different evidence, different conclusions, and each party may be justified on its own data. That again needs A1's analysis, not the symmetry claim.

**A8. "Davis applies it to himself more often and at more cost than most writers on either side of the dispute"** (lede). No comparison is made anywhere in the chapter. Either support it (name the comparison class: Serano, Lawrence, Bailey, Chu, Illy) or cut "than most writers".

**A9. "Costly testimony ... against the writer's interest."** The chapter's own material qualifies this, and the qualification is not carried through. (i) The pseudonym lasted until 2023, so most of the record was written at reduced personal cost. "But the record exposes him to ridicule" glosses over this. (ii) §3 quotes the Day 89 legitimacy motive (to be "trans enough" for his rejection "to mean something"), which is an interest the testimony *serves*. (iii) The draft says the record was written "as a counterpoint", that is, as evidence for one side. By Ch. 5's analysis of "Heads I Win", a record kept to serve as counterevidence is a filtered record unless the filter is disclosed. The chapter should ask whether Davis discloses the filter on his personal writing. Arguably he does ("honestly report my observations, experiences, and inferences. The reader can and should draw their own conclusions"), which is the norm of accounting applied to himself and should be named as such. Saying so would also tie the chapter to the thesis.

**A10. The economic view and the "sanctity of my map" are in tension, and the chapter does not notice.** §7 says the economic view "needs no position on etiology" and "values the wish without needing to settle its cause", and that it is the part of his position the personal evidence supports "most securely". In the same section it says Davis records the costs "because his own decision depends on getting the causal question right." If the decision is a purchase priced by the strength of the wish, why does cause matter? A real answer exists (the expected satisfaction depends on what the wish is, and an erotic wish may not be satisfied by possessing what it eroticizes), and it is exactly where Lawrence and Chu come in (C6). Without it, §7's two claims contradict each other. See F5.

**A11. "His reports of them have been consistent."** The chapter's own quotations show that the stated *reasons* for stopping changed: 2017 has a name, a life, the credibility worry ("bad thing to have thumbing the scales"), and "And that's final"; 2024 has "I chickened out over medical uncertainty." The decision is consistent. The account of why changed, in the direction of a more deflating retrospective reason. In a chapter about retrospective self-report this is a missed observation, and arguably the chapter's best illustration of its own thesis applied fairly to Davis. (Check G9: what reasons Day 156 actually gives.)

**A12. The Fricker frame is set up against an unnamed opponent.** "Davis's critics often rely, explicitly or not, on the concept of epistemic injustice" (and the lede's "often invoked against theories like Davis's"). The only person in the chapter who uses the term is Illy, an ally, on the AGP side. Serano and Veale and adrusi complain about being called liars, which is a testimonial-injustice-shaped complaint, but they are not shown using Fricker. Either cite critics who invoke epistemic injustice against Blanchard/Bailey explicitly (there may be some in the academic literature; search), or say the frame is the study's reconstruction of the critics' position. As written, "explicitly" is unsupported.

**A13. "The economic view ... a reading kinder to the people concerned than either the contagion or the innate-identity story."** Kinder than the innate-identity story is a contestable value judgment that is not argued. Many trans people would find "a purchase at the margin" less kind than "who I am". Cut or argue.

## B. Fairness

**Too hard on Davis**

- **B1. The adrusi exchange (§5).** "adrusi had not proposed an intersex condition. The reply answers the 'woman trapped in a man's body' model, which adrusi had just said hardly anyone holds, and leaves her counter-case where it was." If adrusi proposed a common *biological/developmental* cause (the autism link, a "gifted young programmer" cluster), then Davis's "intersex condition with no particular symptoms other than a mysterious desire to be female" may be a compressed but fair restatement of a brain-based common-cause hypothesis. Davis's 2023 "Empirical Claim" (per the plan: "late-onset gender dysphoria in males is not an intersex condition") suggests "intersex condition" is his term of art for any innate sex-atypical neurology. If so, the reply addresses her hypothesis, perhaps uncharitably, and does not answer a position she disclaimed. Check carefully (G6). The chapter's reliance on this exchange in §6 ("he answered adrusi's counter-case by refuting a position she did not hold") makes it load-bearing.
- **B2. "Crazy or a liar."** Check whether the sentence says it would be crazy or a lie *for Davis* to equate his own wish with a woman's report of being female, or says it of trans women generally. If the former, it is a statement about his own case and fits A1's earned confidence about sequence. (G5.)
- **B3. The male-brain note**, as in A4.
- **B4. The Polly Pocket double count**, as in A3.
- **B5. Bettcher's "erasure" reason (end of §6).** "Bettcher's further reason for rejecting a sexual account of transition, that it would erase trans people's own accounts of their motives, is an appeal to testimony about causes, and so falls under the distinction of §2." This is unfair to Bettcher. Her concern (and her 2009 argument) is ethical and political, about first-person authority over identity, not an epistemic claim that self-reports of cause are accurate. Folding it into §2 dismisses her with Davis's distinction instead of engaging her. Davis is not the one being treated unfairly here, but it is a fairness problem toward his critics that the brief also cares about.

**Too generous to Davis**

- **B6.** The lede and §2 call the experience/cause principle "sound", "correct", and in agreement with "the psychology of introspection". As argued above (and in C1–C3), it is defensible but contested at both ends. The chapter grants Davis's framework the status of settled science and then scolds him for confidence. That ordering flatters the theory and penalizes the person.
- **B7.** "A writer who applies to his allies the charity he asks of his opponents has at least stated the principle symmetrically." The evidence is an *unpublished draft*. An unpublished rebuke to one's allies costs less than a published one, and the chapter should weigh that (or find a published instance; the plan lists the hidden page "What I Said to /r/GenderCritical", which may serve).
- **B8.** The "against interest" point (A9).

**Concessions acknowledged then ignored**

- The contemporaneous record (A1).
- The third-person criterion (A3).
- Ozy's "fair" plus her qualification: the chapter says it "matters later in this chapter" and then uses it only in passing (§4 para 3, last sentence). Either develop it or drop the forward reference.
- "It's my story, not theirs" is quoted and then set aside as the "modest half". Davis says the post was deliberately standalone. The chapter's reading of "people like me" as a hinge is reasonable, but it should register that Davis's conditional advice comes with discriminating criteria he supplies elsewhere (onset order, observed effeminacy, orientation), so the reader is not left to tell "from the inside" alone, contrary to what §4 says on tailcalled's behalf.

## C. Missing objections and scholarship

**C1. Bettcher, "Trans Identities and First-Person Authority" (2009)** — the most important omission. (From memory: in Laurie Shrage, ed., *You've Changed: Sex Reassignment and Personal Identity*, Oxford University Press, 2009, pp. ~98–120; verify.) Bettcher argues that trans self-identifications carry *ethical* first-person authority: they are avowals about who one is and how one asks to be treated, not introspective reports open to correction, nor hypotheses about etiology. This is the strongest philosophical answer to Davis's "not an experience; it's a hypothesis", because it denies that the identity claim belongs to either of his categories. The chapter uses Bettcher 2014 (on erotic structure), which bears more on Ch. 3's terminology than on testimony. The 2009 paper is the one aimed at this chapter's question. There is a strong reply on Davis's behalf: an avowal has authority only over what a commitment can settle (address, pronouns, social role, which Ch. 4 says Davis grants in public practice), not over the past or over causes (onset, the non-sexual origin of the wish). Developing that reply would make the chapter's verdict sharper and fairer to both sides. See F3.

**C2. Philosophy of self-knowledge.** The chapter discusses first-person authority with no philosophy of self-knowledge at all. Minimum: Brie Gertler's SEP entry "Self-Knowledge" (fetchable) for the map of positions; Richard Moran, *Authority and Estrangement* (2001), on the deliberative versus theoretical stance and on "transparency" (one answers "do I believe p?" by considering p). Moran adds something specific. Thesis 4 praises Davis for "treating oneself as a hypothesis". Moran's account names the cost of that stance, *estrangement*: a person who knows his wishes only as an observer of himself has given up the authority that comes from making up his mind. The hormone diary's running audit of motives (Day 89: "introspection is difficult") can be read through this lens, and so can Davis's 2024 "chickened out". This would let the chapter make a critical point about the self-application thesis instead of only praising it. Optionally, Dorit Bar-On's neo-expressivism (*Speaking My Mind*, 2004) on avowals.

**C3. Psychology of introspection: the chapter relies on one 1977 paper read in a degraded scan.** At minimum fetch Schwitzgebel's SEP "Introspection" (it surveys Nisbett & Wilson, their critics, and later work). Specific additions:
- **Schwitzgebel, "The Unreliability of Naive Introspection," *Philosophical Review* 2008** (from memory): people are often wrong even about current conscious experience. This attacks the "reliable about experience" half of Davis's principle, which the chapter treats as secure.
- **Misattribution of arousal** (Schachter & Singer 1962; Dutton & Aron 1974; from memory): the same physiological state is labelled differently depending on the available explanation. Whether a feeling "doesn't feel explicitly erotic" (Davis) or arose "in a nonsexual context" (Serano) is exactly the kind of label this literature shows to be malleable.
- **Chivers et al. 2010, *Archives of Sexual Behavior*, meta-analysis of agreement between self-reported and genital arousal** (from memory; men's concordance substantially higher than women's; verify figures before quoting any). This is directly relevant to how reliable self-reported arousal is, and it links to the phallometric work on crossdressers (Blanchard, Racansky & Steiner 1986, from memory), which Ch. 3 may own, but Ch. 2 needs a sentence on it because it is the original empirical case for discounting self-reports in this area.
- Nisbett & Wilson's critics (e.g. Ericsson & Simon on verbal protocols, from memory), so that "commonplace in psychology" is not overstated. Wilson's own *Strangers to Ourselves* (2002) extends the 1977 view, if a more readable source is wanted.

The upshot for the chapter is that the experience/cause line is real but leaky. "Felt quality" sits partly on the cause side. This cuts against both Davis and Serano, and in the end it strengthens the chapter's case that the sources protected from interpretation (contemporaneous records, other people's observations) are the ones that count.

**C4. Autobiographical memory and narrative identity**, which bear directly on "remembered order" and on the "different books in a different order" sentence:
- Michael Ross, "Relation of Implicit Theories to the Construction of Personal Histories," *Psychological Review* 1989 (from memory): people reconstruct their pasts to fit current theories of stability or change in themselves.
- Recall bias in retrospective reports of childhood gender nonconformity (discussed in Bailey & Zucker 1995's meta-analysis, from memory; there are also prospective and home-video studies, e.g. Rieger et al. 2008, from memory). This is the empirical background to Davis's "identified as effeminate by others" criterion, and it supports him.
- Kate McLean and Moin Syed's "master narratives" framework (*Human Development* 2015, from memory): people build life stories from culturally available master narratives. This is the psychology behind Sophia's "different order" and Davis's own sentence.

**C5. Hacking's looping effects** ("Making Up People," 1986; *Rewriting the Soul*, 1995; from memory). Once a classification is available, the people classified come to experience and report themselves through it. "THERE'S A WORD FOR IT" is a textbook moment of a classification becoming available. Looping implies that the symmetry the chapter finds reaches below causal inference to reported experience itself, on both sides. The plan puts Hacking in Ch. 4 for interactive kinds; Ch. 2 should use the looping mechanism for testimony and cross-refer.

**C6. Trans first-person writing that cuts across the chapter's two camps.** The chapter's map has Davis (desire, no transition) against Serano (identity, transition). It is missing:
- **Anne Lawrence**: accepts the autogynephilic account and transitioned; "Becoming What We Love" (*Archives of Sexual Behavior* 2007, from memory; the plan says the project has it) and *Men Trapped in Men's Bodies* (2013), which collects first-person narratives and argues that autogynephilic trans women misreport. The plan lists Lawrence explicitly for this chapter, and the draft omits her. She is the obvious counterpart: the same causal reading, the opposite decision. That breaks the chapter's implicit link between causal reading and decision, and it is where §7's satisfaction question (A10) gets an answer.
- **Andrea Long Chu**, "On Liking Women," *n+1* no. 30 (2018), and "My New Vagina Won't Make Me Happy," *New York Times* (2018) (both from memory). Chu defends transition on desire alone, without identity or outcome claims. This is the strongest trans-side agreement with Davis's "the wish is the thing" and the strongest rejection of his conclusion, and it speaks directly to notaname versus tailcalled.
- Serano's *Whipping Girl* is quoted only through Davis's quotation (data-src points to his post). Say "as Davis quotes it" in the text, add the book to the works cited, and ideally check the passage and the ten-million-dollar question against the book.

**C7. Epistemic injustice beyond the IEP entry.** Fricker is central to §6 and was "not read". At minimum fetch something more direct. Specifically:
- **Miranda Fricker and Katharine Jenkins, "Epistemic Injustice, Ignorance, and Trans Experiences,"** in *The Routledge Companion to Feminist Philosophy* (2017) (from memory). Fricker herself applies the framework to trans experience. If it discusses clinical gatekeeping or the credibility of trans self-reports, the chapter's "two applications" claim has to be tested against it.
- **Kristie Dotson, "A Cautionary Tale: On Limiting Epistemic Oppression"** (2012), on *contributory* injustice, and **Gaile Pohlhaus, "Relational Knowing and Epistemic Injustice: Toward a Theory of Willful Hermeneutical Ignorance"** (*Hypatia* 2012) (both from memory). Both matter because Davis's case does not fit Fricker's hermeneutical injustice cleanly. The concept *existed* (Blanchard 1989; on Wikipedia in 2006). The problem was not a gap in the collective resource but that a community refused to take up a resource that existed. That is closer to Pohlhaus's willful hermeneutical ignorance or Dotson's contributory injustice. The same refinement fits trans women's complaint (their concepts existed in their communities and clinicians refused them). Using these would make §6's "one on each side" symmetry precise instead of loose. Also José Medina, *The Epistemology of Resistance* (2013), optionally.
- The chapter's own hedge ("Whether the stigma amounts to a 'structural identity prejudice'... is arguable") would benefit from noting that Fricker counts sexuality-based identities as candidates for identity prejudice.

**C8. Missing objection: testimony about one's own *reasons* for a decision** (A11). Davis's changing account of why he stopped is a live instance of post-hoc reason-giving (Nisbett & Wilson's own territory, and the choice-blindness work of Johansson and Hall, from memory). The chapter could apply its principle to Davis here, fairly and concretely, instead of only to his causal etiology.

## D. Prose that reads as AI-generated

The prose is mostly good: plain, specific, well cited. The main tic is a **pithy closing sentence at the end of almost every paragraph**, often in verdict or antithesis form. Read in sequence they produce the drumbeat the brief warns about. Examples:

- "Costly testimony is better evidence of sincerity than cheap testimony. It is not, by itself, better evidence about causes, which is the distinction on which Davis's own epistemology turns." (§1 close)
- "...and Davis's line falls in the same place." (§2 para 1)
- "Both were right. A hypothesis that some reports are false is testable in aggregate; it does not tell anyone which reports are false." (§2 para 3: a short verdict followed by a balanced aphorism)
- "Everyone discounts some testimony, and the question is on what grounds and with how much confidence." (§2 para 4)
- "...it moves the question from what one feels to what others saw." (§3 para 2)
- "A writer who applies to his allies the charity he asks of his opponents has at least stated the principle symmetrically." (§3 close)
- "'People like me' is the hinge." (§4 para 2)
- "The sentence does not show that his reading is wrong. It shows that the reading cannot be what makes it right, and that is why the population claim has to be settled elsewhere." (§5 close: a reflexive "does not X; it Y")
- "The boy is Davis." (§6 para 2: a dramatic reveal; also possibly unsupported, see G10)
- "Both are correct, and between them they state the use of testimony in a decision. ... Davis, who is at the margin, treated it accordingly." (§7 close)

Recommendation: let paragraphs end on evidence or on the next step of the argument at least half the time, and keep perhaps three of these closers in the whole chapter.

**"Not X but Y" / antithesis**, which is frequent: "The unevenness is one of confidence rather than principle"; "is not a testimonial claim at all"; "a remembered order is a report of experience, not of process"; "does not deny privileged access to the contents of experience, only to the causal processes"; "aimed at the concept rather than at the people who use it"; "a serious objection to the word ... and a weak one to Davis's reading"; "as well as a contagion". Each is defensible alone, but there are too many in 6,600 words.

**Triads and lists of three or four**: lede "The principle is sound, it agrees with the psychology of introspection, and Davis applies it..."; "felt quality, remembered order, the fit of details" (lede), repeated as "a reported difference in felt quality, a remembered order of onset, and a pattern in what he finds appealing" (§4) and "felt quality, remembered order, and the pattern of what he wants" (§8). The same triad three times is noticeable. Also "detailed, dated, checked against behavior, and published against interest" (§8); "in money, health, and social standing"; "feeling, motive, and change".

**Signposting and announcing**: "The difficulty lies in the inference Davis draws about himself." (§4 opener); "a qualification that matters later in this chapter" (§2); "The part of the personal writing least affected by these difficulties is" (§7); "The most revealing response came from" ("most revealing" works as an intensifier); "Talia Mae Bettcher's critique goes deeper"; "At full strength" appears twice in §6 and is the plan's phrase leaking into the text. Also the lede, which summarizes the whole chapter's argument including its verdict. A short orienting paragraph is normal in a monograph, but this one is a compressed abstract with five verdict sentences. Consider cutting it to the question and the thesis (see F1).

**Smaller points.** "Davis: <q>" as an attribution device (§4) is fine once. "in so many words" is fine. I found no references to the project's machinery in the body text. The note on method is appropriately procedural.

### F1. A replacement lede (shorter; states the question and the thesis without stacking verdicts)

> The evidential core of Davis's gender writing is a first-person record kept from 2006 [VERIFY: 2005 or 2006] to 2025 by a man who neither transitioned nor disowned the wish to. He reads it with an epistemology he states for everyone: people report their experiences with authority and infer their causes without it. This chapter asks what that record can establish and how evenly Davis applies his rule. Part of the record is better protected against later reinterpretation than most testimony in the dispute, because it was written down early, before Davis had any stake in the controversy, and because it appeals to what other people observed. That part supports the confidence he places in it. The step from the order of his experiences to their cause is not protected in the same way, and his confidence in that step, in his claims about other people's inner lives, and in the population claim of 2023 goes beyond what testimony of any kind can carry. Two lines of criticism press on the rule itself: trans philosophers who hold that a declaration of identity is neither a report nor a hypothesis, and psychologists who doubt that reports of felt quality are as secure as the rule assumes.

### F2. Replacement for §4 paragraph 4 ("On the principle, then, Davis and Serano...") — the core argument

> On the principle, Davis and Serano are in the same position: each holds a causal hypothesis about experiences that introspection presents without their causes. They are not in the same evidential position, and the difference needs to be measured. Davis's account of the order of his experiences does not rest on recall alone. The notebook of August 2006 was written some four years after the onset he dates to thirteen or fourteen [VERIFY: that the notebook, or another document of that time, records the fantasy as already present and the self-identity feeling as distinct], before he had a public position to defend and without a clinician to persuade. Research on autobiographical memory gives that circumstance real weight. People rebuild their pasts in the light of their present theories of themselves (Ross 1989, cited from memory), and a near-contemporaneous record is protected from that revision as a recollection given decades later, in a clinic or a political argument, is not. Davis's third-person criterion works the same way, since what parents and teachers saw in a boy is not changed by what he reads afterwards. So far, the greater confidence he places in his own case is earned, and it is earned by the features of the record credited above.

> It is earned only for the sequence. "The erotic thing definitely came first" reports an order; the step from order to cause is the one Davis himself calls "a pretty obvious guess." ### F3. New paragraphs for §2 or §6: avowal and first-person authority (about 400 words)

> The distinction on which Davis's epistemology turns, between a report of experience and a hypothesis about its cause, leaves no place for a third kind of first-person statement, and the most developed philosophical defence of trans self-identification occupies that place. Talia Mae Bettcher argues that when a trans woman says that she is a woman she is chiefly neither describing an inner state nor proposing an account of her own development. She is avowing an identity, and the authority of such an avowal is ethical: it is the standing a person has to say who she is and how she is to be addressed, which others violate by overriding it, not by correcting a mistake (Bettcher 2009 [from memory; VERIFY title, volume, and that this summary is accurate]). The argument draws on a broader view in the philosophy of self-knowledge on which some first-person claims are settled by the speaker's commitment and not by self-observation (Moran 2001 [from memory]). If Bettcher is right, Davis's reply to Serano addresses the wrong kind of statement. "That explanation could not account" may be a hypothesis, but "I am a woman" is not one, and declining to credit it corrects no causal error.
>
> Davis's position contains an answer. An avowal can carry that authority only over matters a commitment can settle. How a person is addressed is such a matter, and in public Davis uses the pronouns people ask for [VERIFY: Ch. 4 summary says "he follows self-ID pronouns in public"; confirm and cite the source text]. Whether a wish to be female began in childhood, or arose apart from sexuality, is not such a matter, since these are claims about the past and about causes, and avowal gives no authority over them. On this division the dispute narrows. Bettcher's account protects the declaration of identity from being treated as a failed theory, while the claims Davis contests in Serano and in the clinical narratives he discusses are mostly of the other kind. The division also counts against him. His own most confident sentences about other people ("If you were actually a woman, you would not be so erotically obsessed with this idea of being a woman") are claims about what others feel, over which, on his own principle and on Bettcher's, he has the least standing of anyone.

### F4. A paragraph for §2 after the Nisbett–Wilson discussion: felt quality is already interpreted

> The principle also puts more weight on the line between experience and cause than the psychology will bear. Nisbett and Wilson questioned access to the processes behind judgments, not to the judgments themselves. Whether a feeling is erotic, however, is already a judgment. Work on the misattribution of arousal shows people labelling the same bodily state differently according to the explanation their situation offers (Schachter and Singer 1962; Dutton and Aron 1974; both cited from memory). A meta-analysis of studies that compared self-reported with physiological sexual arousal found the two only moderately related, and less related in women than in men (Chivers et al. 2010 [from memory; VERIFY before stating any figure]). Eric Schwitzgebel has argued that people are often mistaken about features of their present conscious experience (Schwitzgebel 2008 [from memory]). Davis's report that the self-identity feeling "doesn't feel explicitly erotic" and Serano's report of thinking about being female "in a nonsexual context" are both reports of this interpreted kind. They are better evidence than a bare causal claim and weaker evidence than the 2016 principle supposes. The point applies to both writers, and it is one reason the parts of the record that do not depend on how a feeling is described, such as dates, documents, and what others saw, carry the argument in what follows.

### F5. A paragraph for §7, after "sanctity of my map": why cause matters to a purchase

> The economic view sits awkwardly with the importance Davis gives to cause. If transition is bought at a price set against the strength of a wish, the origin of the wish seems to drop out of the reckoning. The causal question returns through a prediction: what a person gains from transition depends on what the wish is a wish for. A wish whose object is a female body as an erotic idea may be satisfied differently by having one, or not at all, while a wish that tracks a settled sense of self would be met by it [VERIFY whether Davis states this reasoning anywhere, e.g. in the HRT posts or the memoir; if not, label it as the study's reconstruction]. Writers who share parts of Davis's premises answer the prediction differently. Anne Lawrence, who accepts the autogynephilic account and transitioned, describes the desire as closer to romantic love, which possession fulfils (Lawrence 2007 [VERIFY against the project's copy]). Andrea Long Chu, writing outside the Blanchardian framework, defends transition as the pursuit of a desire that need not promise happiness to be legitimate (Chu 2018 [from memory]). Between them they show that the same account of cause can support opposite decisions, and that what decides the matter for someone at the margin is an empirical question about satisfaction, which no one's introspection, Davis's included, can answer before the fact.
