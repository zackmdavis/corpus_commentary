# Review: Blanchard’s Dangerous Idea

The chapter’s strongest work is its separation of claims that Davis’s polemics sometimes combine, its attention to public corrections, and its comparison of his quoted percentages with their denominators. The 2025 SEXNET draft is especially valuable evidence against a simple account of Davis as an uncritical partisan. These strengths should survive revision.

The principal problem is the chapter’s own hierarchy of conclusions. It repeatedly promotes evidence for correlations into evidence for clusters, and limited evidence against wholesale brain feminization into evidence against an inadequately defined “intersex condition.” Its treatment of Serano’s onset objection also contains a logical error. These problems affect the opening, the conclusion, and the premise handed to Chapter 4. They require substantive revision before polishing.

All source-specific assessments below concern the chapter’s presentation; the underlying posts and papers remain unverified. Suggested scholarship recalled independently is marked [memory].

## 1. Separate the propositions more carefully

In §1, “(c) requires (a) and (b)” is too strong. Autogynephilic arousal could contribute causally to dysphoria in a continuously varying population without two clusters. Likewise, a causal contribution need not depend on women lacking comparable experiences. Yet (b) includes both prevalence among non-androphilic trans women and comparative absence among women. These are separate propositions with different evidential requirements.

The population claim also needs decomposition. A majority being non-androphilic does not establish a majority having autogynephilic fantasies; neither establishes that such fantasies principally cause transition. The chapter recognizes this in §6, but its initial dependency map obscures it.

More fundamentally, the chapter moves among **sexual orientation, childhood gender expression, age at first dysphoria, age at presentation, and age at transition** as if they identified the same groups. Its own discussion of Lawrence’s childhood-onset figures shows otherwise. Evidence about non-androphilic participants cannot automatically settle a proposition about late-onset dysphoria.

**Proposed replacement for §1, paragraph beginning “The claims are logically independent…”**

*This addresses the faulty dependency map and the conflation of onset with orientation.*

> These propositions require different evidence. Autogynephilic arousal could contribute to dysphoria without dividing trans women into discrete types, and it could do so even if some women experienced comparable arousal. Conversely, an association between sexual orientation and developmental history would not establish the cause of that association. The population claim requires evidence about both the distribution of sexual orientations and the frequency and causal importance of the reported fantasies. Age at first dysphoria, age at transition, and sexual orientation must also remain distinct: a study grouping participants by one does not thereby test a claim about another.

The term “paraphilia” in the heading for (b) also prejudges a disputed classification. Use “arousal pattern” in the study’s analytical voice, reserving “paraphilia” for an attributed theoretical or diagnostic usage. Define what the various instruments actually measure before treating their findings as measurements of one construct.

## 2. Correlations do not yet establish clusters

“The honest summary is that the clusters are real as clusters” (§2) repeats the inference the section criticizes. The evidence presented establishes differences between groups defined in advance by sexual orientation. It does not demonstrate multimodality, latent classes, or even unusually dense regions separated by sparse ones.

The distinction matters for fairness to Serano. Her quoted concession concerns correlations. The opening and conclusion convert it into a concession that “the clusters exist.” That is stronger than the quotation supports. “Nobody informed disputes” adds an unnecessary boundary around legitimate disagreement; describe the agreement actually documented.

There is a corresponding fairness issue toward Davis. A useful descriptive grouping need not be discrete. The chapter should specify whether his claim at each date is a convenient orientation-based partition, statistical bimodality, or two etiologically distinct conditions. His 2021 concession, as quoted, withdraws an inference while retaining a judgment. It does not concede that no two-type model can be useful.

The 2022 model “predicts the cases a strict two-type theory cannot accommodate” is also generous. Without parameters, it **accommodates** those cases. A flexible cultural contribution can explain exceptions after they occur; predictive improvement would require specifying their expected frequency or other independently testable consequences. Ask what observations would count against the model, including against the claim that orientation produces more bimodality than selection at the transition threshold.

## 3. The negative claim is not the best established claim

The most consequential overstatement is:

> “Of the five claims, (d) is the best supported…”

The preceding evidence does not warrant that ranking. The chapter describes one structural study directly addressing untreated non-androphilic trans women, qualified reviews, and larger studies that do not separate participants by orientation. By contrast, the reported association between orientation and erotic cross-dressing appears in several differently recruited samples.

Section 5 slides among four propositions:

- Trans women’s measured brain features generally classify closer to male controls.
- Non-androphilic trans women lack broad feminization across measured features.
- No developmentally important sex-atypical neural mechanism contributes to their dysphoria.
- Their condition is not “intersex.”

The first two do not establish the latter two. Overall classification can coexist with a localized causal difference. A difference need not be large relative to whole-brain variation to matter for a particular experience. Nor does the adjective “mild” follow from anatomical localization. The chapter has no stated measure of the severity of a putative intersex condition.

“The usable form of the claim” is therefore a substantive reconstruction, not a clarification. The study should distinguish Davis’s slogan, his qualified psychological claim, and an operational imaging hypothesis. If “intersex” is a classificatory term, specify the definition; if it is a proposed developmental mechanism, specify the mechanism or observable prediction.

The handoff to Chapter 4 is especially vulnerable. Brain morphology does not directly establish where trans women fall across the psychological dimensions relevant to that chapter’s category argument. A classifier trained to distinguish control groups does not supply that missing psychological evidence.

The Haskell paragraph compounds the problem:

> “trans women in a male-dominated field appear at rates set by the male population…”

The figures reported do not establish this. They require population denominators, comparable response rates, and a defensible model of recruitment and identification. Occupational representation also cannot by itself distinguish interests from education, opportunity, socialization, or selection into this particular survey. Spencer’s “91% male” needs its denominator and classification explained before either his objection or the chapter’s reversal of it is intelligible.

Finally, “a neural correlate, which the T-shirt did not anticipate” is unfair. Denying an intersex explanation does not entail denying neural correlates of a psychological experience. Remove the flourish.

**Proposed replacement for §5’s concluding assessment**

*This narrows the conclusion to what the studies, as described, can support.*

> The studies discussed here provide limited support for a narrower proposition: untreated non-androphilic trans women have not been shown to exhibit broad feminization across the brain measures examined. That finding bears against an account predicting such feminization. It does not establish that no sex-atypical developmental mechanism contributes to their dysphoria, or that any relevant difference would be clinically minor. The larger pooled studies do not resolve this question because they do not distinguish the groups at issue. Davis’s rejection of a wholesale female-brain account therefore has more support than his unqualified rejection of an “intersex condition,” whose meaning and empirical implications remain insufficiently specified.

## 4. Correct the treatment of onset and causation

Section 4 quotes Serano as saying:

> “most ‘nonclassical’ trans women either never experience FEFs, or experience FEFs only after they have experienced gender dysphoria”

It then claims that “the first half is contradicted by the frequencies in §3.” This misreads a disjunction. A majority could satisfy *either no fantasies or later fantasies* even if most participants eventually experienced fantasies. The prevalence figures do not contradict the quoted proposition.

Lawrence’s mean ages do not independently establish temporal precedence either. Mean age of first wishing to change sex must be compared with fantasy onset **within the same participants**, using compatible definitions. Davis’s own puberty chronology cannot supply the missing onset measurement for Lawrence’s sample. The chapter correctly warns against generalizing his personal case elsewhere; it should apply that warning here.

There is a legitimate objection to Serano if the quoted passage treats temporal precedence as ruling out every causal contribution. Fantasies that follow an initial wish could still intensify later dysphoria or affect transition decisions. Conversely, earlier fantasies would not exclude a common cause. Distinguish initiating a wish, sustaining distress, and motivating transition.

The chapter is also too quick to infer a concession from Davis’s “Dumb and Bad” remark. The quoted willingness to criticize a faction does not itself withdraw the androphile argument. Check the full “Useful Approximation” exchange and distinguish conceding a logical objection from conceding that proponents have argued badly.

Finally, §4 ends with “autogynephilia is a strong correlate of transition in non-androphilic males.” Most evidence summarized here samples people already seeking or having undergone transition. High prevalence within that selected population does not establish a strong association with transition without an appropriate comparison group. State the narrower result unless the additional studies provide that comparison.

## 5. Apply measurement skepticism symmetrically

Section 3 raises the right questions but sometimes treats methodological criticism as if it already determined the answer.

“The categorical claim that women are not autogynephilic is false as stated” is stronger than the chapter’s finding that measurement equivalence is unresolved. Ann’s account is important counterevidence and should receive the same initial credibility as Davis’s phenomenological report. But “appears to map” does not settle whether two reports measure the same narrowly defined construct. The defensible conclusion is that categorical exclusion is unsupported and challenged by reports that cannot simply be dismissed.

Likewise, “the prior was tested in public and lost” misdescribes the lesbian-phrasing exchange. What failed was a readily searchable claim about who uses a phrase. Whether its uses describe equivalent experiences remained disputed. Davis’s subsequent interpretation may be ad hoc, but that requires examination of the contexts, not the declaration that an entire prior was defeated.

The chapter is too soft in calling the asymmetry’s basis “defensible” because Davis disclosed his prior. Disclosure makes the reasoning assessable; it does not justify the prior’s strength. Ask what observation could revise a “No Way No Chance” commitment and whether the underlying theory has evidence independent of the disputed comparison.

For Davis’s side, however, the two survey inferences are not identical. Using a question to infer the existence of a familiar experience within one population differs from comparing its prevalence across populations. His Reddit inference remains vulnerable to self-selection and loose wording, but the chapter should reconstruct that distinction before concluding he simply applies one standard selectively.

The four-study discussion needs an evidence table recording recruitment, orientation classification, exact item, reference period, denominators, and response rate. “Some history” collapses clothing-related arousal, adolescent experiences, and broader embodiment fantasies. “In every … sample that has asked” should become “in the four samples examined here” unless a systematic search supports exhaustiveness.

## 6. The population estimate and the chronology of correction

The multiplication in §6 gives a spurious appearance of estimation. The reported inputs do not yield a unique “half to two-thirds” range. For illustration, three-quarters multiplied by two-thirds is one-half; four-fifths multiplied by nine-tenths is 72 percent. More importantly, these quantities come from different populations using different measures. Present this as a sensitivity illustration, not an estimate.

The United States Transgender Survey improves the evidence but does not “fill the gap” between selected samples and the contemporary Western population. Its size does not resolve convenience-sample selection. Nor is an exclusively androphilic share “much above a quarter” a demonstrated upper bound when identity labels and attraction measures are acknowledged to differ.

The cultural prediction is also stronger than the threshold model warrants:

> “the share of trans women for whom autogynephilia is the main cause should fall”

Lower transition costs could admit additional people with weaker autogynephilic motivation, additional people with other motivations, or both. The direction depends on the joint distributions and thresholds. That is precisely what the unfitted model leaves unspecified.

The correction narrative needs more exact chronology. The opening says Davis acknowledged that “the causal claim has not been shown” in 2021. The quoted concession instead withdraws a particular argument while retaining the conclusion on parsimony grounds. Continuing to state that conclusion in “Point Man” is not by itself a reversal. The stronger criticism is that polemical confidence may exceed the evidential support he explicitly acknowledges.

Section 8 should also scrutinize “Point Man” on treatment outcomes. Its reported contrast between groups for whom medicine worked well and less well is a separate empirical claim, not established by the typology or fantasy prevalence. Verify exactly what outcome Davis means and what evidence he supplies.

The closing claim that his wider argument “accounts for much of the hostility it met” needs reception evidence identifying those reasons. Neither vehemence nor a plausible reconstruction of objections establishes why readers reacted.

## 7. Scholarship that should change the assessment

The literature review needs targeted additions, rather than a longer list of names.

- **Foundational construct and classification papers.** Blanchard’s 1989 papers on the concept of autogynephilia and the classification of nonhomosexual gender dysphorias are relevant starting points [memory]. They should clarify what was proposed as descriptive, what was causal, and how exceptions were handled. The 1985 clothing item cannot carry the entire later construct.
- **Moser beyond the small women’s survey.** Moser’s 2010 critique, “Blanchard’s Autogynephilia Theory: A Critique,” addresses the theory more broadly [memory]. It is needed to prevent the critics’ case from being represented mainly by one small adapted-scale study.
- **Veale beyond taxometrics.** Veale, Clarke, and Lomax’s 2008 “Sexuality of Male-to-Female Transsexuals” is relevant to the relationships among orientation, identity, and autogynephilic reports [memory]. Read it alongside the taxometric paper and Lawrence’s response, rather than treating the latter exchange as the full empirical challenge.
- **Alternative developmental models.** Veale, Clarke, and Lomax developed an identity-defense account of gender-variant development [memory]. Its relevance is comparative prediction: which observations would distinguish it from Davis’s threshold model and Serano’s embodiment account?
- **Recent work already promised by the plan.** The planned Morandini, Hsu, and Rudd 2025 paper is absent from the bibliography. Determine whether it contributes independent data or reuses participants. The Hsu, Morandini, and Rudd paper discussed through its abstract and Davis’s draft also requires direct methodological assessment; Davis’s recruitment reconstruction remains his report until checked.
- **Imaging interpretation.** Eliot and colleagues’ 2021 synthesis, “Dump the ‘Dimorphism’,” is relevant to distinguishing overall brain-size effects, multivariate classification, and categorical claims about brains [memory]. It would not settle trans-specific etiology, but could discipline the inference from classification to psychology.

The planned 2026 Lawrence–Bailey–Byrne–Hsu exchange should be located and its accessibility reported. No claim about its contents is warranted from the packet.

The Bailey controversy paragraph currently gives a vivid accusation from Dreger and only a generic notice that critics disagreed. Either read the substantive replies or narrow the paragraph substantially. “Pressure” does not explain recruitment defects without evidence connecting the two. The chapter should not replace methodological assessment with a symmetrical story about incentives.

## 8. Structure, overlap, and prose

The chapter largely respects the planned division between testimony and population science. Retain the explicit statement that the book’s honesty thesis does not decide empirical questions. However, disclosure is credited so frequently that it sometimes substitutes for evaluating the disclosed reasoning. The 2025 draft is the strongest example of the norm in practice; it does not need several weaker parallels.

Move most of the general multivariate-classification exposition toward Chapter 4, retaining here its application and limitations. Reduce repetition of Chapter 2’s Illy quotations and *guys like me* analysis. The space should go to measurement, onset, population definitions, and competing causal predictions.

The opening already delivers nearly every verdict; section endings repeat them; §9 repeats them again. A shorter opening and one carefully qualified conclusion would improve the argument. Replace “the honest summary,” “the right moral,” “the most careful sympathetic reader,” and “the best things” with the reasons for those judgments. These phrases recruit agreement before establishing it. Repeated formulas such as “the point is sound” and “the pattern is real” give the prose a mechanical adjudicative rhythm.

The visible apparatus also needs attention. The supplied chapter has no concluding “Note on method,” despite the brief’s requirement. Its bibliography contains abbreviated URLs and title-only unread sources. These may be conversion artifacts, but substantive arguments should not rely on unread replies. Abstract-only evidence should be identified at the point of inference, particularly in §5.
