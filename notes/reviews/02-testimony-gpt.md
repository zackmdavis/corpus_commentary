# Review of Chapter 2: The Scintillating Thought: Testimony and Self-Report

The chapter’s strongest contribution is its distinction between the evidential value of Davis’s record and the correctness of his explanation. It also gives substantial evidence against an easy accusation of inconsistency: Davis questions his motives, acknowledges differences between himself and transitioners, and permits others to dispute his self-understanding. Those concessions should remain central.

The chapter nevertheless needs revision at its methodological center. It repeatedly turns distinctions of degree into categorical exclusions: experience has authority, causes do not; documents escape interpretation, feelings do not; testimony establishes an individual case, science establishes populations. These divisions obscure how testimony, observation, causal reasoning, and documentary evidence actually interact. The result is occasionally unfair to Davis’s inferences and occasionally too generous to the study’s own.

## 1. Specify what first-person authority amounts to

The opening formulation—“people report their experiences with authority and infer their causes without it”—is stronger than the Davis passages presented here establish. His quoted principle makes self-reports important but defeasible evidence. It does not clearly say that causal self-knowledge has no authority, or that reports of experience possess a uniform kind of authority.

The chapter should distinguish privileged access, presumptive credibility, evidential weight, and immunity from correction. A person can have unusually good evidence about his motives without directly observing the processes that produced them. Conversely, a report of a present feeling can deserve belief without being infallible. This distinction matters throughout, particularly where the chapter says a claim exceeds what “any testimony” can support.

In §2, Nisbett and Wilson’s account is made to explain why Serano’s interpretation “would seem obvious to her.” That moves too quickly from a general account of causal reporting to an explanation of a particular person’s judgment. It also reproduces the practice being criticized: an observer supplies a causal account of someone else’s confidence without examining her full argument. Serano might have compared competing explanations across years of experience. Whether that comparison succeeds is separate from whether its result felt obvious.

The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower: introspective confidence alone does not establish a causal explanation. It does not follow that first-person causal reasoning deserves less weight merely because it is first-person.

**Proposed replacement for §2, paragraph beginning “He drew the line most sharply…”**

*Problem addressed: the paragraph moves from identifying an inference to explaining away its apparent force.*

> Davis correctly identifies Serano’s statement as an explanatory judgment. That classification leaves its evidential merits open. A person may infer causes from repeated experiences, their circumstances, and changes over time, even without introspective access to the processes that produce them. The question is therefore what Serano means by an explanation’s failure to account for nonsexual experiences, and whether the sexual explanation Davis favors can accommodate those experiences without becoming difficult to test. Her confidence does not settle that question; identifying her statement as a hypothesis does not settle it either.

## 2. Documentary survival is not independent corroboration

The opening says Davis wrote as a teenager “before he had any stake in the controversy.” That should become “before his public involvement in the controversy.” The 2006 notebook, as described, already encounters a theoretical vocabulary through Wikipedia. An adolescent can also have personal investments in an interpretation without having a public position to defend.

The conclusion contains a clearer error: “before the controversy existed.” The chapter establishes no such chronology, and its own description of the notebook cannot support it. The relevant protection is against subsequent retrospective revision, not against all prior conceptual influence.

Three evidential levels need separation:

- A dated document records that Davis described or interpreted an experience in a certain way at that date.
- His later account reports behavior and other people’s reactions.
- Independent records or other people’s testimony corroborate that account.

The chapter repeatedly treats the second as approaching the third. “HSTS-taxon boys are identified as effeminate by others” states a proposed criterion; it does not itself supply independent observations about Davis. His recollection that others did not regard him as effeminate remains his testimony about their reactions. Calling this “the strongest methodological move in the personal writing” is premature without identifying the observations, their sources, and their relevance to the hypotheses being compared.

Similarly, growing his hair, taking hormones, and stopping them support the seriousness and practical consequences of his wishes. They discriminate poorly between the competing etiologies discussed here. Behavioral consistency is valuable, but its value depends on the claim being tested.

The last sentence of §1 is also too categorical: “Neither cost nor disclosure, however, makes it better evidence about causes.” Credibility and disclosed selection can affect the weight of observations used in causal reasoning. What they cannot do is supply the missing discrimination between causal models.

**Proposed replacement for §1, paragraph beginning “As evidence the record has properties…”**

*Problem addressed: the paragraph conflates contemporaneous documentation, behavioral consistency, and independent confirmation.*

> The record permits comparisons that retrospective testimony alone cannot. The early documents establish that Davis described the fantasy sexually before his public intervention in the dispute. His later reports can be compared with those documents and with his account of taking and stopping hormones. These comparisons support continuity in his reported desires and interpretations. They provide less direct support for his explanation of their causes. His reports of other people’s reactions introduce potentially independent evidence, but their independence depends on whether those reactions are documented beyond his recollection.

The disclosure argument also needs restraint. Stating that a record is a “counterpoint” identifies its purpose; it does not specify everything selected out. An adversarially selected narrative may disclose its advocacy while leaving readers unable to reconstruct the omitted evidence. Chapter 7’s summary recognizes this distinction. Chapter 2 should use the same standard.

## 3. Introspection research is doing more work than it can bear

The Chivers correlations in §2 do not, by themselves, show that participants misdescribe their phenomenal experience. Agreement between genital response and reported arousal is a relation between two measures. To infer error in the report requires an argument that genital response measures the very experience being reported, or supplies an authoritative criterion for it. The chapter currently slides from disagreement between measures to unreliability about subjective experience.

Nor does the quoted material establish that determining whether a feeling is erotic is equivalent to explaining its cause. A phenomenological description can be interpreted and still answer a different question from a causal account. The chapter rightly complicates Davis’s distinction, but risks dissolving it.

The two remembered misattribution studies should not carry this burden without fuller checking. The sentence that people label “the same bodily state according to the explanation their situation offered” compresses different designs and conclusions into a single demonstration. Check Schachter and Singer and Dutton and Aron separately before retaining that generalization.

The resulting hierarchy—“dates, documents, what others saw” carry the most weight—is also too broad. Dates can be remembered incorrectly; documents record interpretations; observers bring expectations. The important difference is not interpretation versus uninterpreted fact, but which sources constrain which claims, and how independently.

## 4. Test symmetry without penalizing uncertainty or excusing overconfidence

The chapter’s strongest criticism concerns the move from temporal succession to causal priority. It correctly identifies alternative explanations and notices that the early documents do not establish the reported order of onset. But several assessments need tightening.

First, the proposed discrimination from fantasy content is underargued:

> “an account on which the wish is an erotic interest turned on the self predicts that the imagined bodies will vary as the objects of a heterosexual man’s desire vary, and an account based on gender identity does not obviously predict that.”

Why would wishing to inhabit a female body predict narrow preferences about age, race, or build? Why would an erotic account predict indifference across those dimensions? Neither prediction follows without further assumptions. A stable wish concerning bodily sex could coexist with flexibility about other characteristics under either account. Retain the details as valuable testimony, but do not assign them a likelihood advantage without specifying the competing models.

Second, §4 understates the force of Davis’s 2024 qualification. “Introspectively, it seems like a pretty good guess” expresses uncertainty about the proposition that autogynephilia was necessary in his own case. The chapter answers that the fantasy “remains the necessary one.” That confuses the content of the hypothesis with his confidence in it. A tentative hypothesis of necessity is not an assertion of certain necessity. The criticism should identify how much confidence the evidence warrants and where his polemical formulations exceed his qualified formulations.

Third, the conditional advice to “people like me” is not automatically “unexceptionable.” If similarity means sharing the relevant cause, the advice assumes its conclusion. If similarity means sharing orientation, temperament, or childhood presentation, the causal conclusion needs additional support. This is a place where the chapter is too soft on Davis.

Fourth, the proposed alternative that fantasy supplies imagery “without being its cause” needs revision. Supplying imagery may itself be a causal contribution. The intended distinction appears to be between shaping an experience’s content and generating the underlying desire.

Finally, the other-minds criticism is overstated. Davis has no privileged access to women’s experience; it does not follow that he has “no standing” to make claims about it. Testimony and comparative evidence can support such claims. His problem, on the chapter’s presentation, is that the confidence and generality of the claims outrun the comparative evidence supplied.

The “crazy or a liar” passage therefore needs its exact target established. Does Davis reject a causal hypothesis about his feelings, an equivalence between two experiences, or an assertion that a wish establishes female status? Those claims require different criticisms. The chapter currently moves among them.

## 5. Give critics’ objections their full logical force

The treatment of Serano’s objections at the end of §2 suggests that accepting onset reports and criticizing their dismissal are themselves evidence of an inconsistent approach to self-report. They are not. A critic can accept reports provisionally and object to dismissals that lack independent grounds. The relevant question is whether Serano claims immunity from correction or instead disputes the particular evidence offered for correction. That requires checking Serano’s own text, rather than relying on Davis’s report of *Whipping Girl*.

The response to Ozy also needs a qualification. A correlation between socially desirable responding and denial may count against a simple model in which denial is wholly unrelated to response incentives. It does not uniquely establish concealed autogynephilia: question interpretation, other traits, or sampling could also produce associations. The chapter correctly distinguishes aggregate evidence from identification of individuals, but “the hypothesis is testable in aggregate” leaves unspecified which hypothesis and against which alternatives.

Likewise, Jessica Taylor’s quoted chronology does not by itself support the incentive argument. Beginning to identify as a woman after trying estrogen could reflect social incentives, altered experience, practical recognition, or several processes together. Check the full comment before presenting it as evidence of pressure to conform.

There is also an unearned endorsement in §5: Davis’s reply is called “a fair answer” to adrusi’s steroid-synthesis hypothesis. A dismissal of a condition with “no particular symptoms” answers that proposal only if its actual evidence and implications fit that description. Either explain the fit from the exchange or leave the biological evaluation to Chapter 3.

## 6. Rebuild the epistemic-injustice and Bettcher section around narrower claims

Section 6 contains important material, but its central scholarship has the weakest source basis in the chapter.

The statement that a discount based on an identity-independent reason “is not prejudice” is too permissive. A general reason can be applied selectively, exaggerated, or accepted without adequate evidence. Invoking incentives does not settle whether a credibility discount is prejudicial. The chapter should examine whether comparable reports receive comparable scrutiny and whether contrary evidence can alter the assessment.

Conversely, Davis’s anger and sense of betrayal do not establish an “ethically bad affective investment.” The chapter should avoid leaving that inference to proximity. His emotions matter only through a demonstrated effect on his treatment of evidence.

The claim that hermeneutical injustice “fits Davis’s case better than it fits his critics’” is unsupported and later effectively withdrawn. Remove the ranking. More fundamentally, misunderstanding, stigma, and lack of uptake do not by themselves establish the structural conditions the quoted definition requires. The chapter must identify who controls the relevant interpretive resources, which communities exclude which descriptions, and how this affects a person’s ability to understand or communicate experience.

Bettcher’s avowal argument is introduced through an internal contradiction:

> “If that is right, Davis’s reply to Serano addresses the wrong statement.”

The next paragraph says most of what Davis contests is causal or historical. On the evidence quoted here, his reply addresses precisely Serano’s explanatory claim. Avowal theory may show that this reply leaves an identity claim untouched; it does not show that he addressed the wrong claim.

The treatment of Bettcher’s 2014 argument should also suspend the verdict that it is “a weak one to Davis’s reading.” The chapter first says she explicitly declines to adjudicate causal and typological claims, then assesses her account as a weak causal objection. Agreement that sexuality includes a gendered self does not establish agreement about explanatory structure. The description of a constituted erotic relation and the explanation of how an identity develops need to be kept apart.

**Proposed replacement for §6, paragraph beginning “This is a serious objection…”**

*Problem addressed: a conceptual account is assessed as though it were a competing developmental explanation.*

> Bettcher’s account challenges the description of the phenomenon before it addresses its developmental history. Including one’s own gendered body in erotic experience need not, on her account, amount to attraction redirected toward oneself. This matters to Davis’s vocabulary even if it leaves his causal hypothesis open. Whether a gendered erotic self precedes, follows, or develops together with a broader self-understanding requires further evidence. The two accounts may describe overlapping experiences while disagreeing about how those experiences should be individuated and explained.

This passage should be retained only after the original English article is checked. A translation can support provisional discussion, but a fine-grained adverse verdict warrants direct verification.

## 7. Strengthen the decision analysis

The chapter rightly treats Davis’s practical stakes as evidence against the charge that his interest is merely ideological. It should nevertheless challenge the equation between an identity model and infinitely inelastic demand. An intrinsic or enduring characteristic can coexist with price-sensitive choices about interventions. The costs of treatment do not determine whether the underlying identity is intrinsic. “Lesser-Known Demand Curves” may expose a rhetoric that conflates identity and action; the chapter should identify that target rather than endorse the equivalence generally.

The HRT passage requires a similar distinction. “Cause predicts what is bought” is valid only when the proposed causal theory actually predicts treatment response. The inference from an “inner female gender” to a brain designed to “run on” estrogen is a substantive bridging hypothesis, not a consequence of either description. An erotic origin would not, without further evidence, exclude psychological benefit from hormones.

Davis’s strongest defense is therefore that he reasonably wanted information relevant to a consequential decision. That does not establish that resolving this etiological dispute would provide the needed information.

The concluding sentence that introspection cannot answer satisfaction questions “in advance” is unnecessarily absolute. Anticipated preferences can contribute evidence while remaining uncertain. Likewise, a satisfied transitioner’s report may inform another person’s prospects when relevant similarities are established; calling someone “marginal” does not itself quantify how poorly the evidence transfers.

## 8. Scholarship, structure, and prose

The following additions would address specific gaps. All descriptions in this list are from memory and require verification.

- **Richard Moran, *Authority and Estrangement*; Dorit Bar-On, *Speaking My Mind*.** [memory] These offer accounts of first-person authority that are not exhausted by inner observation. They would help distinguish causal self-explanation, avowal, and responsibility for one’s attitudes.
- **Jennifer Lackey, *Learning from Words*.** [memory] Its treatment of testimonial knowledge would help clarify why testimony is not a separate evidential substance displaced when “science” begins. Replace claims that testimony cannot support population conclusions with claims about insufficient sampling and causal identification.
- **Eric Schwitzgebel, “The Unreliability of Naive Introspection”; Russell Hurlburt and Schwitzgebel, *Describing Inner Experience?*** [memory] These would provide a more direct basis for discussing error in reports of experience than genital–subjective concordance alone.
- **Katharine Jenkins, “Amelioration and Inclusion: Gender Identity and the Concept of Woman.”** [memory] Jenkins distinguishes gender identity from a simple belief about one’s biological classification. This would supply a substantive alternative to the chapter’s recurrent contrast between causal hypothesis and ethically authoritative avowal.
- **Philippe Lejeune, *On Autobiography*; Sidonie Smith and Julia Watson, *Reading Autobiography*.** [memory] These address the referential commitments, retrospective construction, and situated address of life writing. They would help evaluate Davis’s notebooks, published diary, retrospective essays, and polemical memoir as different documentary forms rather than one continuous evidential record.

The chapter broadly follows the plan and commendably declines to force the coordination argument into its conclusion. Its main structural problem is repetition: the opening, §§3–4, and conclusion repeatedly announce symmetry, documentary protection, and causal limits. State the evidential distinctions once, then organize the close reading around cases that test them.

Preserve the testimony/population-science seam. Chapter 3’s summary already repeats the 2018 uncertainty, 2024 multiple-causes concession, women’s-arousal dispute, and population claim. Chapter 2 should own their testimonial significance; Chapter 3 should refer back while assessing the empirical literature. General narrative-selection analysis belongs principally in Chapter 7, though the distinction between disclosed purpose and disclosed omissions must remain here.

The prose often substitutes short verdicts for explanations: “Both were right,” “The other half was not,” “The answer is the right kind,” and “That is a fair answer.” These should become sentences specifying what an argument establishes and what remains open. “The same materials, read twice” is evocative but underspecifies a section containing chronology, incentives, alternative causes, and confidence. A title such as “What the personal record supports” would better reflect its work.
