# Review: 03-two-type-taxonomy (Fable)

## General

This is a careful chapter, and draft 2 has already absorbed two rounds of review. The five-claim decomposition in §1 is the right instrument, the re-derivations (Nuttbrock's 73.2 vs 69.0; the Haskell over-representation; D = 1.49 vs 2.7) are the kind of thing a reader cannot get elsewhere, and the verdicts in §9 are proportioned to the evidence. The chapter is also over its ceiling (~9,600 words against 8,800), and some of the excess is repetition and a paragraph (Dreger) that the chapter itself says rests on unread sources.

The comments below are in order of importance: substantive gaps first, then fairness, then scholarship, then prose and length. Everything I say about a paper or a post from memory is marked [memory].

## 1. Substantive gaps and overstatements

**1.1 The proponents' onset evidence is not where the chapter looks for it.** §4 rightly says that order of onset is the evidence that would decide between the causal claim and its rivals, then examines Lawrence 2005 Table VI and finds it cannot show order. But Table VI is a table of age at first wish to change sex, not the proponents' main onset evidence. Their main evidence, as I recall it, is the natural-history literature on cross-dressers: fetishistic cross-dressing beginning at or before puberty, with gender dysphoria emerging years or decades later in a subset [memory: Docter, *Transvestites and Transsexuals* (1988), and the "secondary transsexualism" literature of Person and Ovesey (1974), both predating Blanchard; Lawrence's 2013 narrative collection *Men Trapped in Men's Bodies* presents the same progression in first-person accounts]. Whatever its weaknesses (clinical and self-selected samples; retrospection, which the chapter's Blanchard–Clemmensen–Steiner point already covers), this is the body of evidence Serano's "FEFs only after they have experienced gender dysphoria" has to answer, and the chapter does not mention it. The sentence "the evidence is poor on both sides" is defensible, but the chapter currently shows it only for one side's table. If the main agent does not want to fetch Docter or Lawrence 2013, the chapter should at least say that the proponents' onset case rests on the cross-dresser progression literature, that it was not read, and that the retrospection problem applies to it. Either way the verdict is unchanged; the fairness of the route to it is improved.

**1.2 Moser's age argument has an answer the study has already read.** §4: "Moser's version is harder to answer that way: sexual motives weaken with age, yet 'Yet, older, often autogynephilic, MTFs continue to pursue SRS.'" Lawrence's reply to exactly this objection is that autogynephilia is not only an arousal pattern but a sexual orientation with an attachment component, which persists when arousal fades; Chapter 2 quotes her 2007 phrase "a variety of romantic love". The chapter should cross-reference that reply rather than leave the objection as "harder to answer". Whether the reply succeeds is a fair question (it makes the construct less testable, since the defining arousal can be absent), and the chapter can say so, but presenting Moser's point without the proponents' standard answer is a less charitable reading than the sources support.

**1.3 "Social Construction" does not depend on (e) in its strong form.** §8: "The claim depends on (e) in its strong form and on a theory of political outcomes nowhere argued." The sentence quoted ("Autogynephilic males are better at large-scale coalitional politics than actual lesbians") requires that the people leading trans-movement politics be disproportionately non-androphilic natal males with male-typical traits; that is the weak reading of (e) plus a leadership claim plus the psychological premise of (d). It does not require that they transition *mainly because of* autogynephilia. The chapter's own distinction between readings is undercut when the strong reading is invoked where the weak one would do. The second half of the sentence ("a theory of political outcomes nowhere argued") is the sound objection; keep that and drop the first half.

**1.4 The "online populations" verdict on (e) is borrowed from the side that distrusts those populations.** §6 says that "Online samples elsewhere look similar: in Veale's, by Lawrence's count, at most 18 of 308 were androphilic." But Lawrence's count was made to argue that Veale's online sample was unrepresentative, with far too few androphiles to detect the typology; it is the proponents' standing objection to online samples. The chapter uses it as corroborating evidence about the online population and then, correctly, scopes the verdict to "online populations". The problem is that Davis's claim is about "trans women under modern conditions in Western countries", not online forums, and the clinic and community samples (Nuttbrock's two-thirds androphilic; Toronto 61 percent; the Dutch 54 percent) point the other way. The chapter half-says this through Nuttbrock. It should say it plainly: the non-androphilic majority is probably right for the self-selected online populations the corpus's debates concern, the evidence for the population Davis actually names is divided by recruitment method, and the USTS, being online and young, is subject to the same selection the proponents raise against Veale. (Nuttbrock also reported, if I recall, that the association between non-homosexual orientation and transvestic fetishism was weaker among younger and non-White participants [memory]; if the paper says so, it strengthens the "not the same population" point and should be cited.)

**1.5 The falsifier the chapter says Davis never supplied may be in a draft the plan lists.** §5: "Davis has not said what finding would count as more than mild." The plan's core texts for this chapter include the unpublished draft "Falsifiability and What We Expect Not to See", which the chapter does not cite and the Note on method does not mention. The title suggests it is about exactly this question. If it is, the sentence is false as written; if it is not, the chapter should still say the draft was read and does not supply one. This is the most important check in the list below.

**1.6 Two plan sources on the 2025 studies are absent.** The plan lists "Morandini, Hsu & Rudd 2025 (OA)" as open access, separately from Hsu, Morandini & Rudd 2025; and it lists "Bailey 2022" alongside Serano & Veale 2022. Neither appears in the works cited. If Bailey and Hsu published a rejoinder to Serano and Veale, §3 reports one side's objection to the 2022 study without the reply; it should either report the reply or say it was not read. The open-access 2025 paper, if it reports recruitment, bears directly on the SEXNET draft's sampling objection and on the chapter's remark that "the recruitment details come from his own reconstruction and were not checked". The plan's "referral-demographic shift papers (Aitken et al. 2015; de Graaf et al. 2018)" are also unused; they bear on (e) only indirectly, and dropping them is fine, but the Note on method should say what was sought and not used.

**1.7 The lede's frequency sentence is broader than §3.** "The frequency of the arousal among non-androphilic trans women is high in every sample that has measured it." §3 examines four samples, all clinical, surgical or community, mostly decades old, and the chapter never reports the rate in Veale 2008's non-clinical sample (which measured it) or in any online sample. "Every sample that has measured it" is a claim about the whole literature that the chapter has not surveyed. Say "in the four samples Davis cites" or "in every clinical and community sample examined here". §9 has the same phrase ("in every sample that has asked") and should be corrected with it.

**1.8 A circularity in §1's independence claims.** "The population claim, on the weaker of the two readings distinguished in §6, needs only (a) and (b)." The weak reading as §6 defines it (a majority not exclusively androphilic; most of these with some history of the arousal) needs (b) and an orientation distribution, not (a) in the sense of clusters. Since the chapter's §2 verdict is that clusters are open while correlations are established, it matters that (e)-weak does not depend on clusters. Replace "(a) and (b)" with "the orientation distribution and (b)".

**1.9 Veale 2008 and Veale 2014: 234 vs 308.** The chapter gives Veale, Clarke and Lomax 2008 a sample of 234 and Veale 2014 a sample of 308. If the 2014 taxometric analysis reused the 2008 data set (I do not remember whether it did [memory]), one of these numbers is wrong; if they are different samples, a reader will want a word saying so.

## 2. Fairness to Davis, in both directions

**2.1 The sampling critique is applied to Davis's poll and not to tailcalled's surveys.** §3 rightly says the r/MtF poll has validity and sampling problems "Davis would raise against a survey whose result he doubted". The chapter then relies, at three points, on tailcalled's own survey findings as evidence: that "autogynephilia in cis women and autoandrophilia in cis men does sometimes exist" (§3), the two-way-causation guess (§4), and the multivariate brain summary (§5). tailcalled's surveys are, as far as the chapter tells us, unreviewed online convenience samples; the chapter flags the brain summary's limits but not the others. Apply the same standard once, in a sentence: tailcalled's findings are reported as his, from samples of the same kind the chapter has just discounted. Otherwise the asymmetry the chapter charges Davis with is one it commits.

**2.2 "Davis called that model a stretch without saying why."** Check "You Are Right and I Was Wrong" for whether the "a stretch" remark carries any reason (even a clause). If it does, the sentence is unfair; if it does not, keep it. Also confirm that the remark is about the identity-defense model specifically and not about something else tailcalled raised.

**2.3 "He left the sentence in the post."** §4 faults Davis for not editing the androphile argument out of "Useful Approximation" after conceding the faction in comments. This is a small demand that the chapter elsewhere does not make of anyone else (Serano's 2020 paper is not faulted for not being revised after Bailey and Hsu). Either make the point once, as part of the §8 "did not go back and narrow the essays" observation, or drop it here.

**2.4 Too soft: the T-shirt's accusation.** §1 quotes the 2016 T-shirt ("…NOT AN INTERSEX CONDITION, YOU LYING BASTARDS") and §5 then finds that the proposition rests on "very little evidence either way". The chapter never remarks that the 2016 formulation charged someone with lying about a claim for which, on the chapter's own finding, the evidence was and is thin in both directions. In a study whose thesis separates the norm of accounting from the inference to motive, this is the clearest case in the gender writing of a motive attribution outrunning the evidence, and it is in a vignette rather than an argument, which is itself a point for Chapter 8. One sentence in §8's baseline paragraph would do: the 2016 line asserts not only the proposition but that its deniers know better, and nothing in the evidence the chapter has reviewed supports the second part. (Note that "a vignette had its narrator wearing" is the right description; the chapter should not treat fiction as assertion, but the 2024 restatement without the insult shows Davis owned the proposition.)

**2.5 Too soft or too compressed: "the minimum ask".** §7 says the first half of the ask ("clearly a causal factor in many gender problems") is "somewhat stronger than the literature warrants". The chapter's own §4 verdict is "plausibly among the causes of transition for many of them". The gap is between "clearly" and "plausibly", and the chapter should say that is the whole gap, so that a reader does not take "somewhat stronger" as a vaguer discount than the chapter means.

**2.6 Possibly unfair: "did not go back and narrow the essays".** The main narrowing of the considered position came in 2021–22 (tailcalled, "Useful Approximation"), and "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea" (2023) postdates it and carries "approximately". The later narrowing (Illy 2024, the SEXNET draft 2025) postdates one essay. "The essays" in the plural suggests a pattern across many texts; what the record shows is one essay not revised after one podcast. State the number.

**2.7 "Point Man" and the single link.** The sentence "a claim about outcomes supported by a single link to a blog post about passing" is checkable and, if true, damaging. Confirm that no other support is given in the post for the claim that medicine worked less well for the second group.

## 3. Scholarship

- The history of the typology predates Blanchard. The "primary/secondary" and "nuclear/fetishistic" distinctions in Person and Ovesey and in Buhrich and McConaghy [memory, 1970s] observed the same correlations clinically; Blanchard's contribution was the orientation criterion and the autogynephilia construct. Serano 2020, I believe, mentions this history [memory]. One sentence in §2 would give the correlations their proper age and make "the critics concede them" less surprising.
- Blanchard's 1989 papers, which collapsed the three non-homosexual groups into one on the strength of their similarity, are the empirical basis for *two* types rather than four [memory: "The classification and labeling of nonhomosexual gender dysphorias", *Arch Sex Behav* 1989]. The chapter gets the same point from Blanchard 1985 Table II; fine, but the 1989 cluster analysis is the paper usually cited.
- Blanchard 1992 on the nonmonotonic relation between autogynephilia and heterosexual attraction [memory] is the finding Lawrence uses to argue that autogynephilia competes with partner-directed sexuality, which is relevant to Moser's age objection (2.2 above). Optional.
- The 2026 *Archives* exchange: the Note on method says it was sought and not read but gives no titles. The plan said "titles only, say so". Add the titles to the works cited, marked unread.
- Guillamon's review: the chapter quotes "Cantor seems to be right." Cantor's prediction, as I recall, was that non-homosexual MtFs' brains would differ from controls in regions related to body perception rather than in sexually dimorphic regions [memory]. If the chapter's gloss ("a prediction by Blanchard and James Cantor along these lines") is from the review, fine; if it is a reconstruction, say whose.

- Unused core texts from the plan: "Nothing New Under the Sun" (2017), "Terminology Proposal: 'Developmental Sex'" (2019), "Falsifiability and What We Expect Not to See", "Faster Than Science", *Closetspace*/*Lease Bound*, the *Human Diversity* review's Appendix-2 remarks. "Choir" is in the works cited and nowhere in the text; the unattributed "about 3% of men" draft quotation in §4 is probably it, and should be cited by name there. The Note on method should list the plan's core texts that were read and not used, as other chapters do.
- The formula "met the norm of accounting in its qualifications and failed it in its emphasis" appears three times (§8 on "Point Man", §8 motte-and-bailey paragraph, §9). Once is a finding; three times is a refrain. Keep it in §8's motte-and-bailey paragraph and vary the other two.
- §7's Univariate Fallacy paragraph overlaps Ch. 4's treatment; the chapter says Ch. 4 "takes up the use Davis made of the result". The paragraph can be cut to the Joel/Del Giudice priority point and the independence caveat.
- The chapter's handoff to Ch. 4 (the psychological premise) is consistent with Ch. 4's summary. The handoff to Ch. 2 on "guys like me" is consistent. No drift found against the thesis; the chapter correctly says the thesis applies "only at its edges", as the plan instructed.

## 5. Prose, structure, length

- §5's second paragraph (Zucker, Luders, Guillamon, Savic & Arver, Cantor) is compressed past clarity, with note 1 carrying a discrepancy between the reviews that a reader needs in the text. Restructure: first the Guillamon sentence that structural evidence for the non-homosexual group is one study, then the functional findings pointing the other way, then the discrepancy over Luders in a clause. Proposed text below.
- Dreger paragraph (§8): the chapter reports her conclusion, says the commentaries and the public accounts of the people she names were not read, and reports her conclusion "as hers". The brief says that where the corpus narrates a dispute the study looks for the other parties' accounts before characterizing it. Either do the reading (Conway's and James's pages are public; the commentaries ran in the same issue) or cut the paragraph to the two sentences about Davis's own stance (the email subject line; the ITT remark), which is what the plan asked for ("only as it bears on how evidence was produced and received"). The paragraph's best sentence, the Murray-review comparison, survives either way.
- Announcing: "Whether he wrote as if this were so is the question of §8." (§1); "§4 returns to it." (§2); "the chapter ends by asking what that gap amounts to" (lede). The last is acceptable in a lede; cut the first two.
- "The selection deserves more notice." (§3) and "The point is sound, and it applies to every comparison…" are short verdict sentences; harmless singly.
- §9: "that gap explains some of the hostility the theory met in his hands" is a causal claim about readers' reactions for which the chapter gives no reception evidence. "May account for" or a cross-reference to Chapter 2's reception section.
- §9 uses "men like him"; §1 and §6 quote "guys like me". Keep the quoted form or mark the paraphrase.
- Length: cutting the Dreger paragraph to two sentences, the refrain to one instance, and §7's univariate paragraph by half recovers roughly 500 words; the additions proposed below cost perhaps 250.

## 6. Proposed prose

**Proposed replacement for §5, paragraph beginning "The imaging evidence that separates the groups is very small."** (Addresses compression; moves the one-study point to the front and the Luders discrepancy into the text.)

> The imaging evidence that separates the groups by orientation is very small. Guillamon, Junque and Gómez-Gil, reviewing the structural studies, put it plainly: “All we know about the morphology of the brain of nonhomosexual MtFs comes from a single VBM study (Savic & Arver, 2011).” That study found the brains of non-androphilic trans women not feminized but unusual in regions where men and women do not differ, regions associated with the perception of one's own body: “In summary, the cortex of nonhomosexual MtFs presents morphological peculiarities in regions in which male and female controls do not differ.” The reviewers judged that a prediction by Blanchard and James Cantor along these lines had held up so far (“Cantor seems to be right.”) and that “to fully confirm the hypothesis, more independent studies on nonhomosexual MtFs are needed” (Guillamon, Junque and Gómez-Gil 2016). The 2016 review of adult gender dysphoria, of which Lawrence was a coauthor, reached the same conclusion in stronger words, “Overall, evidence supports the sexual differentiation hypothesis in homosexual MtFs, but not in nonhomosexual MtFs,” resting it on two gray-matter studies, one of which the other review classes as mixed by orientation.1 The same review reports functional findings that point the other way: “In nonhomosexual MtFs, a femalelike response in hypothalamic activation while smelling odorous steroids (Berglund et al. 2008) and brain activity while viewing erotic videos (Gizewski et al. 2009) were detected, arguing for sex-atypical reactions in these groups.” It also reports greater cortical thickness than in control men, and white-matter differences from control men in a sample mixed by orientation (Zucker, Lawrence and Kreukels 2016, 232–233). tailcalled asked in the LessWrong thread whether the manual's section on the subject had been written under a friend of Blanchard and Bailey (tailcalled, 9 Jul 2023).2 The structural evidence for (d) is one study; the functional evidence is two studies pointing the other way.

**Proposed addition to §4, after the paragraph ending "…including Davis's own (Chapter 2, §4)."** (Addresses 1.1. Needs the main agent to confirm or label the memory claims.)

> The proponents' own evidence for the order of onset is not in Lawrence's tables but in the older clinical literature on cross-dressers, which describes fetishistic cross-dressing beginning around puberty and gender dysphoria emerging, in a minority, years or decades later [memory: Docter 1988; Person and Ovesey 1974; Lawrence 2013]. [QUOTE NEEDED: a sentence from one of these, or from Lawrence 2013 or Blanchard, stating the progression from fetishistic cross-dressing to dysphoria.] That literature was not read for this study. Its samples are clinical and self-selected, and its histories are retrospective, so it is subject to the same distortion the Toronto group documented; it establishes that the progression occurs, not how often, and it is silent about people who never cross-dressed.

**Proposed replacement for §4, sentence beginning "Moser's version is harder to answer that way".** (Addresses 1.2.)

> Moser's version does not have that flaw: sexual motives weaken with age, yet “Yet, older, often autogynephilic, MTFs continue to pursue SRS.” (Moser 2010, 805). Lawrence's answer, quoted in Chapter 2, is that autogynephilia is an orientation with an attachment component, “a variety of romantic love,” which outlasts arousal. The answer saves the theory at a cost to its testability, since the defining arousal may then be absent in the people the theory is about.

**Proposed replacement for §8, sentence beginning "The claim depends on (e) in its strong form".** (Addresses 1.3.)

**Proposed replacement for §3, after "…(tailcalled, 9 Jul 2023)" at the end of the KartoffelElf/Ann paragraph.** (Addresses 2.1.)

**Proposed replacement for the Dreger paragraph in §8, if the commentaries are not read.** (Addresses §5, Dreger.)

> Davis's attitude to the controversy over Bailey's *The Man Who Would Be Queen* (2003) was partisan from the start: a 2016 email to Alexander, as he reports it, carried the subject line “J. Michael Bailey did nothing wrong”, and his 2017 commentary on the Turing test held that “the critics who disparage Queen as "unscientific" are missing the point” because a popular book need not marshal all the evidence. The controversy itself, and the dispute over Alice Dreger's history of it (Dreger 2008), are not assessed here; the other parties' accounts were not read. What bears on this chapter is the standard.
