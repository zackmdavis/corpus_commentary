# Review: 07-whole-dumb-story (Fable)

## Summary judgment

This is a strong chapter and, on the plan's hardest assignment, a fair one. It does what the plan required that the earlier drafts of the thesis could not: it keeps the textual claim, the role-relative charge and the motive inference apart, gives each its own verdict, and uses Davis's own theory of deception as the instrument for assessing his treatment of Yudkowsky. The self-portrait section (§5) and the new paragraph on costs imposed on others are the right correction to a reading that would otherwise reward candor with lenience. The press section is honestly scoped.

Its weaknesses are of three kinds. First, the chapter three times uses the September 2020 clarification as the fact Davis's case "has to reckon with" without ever presenting Davis's reasons for discounting it, which the plan asked for and which fairness requires. Second, the chapter's main critical point, that the 2021 disclaimer was a partial accounting Davis should have credited, may conflate two different filters, and the chapter's own evidence base for that disclaimer is Davis's quotation of a login-walled post. Third, the "established" verdict on the textual claim is stated without the caveat that two of its three documents are known only through Davis. There are also a counting inconsistency in the installments, a handful of jargon terms left unglossed for the brief's reader, and a few inferences that press harder on Davis than the evidence given supports (the Metz comparison, the "continued after the grievance was settled" argument).

## 1. The September 2020 clarification: Davis's side is missing

The plan's entry for this chapter says the 2020 concession is to be "weighed with Davis's own account of why it was insufficient (late, private-seeming, undone by the February 2021 pronoun post)." The chapter invokes the clarification as a counterweight three times: in the role section ("the correction of September 2020 counts against a pattern of refusing correction, and Davis acknowledged it at the time as success"), in §4 ("Yudkowsky's correction of 2020 is again the fact it has to reckon with"), and in the conclusion. At no point does it state why Davis thought the correction insufficient. A reader is left with the impression that Davis got what he asked for in 2020 and went on complaining, which is the uncharitable reading the plan warned against.

Whatever Davis's reasons are (the plan lists three; the chapter should check the memoir for his own statement of them, probably in "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them" and the opening of "Agreeing With Stalin"), they need to appear where the clarification is first used as a counterweight, and the chapter should say whether they hold. Two of them bear directly on the Gould criterion the chapter adopts. "Without informing others of the criticism" is a condition about the audience that received the original: if the 2018 thread reached a hundred and forty likes on Twitter and the 2020 clarification was a Facebook post to a different audience, the correction informed fewer of the people who had been misinformed, and that is the kind of fact the chapter's own Chapter 5 standard (disclosure must reach the listeners who decoded the original) makes relevant. The "undone by February 2021" reason is tied to the chapter's decision, discussed next, that the 2021 proposal is not a repeat departure.

**Proposed addition to §2, "The role-relative charge," after "and Davis acknowledged it at the time as success."**

> Davis's later discounting of that success rests on three things, which he states: [QUOTE NEEDED: the memoir's own reasons, which the plan summarizes as lateness, the venue, and the 2021 proposal]. The first two are complaints about reach, and on the theory of disclosure this chapter applies they are not nothing: a correction that reaches a smaller audience than the error does not restore the map for the readers who received the error. The third depends on whether the 2021 proposal repeated the departure or made a different mistake, and the chapter has argued that it made a different one. Taken together they reduce the credit the clarification earns without removing it.

(The problem addressed: the clarification is used against Davis three times and his reasons for discounting it appear nowhere.)

## 2. Is the 2021 proposal really "not a departure"?

The chapter decides that proposing a pronoun convention "with its reasons" is not a departure from the philosophy of categories, "which allows announced conventions; the fault is in the accounting of costs." But the chapter's own summary of Davis's objection has two parts: the proposal understated its costs *and* "misdescribed the meaning pronouns already carry." The second is not a cost-accounting error. If the 2008 philosophy's point was that words carry inferential load and cannot be redefined by fiat without that load coming along, then a proposal that says a pronoun under the new protocol carries no information about sex, when in actual usage it does, repeats the 2018 error in a new form: it treats a word's meaning as settled by announcement. The chapter should argue, not assert, that the misdescription belongs with the costs rather than with the departure. Chapter 4 may already have done this work (its summary says announced conventions are allowed and the 2021 reply was "judged sound"), in which case a sentence pointing to where Chapter 4 settles it would do. As it stands, the chapter's verdict that the textual claim is "established for one statement of 2018 and weaker elsewhere" rests partly on a classification it has not defended, and the classification is the one most favorable to Yudkowsky.

## 3. The disclaimer: two filters, and whose text

The chapter's main critical point is that Yudkowsky's 2021 disclaimer was a disclosed filter of the kind "Heads I Win" says turns distortion into missing information, and that Davis "counted the claim and not the warning." Three things about this.

(a) As the chapter quotes it, the disclaimer discloses a filter on the *audience's* evidence: good counterarguments would not be voiced in public, only Davis would say anything, "This is a filter affecting your evidence." That is a warning about the reception the reader will see. Davis's complaint, as the chapter states it, is about the *post's own* selectivity: it omitted the obvious costs of its proposal. "Heads I Win" concerns an author's selection within his own report. A disclaimer that says "you will not hear many replies" does not disclose "my argument leaves out the costs"; and the confidence clause ("I can independently invent everything important on the other side") says the opposite, that the post's own selection is complete. On this reading the disclaimer is a partial accounting for a different filter from the one Davis charged, and the credit the chapter wants Davis to have given is smaller than the chapter allows. The chapter should either show that the disclaimer also covered the post's own omissions, or narrow its criticism of Davis to the reception filter. This is the chapter's central original point, so it is worth getting exactly right.

(b) The disclaimer "named its most persistent source." The chapter reads the naming of Davis as helping readers adjust. The other reading, which a hostile reader will supply, is that naming the one dissenter as a known obsessive marks the counterarguments as idiosyncratic and reduces adjustment. Both readings should be on the page; the chapter can still prefer the first.

(c) The disclaimer and the post it sits in are known to the study only as Davis quotes them (the Note says the February 2021 Facebook post is login-walled). The chapter's criticism of Davis for under-crediting a text, where the study's only access to the text is Davis's quotation of it, needs that caveat at the point of use, not only in the Note. The same applies to the verdict "The textual claim, so restricted, is established": of its three documents, only the 2018 thread was read in the archive. "Established" should be qualified to "established on the texts as Davis quotes them, which, where they could be checked, are accurate."

**Proposed replacement for the sentence in §2 beginning "Both objections are sound, and the first is stronger than a simple account of disclosure would allow":**

> Both objections are sound. The disclaimer, moreover, disclosed a filter on what readers would hear in reply; it did not disclose, and its claim of confidence denied, any selection within the post's own argument, which is where Davis located the omission. What the post gave its readers was an accounting of the reception and an assurance about the argument, the first partly undone by the second. That is less than concealment, and more than Davis's verdict allows for, but the credit it earns is for the warning about replies, not for the argument's completeness.

(The problem addressed: the draft's sentence is hard to parse, and it treats the reception filter as if it were the argument filter.)

## 4. The role claim is thinner on the page than in the record

"Whether Yudkowsky claimed the role that would make the standard apply is a further question. The memoir's evidence is a handful of remarks." The chapter then finds "an aspiration and a public persona more securely than a promise." This understates the case on Davis's side. The role does not rest mainly on remarks. Yudkowsky wrote a multi-year sequence of essays presented as instruction in how to think, around which an institution and a community organized, and in 2007 he applied the very standard at issue to a scientist whose offense was misrepresenting a field to the public that trusted him. The chapter cites the Gould post for the standard but not for the parity: the author of "Beware of Stephen J. Gould" had, by his own criterion, opted into the role of someone who informs the public and must inform them of criticism. The study should say that the role claim rests on the Sequences as a body of teaching and on Yudkowsky's own application of the standard to others, and only secondarily on the "aspires" and "shut up and listen" remarks. That strengthens Davis's premise, and the chapter can still hold that a public teaching role is not a promise of consistency on every topic. (Check whether the "aspires to make sure [his] departures from perfection aren't noticeable" line is from a serious or a joking context; the chapter says "some of the remarks are jokes" without saying which.)

A related point the chapter should make against Davis's standard, since it makes it fair to Alexander. A standard that applies only to those who *claim* authority, and exempts those who disclaim it, is gameable: an author can keep the influence and shed the standard by saying he is "some guy with a blog." Alexander's categories essay was, by Davis's own account, the most influential statement of the view Davis spent five years fighting. Chapter 5 found that high standards "protect optimized deniability"; the opt-in standard has the same shape, and the chapter should notice that Davis's exemption of Alexander, while consistent, applies a criterion his own theory would treat with suspicion if an opponent used it.

## 5. The coordination argument section: permission or obligation?

"Here the norm admits no price." The Xu passage supports this: "I'm fine with my inferences causing more harm than benefit." But two cautions. First, that passage is an unpublished draft's reply to a list of cruxes, and the chapter calls it "the plainest form" of (c); the chapter should say in the sentence, not only by citation, that it is from a draft. Second, the published passage is quoted as saying one must "be able to say" what sex trans women are "even if it probabilistically contributes to that person's suicide." "Be able to" is about permission, not obligation. A norm that forbids forbidding a truth is weaker than a norm that requires stating it regardless of harm; the first is a free-speech principle, the second is the sacred value. The chapter should check the passage's modality. If it is "must be able to," the sentence "the same installment that allowed that deceiving readers might be right denies that sparing them could be" overstates: a permission to speak does not deny that one may spare.

The paragraph's closing judgment ("he answered them with a sacred value he did not hold") is one of the chapter's best lines and should stay.

## 6. Where the chapter is harder on Davis than its evidence supports

**The Metz comparison (§7).** "The restraint shows that Davis could apply the discipline of attribution when he chose to, which makes its absence toward Yudkowsky harder to excuse." The two cases differ in evidence, not only in choice. Davis had years of public statements by Yudkowsky and the comparisons the chapter credits; he had a few hours with Metz. The "too self-centered" reply is a competing-hypothesis argument, which is the discipline the chapter asks for, but it is a cheap one to apply when the attributor has no stake and little data. The asymmetry in confidence may track the asymmetry in evidence. The chapter should either show that Davis's confidence about Yudkowsky exceeded what his larger evidence base supports (which §2 already argues, so a cross-reference would do) or drop the inference from Metz.

**"Continued at full intensity after the grievance was settled" (§6).** By the memoir's own account the grievance was reopened in February 2021, five months after the September 2020 settlement. The writing "after the war ended" that the chapter can point to is within that window. Five months is thin support for "the writing survived the loss of its fuel," especially since the memoir itself, the largest body of post-2020 writing, is fuelled by the 2021 reopening. The verdict in §6 may still be right; this leg of it is weak and should be weighed as such.

**Habryka as "independent."** Twice the chapter treats Habryka's comment as evidence: that the grievance "did not require Vassar to be seen" (fair, since Habryka had no Vassar), and, in the motive section, as "an independent reader who drew a similar conclusion from the same behavior." A reader persuaded by Davis's post is evidence that the post is persuasive to someone not predisposed, which is evidence about the argument; it is not additional evidence about Yudkowsky's motives. The second use should be reworded to say what it shows.

## 7. Where the chapter is softer than it should be

**"Nothing found in this study suggests that Davis misquotes his opponents."** True of quotation, and the chapter's check (one archived thread) is honestly described. But Chapters 2 and 3 found Davis misreading opponents (the ITT post's "confident misreads"; the Taylor chronology). The sentence should be narrowed to quotation, since a reader will take "does not misquote" as "characterizes accurately," which is the question §2 exists to answer.

**Privacy of the Discord transcripts.** The chapter credits Davis's privacy rule and then quotes Yudkowsky and Lintamande at length from the Discord messages transcribed in "Standing Under the Same Sky." Were those messages public? If the server was private or semi-private, Davis's draft transcribing them sits in the same relation to his rule as his quotation of Alexander's emails, which the chapter criticizes; and the study's reproducing them inherits the question. The draft presumably says what the server's status was. The chapter should state it, and if the messages were not public, say why the study quotes them (the draft is publicly accessible, the speakers are public figures on the subject) and apply the same scrutiny it applies to the Alexander emails.

**The "I hate them, too!" line.** The chapter says "in part," which is the right caution, but a reader who has not read the memoir will take the sentence at face value. If the context narrows "them" (to a kind of activist, say), the chapter should say so in a clause; if it does not, the chapter should say that too.

## 9. Scholarship and context

The packet asked about journalism and histories of the community, writing on authority and charisma, and memoir as evidence. All from memory, to be fetched or labeled:

- **Bromley's typology** [memory]. Bromley's 1998 volume distinguishes three exit roles, defector, whistleblower and apostate, by the organization's standing with the wider society and the exiter's alliance with an outside audience. On that scheme Davis is not an apostate (he stays, and attacks from inside) and his dealings with Metz are the whistleblower's move: taking the dispute to an external audience that the organization regards as hostile. This connects §1's genre caution to §7's press section, which currently stand apart, and it gives the "heresy blog, not an apostate blog" line a sociological reading Davis did not supply.
- **Heresy as the insider's deviation** [memory: Lester Kurtz, "The Politics of Heresy," *American Journal of Sociology* 1983]. Heretics are typically those closest to the center, and institutions treat the near-insider's deviation as more threatening than outsiders' unbelief. This is a better explanation of the community's reaction to Davis (and of his to Yudkowsky) than the "robot cult" frame alone, and it supports the chapter's judgment that the religion frame explains intensity better than it justifies the charge.
- **Weber on charismatic authority** [memory]. The charismatic claim must be continually "proved" to followers, and fails when it is not. Davis's "false advertising" charge is, in these terms, a follower reporting that the proof failed; the ice-cream analogy he accepts is Weber's point made against himself. One sentence would do.
- **Journalism.** The plan listed Chivers 2019 and Lewis-Kraus 2020 as outside accounts of the community; the chapter uses neither and the Note mentions only Metz's articles. If they were not read, the Note should say so, since the press section otherwise rests entirely on Davis's transcripts of his own interviews.
- **Memoir as evidence.** The chapter's best methodological move, corroborating the memoir's retrospective account of costs against posts written at the time ("before the memoir gave them a story"), is stated only in §5. It deserves a sentence in §1 as the chapter's method: contemporaneous posts are used to check the retrospective narrative wherever both exist.

## 10. Structure, clarity, prose

- **Installment counting is inconsistent.** The lede says "five installments." §1 lists Sexual Dimorphism, BDI, Hill, If Clarity, Stalin. But the chapter then calls Hill "the second installment" (the Valentine and Max H comments are on Hill), If Clarity "the third" ("outright bad faith" footnote; "probabilistic inference"), and Stalin "the last." That is a four-count with BDI first. Either Sexual Dimorphism is a prologue and the count is four, or it is the first and the ordinals are off by one. Fix throughout; also "the second installment treats as the casus belli" in §1 (is that BDI or Hill?).
- **"Social Strategy Notes, March 2019"** is cited in text as "notes of 2019," in works cited as "(dated 2020)," with a filename saying February. Reconcile.
- **Unglossed jargon** for the brief's reader: "cheerful price" (a price at which one would gladly do the task, used here as an offer of money for a reply), "quokka policy" (the community's self-mocking image of itself as a trusting animal that gets eaten; gloss needed, with source), "Isso" (the blog's comment system), "dath ilan"/"Planecrash" (glossed only glancingly), "robot cult" (fine, Davis's phrase, but attribute). "Casus belli" is fine.
- **"These are the functional attributions he makes of Yudkowsky, made of himself, and they are offered without the hedges he declined to give Yudkowsky."** On first reading this seems to say the opposite of what it means. Suggest: "and he offers them with no more hedging than he gave Yudkowsky."
- **Short verdict sentences** the brief flags: "The study does not discount the memoir for its genre. It examines its claims." and "whether his advocacy discloses its filter. It does." Both can be folded into their neighbors.
- **"The norm of accounting"** appears often enough in this chapter to read as machinery. Several instances can be "the demand that the cost be stated" or simply dropped where the sentence already says what the norm requires.
- **"Kolmogorov complicity, in Alexander's parable, is the scholar's choice to keep silent"** [memory: the parable may also involve the scholar making required ritual affirmations, not only silence; if so, "Davis's complaint is that Yudkowsky did not keep silent" loses some of its neatness, since affirming the orthodoxy is part of the Kolmogorov option]. Check against the essay, which the plan lists as external.
- **Length.** At the ceiling. §4 could lose a third without loss (the Vassar quotations can be reduced to the two that matter, "criminals or enemy combatants" and "less precise targeting"); the cost catalogue in §5 could lose two items.
- **The ending** is good and should not be touched.
