# Deniably Allegorical: The Forms

The chapter identifies an important problem: Davis’s literary devices alter what he commits himself to, how readers encounter an argument, and what criticism he exposes himself to. Its strongest material is Davis’s commentary on those devices. But it too often accepts that commentary as an adequate account of how the writing works. The resulting argument also presses unlike forms into one developmental story: deniability first, disclosure later, self-application throughout. The examples establish several uses of indirection; they do not yet establish that trajectory.

The revision should distinguish fictional speech from evasive speech, examine what each story makes its reader accept, and replace general judgments of literary success with close readings. All source-dependent objections below concern the chapter’s presentation; the named texts require checking.

## 1. The central argument needs narrower claims

The opening says that the devices conflict with accounting “since deniability is a way of declining to say what one means.” This assumes the point at issue. Writing a dialogue can distinguish entertaining an argument from endorsing it. An allegory can announce its political application while withholding names. A poem can articulate uncertainty without concealing a settled proposition. None necessarily involves declining to disclose what the author believes.

The chapter needs to separate at least three questions: whether a character’s assertions belong to Davis; whether a work’s application is identifiable; and whether the work conceals something readers need to assess its argument. Those questions receive different answers in “October Eleventh,” “Blegg Mode,” and the AI dialogues. Grouping them under deniability makes legitimate provisional thinking look suspicious while making potentially manipulative analogy look like ordinary literary freedom.

There is also an internal problem. Section 3 acknowledges that the tag supplies “an accounting of a kind,” and §2 finds an explicit disclosure of humor’s function in “Interlude III.” Yet the conclusion returns to a broad conflict between indirection and accounting. These concessions should change the claim, rather than merely accompany it. Under the study’s own account of disclosed filters, a prominently marked allegory may already satisfy the relevant obligation. What further information must Davis supply, and why?

The developmental claim is especially weak:

> “Davis knew this, theorized it, and over fifteen years moved from deniability toward disclosure.”

The chapter begins with self-mockery in 2011 and an explicit convention for marking mixed commitment in 2012. It later finds praise of deniability in 2023 and a tagged draft in 2025. This is evidence of continuing alternatives, not a demonstrated transition. The 2026 comment establishes a specific acknowledgment about one AI dialogue. It does not establish that Davis renounced the device generally, or even that the views acknowledged in 2026 had been settled when he wrote that dialogue.

Section 4 then makes retrospective disclosure do too much:

> “It is the best model the corpus offers for how deniability and the norm of accounting can be reconciled, by sequence rather than compromise.”

Later disclosure cannot automatically repair an earlier misleading impression. Some readers will never encounter it; others may already have acted on the original text. Conversely, if the original dialogue accurately marked uncertainty, nothing required repair. The chapter must first establish what readers were invited to believe, and whether that invitation was misleading. Otherwise “disclosure by sequence” becomes an exemption from the book’s central standard.

**Proposed replacement for the opening paragraph’s thesis sentences**

*Problem addressed: conflation of literary indirection with concealment, and an unsupported developmental arc.*

> Davis’s commentary distinguishes several reasons for speaking indirectly. A dialogue permits him to try an argument without endorsing it; an allegory can leave its application unstated; a joke can make a disputed claim available without requiring an immediate confrontation. These devices raise different questions about candor. The relevant question is what readers need to know to assess the claim being made. The AI dialogues provide one documented case in which Davis later specified his commitment more fully. Elsewhere, disclosure and deniability remain concurrent practices.

## 2. Dialogue and humor: identify the actual mechanism

Section 1 moves too quickly from Synthia’s embarrassment to “The pedant is the author’s double and the dialogue is on the friend’s side.” The quoted sentence is explicitly Quiana’s interpretation of a face. Its mediation matters: Synthia’s apparent self-condemnation arrives through another character’s reading. Does the scene endorse Quiana’s conventional position, expose Synthia’s inconsistency, or preserve Synthia’s objection while showing that she cannot live by it? Hypocrisy alone does not refute her argument about celebrations. “Dialogue Concerning Birthdays” deserves a few sentences on that distinction.

Similarly, “They were easier because they externalized the argument the author was having with himself” turns an interpretation into a causal explanation of composition. “Genre,” as summarized here, says dialogues are easier; it does not establish why. Attribute the explanation to the study unless another passage supplies it.

The treatment of “Interlude II” is more promising. Announcing a reservation price makes the speaker vulnerable, and his correction compromises the posture of unconditional resistance. But the chapter identifies this immediately with the anti-extortion argument later discussed in Chapter 10. Give the scene room to complicate that argument. Is the speaker proud of his honesty, embarrassed by his strategic incompetence, or using the incompetence as a flattering self-description? The joke can expose a limitation and advertise a virtue simultaneously. That possibility qualifies the claim that comic self-application is especially undefended.

In §2, “the writer marks the proportion” overstates what the scare-quote convention supplies. The passage marks mixed commitment without quantifying it. The reader still must infer which part is sincere and how far the skepticism extends. This is useful accounting, but it does not eliminate interpretive work.

The satire paragraph is both unfair to Davis and too forgiving of him:

> “That is a fair assessment of satire’s limits as argument.”

His reported claim that hostile readers “will always be able to invent an excuse” treats objections to an analogy as evasions before evaluating them. The chapter’s next sentence correctly allows that differences between cases can matter. Those positions should not receive the same endorsement.

Conversely:

> “the reader who already rejects the original position is the only one it convinces.”

This categorical claim has no evidence here. A reader may accept the target position but change their mind when a transposition isolates an inconsistency. A committed opponent may also remain unconvinced because the analogy is poor. The relevant issue is whether the transposition preserves the contested relationship, not whether satire can persuade only allies.

For “If the Gay Community Were Like the Trans Community,” reconstruct one inference precisely. Which relation among attraction, identity, and classification is transferred? What distinction would a defender of gender identity consider relevant? The chapter need not settle the underlying philosophy again, but it must show what the literary substitution proves and what it assumes.

**Proposed replacement for §2’s final three sentences**

*Problem addressed: endorsement of the dismissal of critics, followed by an unsupported dismissal of satire.*

> Davis identifies a problem of repetition: readers familiar with the device may learn little from another example. His description of hostile readers is less satisfactory. An objection to a transposition can identify a relevant difference between the cases. The force of this satire therefore depends on whether it preserves the relation under dispute. Its comic effect may make that relation easier to see, but cannot establish that the transfer is sound.

## 3. Allegory and fiction: scrutinize the premises built into the world

“Grim Trigger” supplies the best opportunity for a sustained close reading. At present, §3 offers a plot summary followed by conclusions that the summary does not settle.

The tribal names are said to be transparent to initiated readers, “which is the sense in which the allegory is deniable.” Transparency and deniability need disentangling. A recognizable target can coexist with the absence of explicit identification, but that is different from a plausible alternative reading. Specify who could deny what, and to whom. The tag itself may make the refusal to identify the referent a joke rather than an effective concealment.

More seriously, the fictional world appears to settle the contested issue through genealogical facts: the honest man investigates and discovers the truth. On the chapter’s account, opponents then offer kindness and social pressure rather than competing evidence. That construction grants the protagonist both factual authority and moral isolation. A critic could reasonably object that the story secures its conclusion through the distribution of knowledge among characters.

Davis’s strongest answer is that a fable can investigate what follows *if* the epistemic question has already been settled. The chapter should distinguish that conditional achievement from evidence that the corresponding real-world question is settled. This is where form contributes something philosophically specific.

> “The parable does not endorse that ending and does not criticize it either.”

This is too categorical without examining the narrator, biblical cadence, escalating repetition, and detonator. Those features could make the ending heroic, grotesque, tragic, or deliberately unstable. The absence of an explicit moral does not establish neutrality. Nor does a grim-trigger strategy by itself establish the study’s part (c): retaliating after defection and requiring exceptionless truthfulness are distinguishable commitments. Show how the story joins them.

The reading of “Blegg Mode” should likewise distinguish concealing an application from concealing a filter. An abstract argument can be assessed independently of its author’s political purpose. Davis’s strongest defense is that withholding the application prevents prejudice from substituting for evaluation. The critics’ strongest reply is that the application may determine whether the chosen similarities are relevant and whether the example has been constructed tendentiously. The chapter currently gives Davis’s reason, then treats the book’s disclosure principle as sufficient to defeat it.

The discussion of *Johnny the Walrus* accepts another disputed premise:

> “The review is a craft argument and a good one.”

Davis’s contrast between satire and allegory should be presented as his working distinction. The two need not be mutually exclusive. More importantly, removing explicit political markers could improve a story’s independent interest while making its ideological lesson less available to scrutiny. A parent’s willingness to read it aloud is not a sufficient aesthetic criterion. Test whether the story’s characters and action remain interesting without recognition of the target, rather than equating subtlety with success.

The chapter is also too gentle with “Blame Me for Trying.” Its sympathy for the spambot is intelligible, but the reported story makes the therapist profit from deterioration. That is a strong causal and moral allocation, not simply an unnamed man’s predicament. What alternative explanations does the fiction exclude? How does the final sentence make “being in touch with reality” compatible with exploitation? Analyze that irony without guessing the identity of a real-world target.

Finally, “the self-deceiver is usually a version of the author” is unsupported by the small set offered. “Blindspot” provides a plausible parallel; a technical occupation does not establish one. Synthia, the honest man, and the closet speaker are not all shown here to be self-deceivers. Distinguish autobiographical resemblance, argumentative surrogate, and self-criticism.

The further claim that fiction treats self-reports “as evidence to be weighed” confuses an invented test case with testimonial evidence. Fiction can model self-correction without documenting that its author has undergone it.

## 4. Verse and memoir need literary analysis

Section 5 says that verse concerns “the thing he could not yet say in prose.” The chapter establishes that he had not said it plainly, not that he could not. Privacy, uncertainty, pleasure in indirection, and aesthetic preference remain possible explanations.

The assertion that a 2015 reader “could not” see what the closet held is also too strong. The date, closet, and refusal to emerge evidently provide clues, even if the later corpus supplies a more determinate interpretation. Avoid retrospective certainty: the poem may preserve possibilities that subsequent testimony does not exhaust.

Most importantly, the discussion does little with verse as verse. Quote a short complete unit and examine its syntax, sound, line endings, and movement toward the closing refusal. Explain what the dragon contributes beyond serving as a concealed referent. The “private calendar” claim currently rests on one public anniversary; either add a second example or narrow it.

> “The reading is a misreading, and it says so.”

The quoted Patmore comment announces an assumption about irony; it does not explicitly call itself a misreading. The chapter also supplies insufficient evidence that the poem’s contentment must be sincere. Distinguish a playful appropriation from an incorrect interpretation, and establish the latter before asserting it.

Section 6’s memoir assessment is almost entirely verdict:

> “the memoir meets the standard in its portraits and its timing and fails it in its length.”

Neither praise nor criticism is demonstrated. Choose one passage whose sequence produces an effective revelation and one recurring episode whose repetition adds little. Length becomes an aesthetic fault through its effect on the reading, not through the word count alone. Repetition might also reproduce fixation or grief; the question is whether that effect earns its cost.

The Moore epigraph and captions arrive as a detached closing gesture. Explain how the disavowal of erotica frames the material that follows, or relocate it. The chapter should assess the relation between a declared serious purpose and the pleasures or embarrassments that declaration regulates.

## 5. Scholarship that would sharpen the analysis

These are research leads, not authorities already checked. Each description below is from memory.

- **Philosophical dialogue.** [memory] Vittorio Hösle’s *The Philosophical Dialogue: A Poetics and a Hermeneutics* treats dialogue as a literary and philosophical form. It would help distinguish the allocation of arguments among speakers from a search for the author’s mouthpiece. Apply this to the unsupported claim that the AI dialogue’s balance depends on readers not knowing which character represents Davis.

- **Dialogue and independent voices.** [memory] Mikhail Bakhtin’s *Problems of Dostoevsky’s Poetics* develops an account of characters whose voices are not simply subordinated to an authorial verdict. Used cautiously, this offers a question rather than an honorific: do Davis’s interlocutors retain reasons and concerns the winning argument cannot absorb?

- **Satire and irony.** [memory] Dustin Griffin’s *Satire: A Critical Reintroduction* challenges accounts of satire as merely enforcing settled moral judgments. [memory] Linda Hutcheon’s *Irony’s Edge* emphasizes the interpretive communities and social relations through which irony operates. These would sharpen the distinction among shared recognition, plausible deniability, and unequal access to the joke.

- **Allegory.** [memory] Angus Fletcher’s *Allegory: The Theory of a Symbolic Mode* offers an account broader than a story with concealed contemporary referents. It could help assess the conspicuous names, restricted character roles, and repeated actions in “Grim Trigger,” rather than treating successful concealment as the defining literary achievement.

- **Humor.** [memory] John Morreall’s *Comic Relief* examines humor in relation to cognitive shifts and disengagement from immediate practical demands. It offers an alternative to the chapter’s two-decoding model: humor may allow a proposition to be considered provisionally without supplying an innocent meaning to retreat into. Test both possibilities against “Interlude II.”

- **Didactic fable.** [memory] G. E. Lessing’s essays on fable, commonly collected as *Abhandlungen über die Fabel*, concern the relation between concrete narrative and general moral instruction. This is directly relevant to whether “Funk-tunul’s Legacy” illustrates stipulated assumptions or invites readers to accept those assumptions about social life.

Two or three of these, used on particular passages, would suffice. A literature survey would consume space better spent on the texts.

## 6. Structure, overlap, and prose

The chapter is below its planned length but spreads its attention across too many examples. Combine §§1 and 4 around dialogue’s allocation of commitment. Follow with humor and satirical transposition, then a sustained reading of “Grim Trigger” alongside “Blegg Mode.” Give verse and memoir enough room to demonstrate their distinctive effects.

Several handoffs currently leave material unexamined. Chapter 10’s summary sends “Two Political Short Stories” here, while this chapter’s summary sends it there; the chapter itself omits it. Chapter 11 sends the fictional form of *Feature Selection* here, but this chapter gives no reading. The plan assigns captions as a genre to Chapter 8, while §6 redirects them to Chapter 2, whose summary sends captions here. Resolve these assignments explicitly.

The account of the memoir’s reputational ethics belongs in Chapter 7; retain here only what bears on narrative selection, pacing, quotation, and characterization. Likewise, Chapter 11 should assess the AI arguments, while this chapter examines turn-taking, names, concessions, and endings. Cross-reference shared quotations rather than repeating them without a new formal analysis.

The opening “Much of the corpus is not argument in prose” is misleading: most examples are prose and many are arguments. “Davis frequently stages arguments in fictional scenes” would identify the distinction.

Other phrases replace analysis with certification: “what matters is the portrait,” “stated exactly,” “a craft argument and a good one,” “the best model,” and “the least defensive writing in the corpus.” The last is particularly unwarranted after describing forms that reduce reputational exposure. Self-deprecation may be candid, but it can also let a writer choose the charge against himself.

The conclusion largely repeats the opening and §4. A narrower ending would be stronger.

**Proposed replacement for §7**

*Problem addressed: repetitive summary and excessive confidence in later disclosure.*

> The literary devices redistribute commitment. A character can voice an objection that Davis has not accepted, while an allegory can make a disputed premise appear as a fact of its fictional world. These possibilities require different standards of assessment. Davis’s later acknowledgment of Simplicia’s views helps readers locate his commitment in one dialogue. It does not settle whether the other speaker received a fair hearing. The achievement of the form depends on what each voice is allowed to reveal, including what remains unanswered when the scene ends.
