# Review: Ch. 4, "Where to Draw the Boundaries: The Philosophy of Categorization"

Reviewer: Fable 5.1 (fresh subagent), 2026-10-17. Read: `BRIEF.md`, `notes/plan.md`, `notes/chapter-summaries.md`, `study/04-categorization.html`. Not read: the corpus, reception data, research files, pilots. Everything I say about what a post or comment contains is from the chapter's own presentation or from memory and is unverified; the "Checks" list at the end collects the ones that matter.

Overall: the chapter is well organized, reads the texts closely, and its reconstruction of the distinction (§§3–5, §7) is the best part. The assessment (§8) is where it is weakest, for one main reason: the "law" it refutes is never located in a text with the precision the reconstruction brings to everything else, and the refutation of the law rests on an assertion ("a norm has exceptions") that the plan's own thesis says needs an argument. Details follow, grouped as the brief asks.

## 1. Weak or overstated arguments

### 1.1 Where does the "law" live? (§1, §6, §8, §10)

The chapter's thesis sentence (§1) says Davis "attached a stronger claim": "any departure from predictively optimal categories is a kind of deception, and an appeal to consequences is never a valid argument about where a boundary goes." §10 restates it: "That ambition was to show that departures from predictively optimal categories are not merely costly but illegitimate, a kind of lying."

But the chapter's own §6 shows the capstone essay saying the opposite: "He does not say deception is always wrong: 'Depending on your goals, deception can be rational!' The claim is that you should know when you are doing it." And it shows the essay ending with "I can't prove that that's not the right the answer." So the 2021 essay, presented as the summit of the program, already holds position (b). The chapter then describes Davis's "most careful writing" as "retreating" from a law that, on its own showing, the most careful writing never asserted.

The law does appear in the corpus, and the chapter quotes it, but in two other places: the 2019 "Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist" ("Appeals to consequences are invalid because…"; "we're not allowed to care about which descriptions sound nice") and the 2018 polemical register ("universal socially-mandatory lies," which the chapter buries in a parenthesis in §8). The honest shape of the story is therefore not peak-then-retreat but: a norm-with-teeth stated as a law in 2018–19, in the polemics and in the appeals-to-consequences post; a mathematical program that, when finished in 2021, supports only the norm; and a title ("Optimized for Deception") that carries the 2019 rhetoric onto the 2021 result. The chapter should say this directly in §1 and §8, name the texts in which the law is stated as a law, and stop describing the 2021 essay as both the summit and the retreat. The plan (§1, thesis 2, last sentence) anticipates exactly this: "If, on close reading, a chapter finds Davis's practice there is not driven by (c), the chapter says so." Partly it is not, and the chapter should say so rather than preserve the arc.

A related overstatement: "Davis's most careful writing retreats from it" uses a 2021 private email reported in a 2023 memoir, a draft outline ("The View From Nowhere"), and a comment reply (Martínez) as the retreat. Those are the right texts, but the chapter says "the assessment in §8 turns on that sentence" of the email. An assessment of a public philosophical program should not turn on one sentence of reported private correspondence. It can be evidence of what Davis came to think; the argument that the law fails has to be made on the merits, and the chapter has the materials to make it (next point).

### 1.2 "A norm has exceptions" is asserted, not argued (§8, "Appeals to consequences," second paragraph)

"The norm may still be a good one. Demski's version is strong… But a norm has exceptions, and arguing about where they fall is arguing about consequences."

This is the hinge of thesis 2 and it is one sentence of assertion. The position Davis actually holds (plan §1, part (c)) is that the norm must be held *without* exception because the alternative has no stable intermediate point. That is a standard and respectable two-level consequentialist structure: a rule justified by consequences can still be one that agents should treat as exceptionless, because case-by-case weighing is exactly what the rule exists to prevent (Hare's two-level theory; Hooker's rule-consequentialism; from memory). Demski's "Schelling fence" defense is this structure. So "it is a norm adopted for its consequences, and its scope is a question about consequences" does not refute Davis; a rule-consequentialist agrees with the first clause and denies that the second clause licenses case-by-case exceptions.

The plan supplies the argument the chapter needs and the chapter never makes it. Plan §1, thesis 2: "a disclosed, costed departure is not a defection, and disclosure is exactly the 'salient intermediate point' whose absence the slippery-slope argument assumes." That is the move: the slippery-slope form of (c) says there is no stable point between x = 0 and x = ∞; the norm of accounting *is* a stable point, because "I am using the word this way, for this reason, at this cost" is publicly checkable in a way that silent gerrymandering is not. The chapter gestures at this in §8 ("the people making it owe the public an honest statement of the trade") and §10 ("say so"), but never states it as the answer to the exceptionlessness argument. Without it, thesis 2 is not established in this chapter.

**Proposed insertion for §8, replacing the paragraph beginning "The norm may still be a good one."**

> The norm may still be a good one, and Demski's defense of it is strong: a community that lets itself depart from accuracy for the sake of consequences loses the instrument it would need to tell whether the departure helped. Davis's own version is stronger still. His coordination argument holds that between "always describe accurately" and "describe however is advantageous" there is no stable resting place; any compromise line will be pushed. [QUOTE NEEDED: the "only Schelling points are x = ∞ and x = 0" sentence from "Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point," or its equivalent in "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point."] If that were right, the norm would have to be exceptionless, and arguing about exceptions would be the first step down the slope.
>
> It is not right, and the reason is supplied by Davis's other commitment. A departure that is disclosed is not the same act as one that is concealed. "I am using *fish* to include whales for the purpose of this fishing regulation, and I know they are mammals" costs the listener nothing he cannot recover, because the departure is stated and the reason is checkable. The gerrymandered category of "Unnatural Categories" is deceptive precisely because it is not announced: the engineer does not say that his labels are optimized for supplier revenue. Disclosure is the salient intermediate point the slippery-slope argument says does not exist. That is why the demand that recurs throughout Davis's work is not "never depart" but "say that you are departing, and why": the September 2020 email, the Martínez comment, the meat-substitute passage, the pronoun essay's "I'm not claiming that Yudkowsky should have a different pronoun usage policy." Each is a case in which Davis accepts a departure once it is costed. The norm of accounting is therefore not a softened version of the law; it is the thing that makes the law unnecessary.

(Main agent: the quotation from "Speaking Truth to Power" is in plan §1 and presumably verified; I have not read the post.)

### 1.3 "Contradicts the two-kinds distinction" (§8, "Appeals to consequences," first paragraph)

"As a rule for definitions it is ambiguous, and on one reading contradicts the two-kinds distinction, which grants that values choose the space."

Davis would answer, with the chapter's own §4 quotation, that choosing the space is not an "appeal to consequences" in his sense at all: an appeal to consequences is an argument from the effects of *holding or uttering* the description other than through its predictive accuracy ("a utility function defined over features of the model itself"), whereas choosing which features to predict is "a utility function defined over a statistical model's performance against specific real-world data." The distinction exists to separate these two. So the rule does not contradict the distinction; it depends on it. The real weakness is the one the chapter already identified in §4: the distinction is clean in the abstract and Davis admitted he had no "rigorous reduction" of context-relevance. The paragraph should be rewritten around that admission (the rule is only as sharp as the distinction, and in application the distinction is not sharp) and drop "contradicts."

### 1.4 "The lying equivalence holds only relative to an entrenched convention" (§8)

This is presented as an objection but it is Davis's thesis. The piggyback diagnosis, quoted two subsections earlier, says the point of redefining an *existing* word is to borrow the meaning it already has; the Lewis–Skyrms account the chapter credits him with makes meaning conventional by construction. "Not the timeless claim of the title" misreads the title: "optimized for deception" is a claim about what a redefinition is *for* at the moment it is pushed, not about the word's meaning for all time. Davis's reply to tslarm (quoted in §6) concedes the convention-relativity without strain.

What remains a real objection is the transitional case, and the chapter reaches it only via Wei Dai: when a community *openly* advocates a new usage ("trans women are women" is a slogan, not a disguised report), is using the new sense deceptive? Davis's framework says no, if the advocacy is on the merits; the question is then whether the merits may include the effect on the people classified, which is the Khalidi/interactive-kinds point. The subsection should be cut to that and the entrenched-convention paragraph dropped or reduced to a sentence. The pronoun point (non-propositional signals) is good and should stay.

### 1.5 The lesbians *reductio* (§8, paragraph beginning "Ozy's *reductio*")

"The causal account supplies the missing answer: lesbians differ from other women in some dimensions, through a cause that leaves the sex-rooted structure in place, while on the two-type hypothesis most trans women differ across that whole structure. That answer is only as strong as the hypothesis."

I think this is wrong, or at least misdirected. The causal answer to Ozy is: lesbians are developmentally female, so the sex-rooted conditional-independence structure is intact; trans women are developmentally male, so it is not. That answer requires only that transition does not change developmental sex, which nobody disputes. The two-type taxonomy is relevant to a different question: whether trans women resemble women in the *psychological* subspace (the "female brain" hypothesis vs. the AGP/HSTS account). If the chapter means that the psychological subspace is where the dispute over the word actually lives, so that the two-type hypothesis decides whether trans women cluster with women *there*, it should say that; as written, "differ across that whole structure" conflates the developmental root with the psychological branches. Either way, "only as strong as the hypothesis" should be re-argued or withdrawn. (Check against "I Mean, Yes, I Agree…" and the 2018 essay's "part of male cluster along most dimensions.")

### 1.6 "The data fix the clusters" is stated too strongly (§1, §8 "What holds")

"In its general form, the thesis that values pick the space and the metric while the data fix the clusters is standard in statistics and machine learning." Partly. Given a space and metric, clustering is still underdetermined by algorithm choice, number of clusters, and linkage; Kleinberg's impossibility theorem for clustering (2002; from memory: no clustering function satisfies scale-invariance, richness, and consistency together) is the standard citation for the point that there is no uniquely correct clustering even given a metric. This does not rescue Alexander, but it means "fixed by the distribution" should become "constrained by the distribution," and the chapter should note that model selection (§5's MDL criterion) is Davis's own answer to the residual freedom. gjm's "multiple roughly-equally-good categorizations" is this point in plain words, and the chapter treats it as settled by Davis's "shocking coincidence" reply, which it is not.

### 1.7 Is Alexander's move an equivocation, or a claim about what kind of category *woman* is? (§2, §8)

The chapter calls the §I–III → §IV move "a real equivocation" and "What holds." But §8's own interactive-kinds subsection concedes that "where we have control, a proposed category is partly a proposal about what to make true, and arguing for it is legitimately arguing about consequences." If that is right, then Alexander's §IV is an equivocation *only if* "woman" is a purely predictive category, and Alexander's position is that it is not (the title says the categories were made for man). What Alexander failed to do is argue that; he asserted it with a border metaphor. The chapter should state the charge in that form: not "Alexander equivocated between two kinds of value-dependence" but "Alexander's conclusion needs a premise about what kind of category *woman* is, which he did not supply, and which Davis correctly demanded." That is both more accurate and more consistent with §8.

## 2. Fairness to Davis

### 2.1 Too soft

- **The stealth reply (§9).** "The rationale is defensible." The chapter does not draw the obvious connection: concealing the political context of "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" is a failure to disclose one's filter, which is the norm of accounting (part (b)) applied to one's own writing. Davis himself half-says so ("unfair of me"). The chapter owes a sentence saying that the stealth reply violates the norm the chapter credits him with, and that he acknowledged it. As written §9 reads as exculpation.
- **The polemical register.** The chapter repeatedly says "the polemics forget" the concessions, but quotes the polemics only in one parenthesis ("universal socially-mandatory lies"). If the claim is that the published rhetoric overshoots the published argument, show two or three instances with dates. Otherwise the reader has to take it on trust.
- **Habryka's endorsement (§8).** "found 'basically everything in this post' obviously true" is used as support. Check whether the comment also said the post was therefore uninteresting or beside the point; if so, quoting only the first half is the selective reporting the chapter elsewhere criticizes.

### 2.2 Too hard, or less charitable than the text supports

- **Perceived sex as a "natural category" (§8, "Two natural categories").** Within Davis's own framework, a category is a latent node; perceived sex is the *output of a classifier over observed features*, i.e., evidence about the latent, not a second latent. Jessica Taylor's comment (quoted in §3: observations are downstream of ontological reality) is exactly this point and the chapter does not bring it back here. The chapter does give Davis the "proxy" reply but calls it his "best reply" as if it were a rhetorical option; structurally it is what his framework says. The chapter's counter (for ordinary social life the proxy does the work) is still a good one, but it should be made against the framework correctly stated. Also: the chapter slides between "perceived sex" and "social gender" (the 2018 concession is about the latter); they are not the same category, and the slide matters because social gender *is* an interactive kind while perceived sex is not.
- **§9, motive.** "Davis was quick to diagnose motivated dishonesty… and the unpublished record shows it more plainly than the published one." The evidence offered is a *draft he did not publish*, and the chapter itself says the published reply "is ironic rather than accusatory." That he wrote the accusation and withheld it is as much evidence of restraint as of quickness; the chapter should weigh both readings. Also "not held with the uncertainty that Davis's own theory of evidence would assign them" is a conclusion about a pattern, supported here by one draft; either add instances or soften.
- **§7, "The reception of the dolphin posts did Davis's suspicions few favors."** This implies Davis had published a suspicion about Soares's motives. If the published reply did not impute motive (as §9 says), the sentence is unfair; if it did, quote it.

### 2.3 Concession acknowledged and then dropped

§4 ends: deciding which dimensions are context-relevant "belongs to the first kind of value-dependence, the legitimate kind." That is a large concession to Davis (the substantive gender disagreement is, on this reading, a legitimate first-kind dispute about relevance). §8 then treats the same disagreement as a "metalinguistic negotiation" whose resolution lies with "social science and ethics." These are compatible but the chapter never connects them: say explicitly that the negotiation is over which features the word must serve, that this is a first-kind choice, and that Davis's framework therefore classifies the gender dispute as legitimate value-dependence, not as the second kind. That is a stronger and more interesting conclusion than the chapter draws, and it is favorable to Davis's framework while unfavorable to his polemic.

## 3. Missing objections

From Davis's side: §1.2 (exceptionless rules), §1.3 (appeals-to-consequences vs. feature choice), §2.2 (perceived sex as evidence).

From the critics:

- **Goal-derived and ad hoc categories.** The plan names Rosch and Barsalou; the chapter has neither. Barsalou's "ad hoc categories" (1983; "things to take out of a burning house") are categories humans form and use fluently that have no cluster structure in any fixed subspace and are defined by a goal. That is an empirical challenge to the naive-Bayes picture of human concepts, and to the claim that a category must "pay rent in better predictions." Murphy and Medin, "The Role of Theories in Conceptual Coherence" (1985), argues that similarity cannot ground category coherence without a background theory, which is the cognitive-science version of Davis's own causal turn and could be credited to him. Lakoff's radial categories (1987) would be the critics' version. All from memory.
- **Biological pluralism.** The dolphin argument is made as if phylogeny were the one legitimate biological classification. Philosophy of biology has a long literature holding the opposite: Dupré's "promiscuous realism" (1981, "Natural Kinds and Biological Taxa," which uses the whale/fish case; from memory) and Ereshefsky's species pluralism argue that multiple cross-cutting classifications (phylogenetic, ecological, morphological) are all real. Vaniver's "bad phylogenetics" and aphyer's "fruit" comments are this literature in amateur form. The chapter should note that Davis's "thicker subspace" argument is a reason to *prefer* phylogeny for a wide range of inferences, not a refutation of pluralism, and that Davis conceded as much to Yvain.
- **Social ontology.** The money analogy is discussed without Searle's status functions ("X counts as Y in context C," *The Construction of Social Reality*, 1995) or Ásta's conferralism (*Categories We Live By*, 2018), both of which model gender on exactly the money pattern Ozy proposed. Dembroff ("Real Talk on the Metaphysics of Gender," 2018; "Escaping the Natural Attitude about Gender," 2021) and Jenkins ("Amelioration and Inclusion," 2016) are the academic developments of Ozy's objection and of Haslanger's reply to it. The plan lists Dembroff; the chapter omits her.
- **Byrne.** The plan lists Byrne's polysemy puzzle; the chapter omits Byrne entirely. Alex Byrne, "Are Women Adult Human Females?" (*Philosophical Studies*, 2020), is the nearest academic statement of Davis's position and defends it against the same objections; Dembroff's reply and Byrne's rejoinder exist (from memory). Given that the chapter claims Davis "reached his positions independently" and that "the academic literature had already worked through objections he first met in comment threads," it is odd to omit the one academic who worked through them on his side. Bogardus, "Evaluating Arguments for the Sex/Gender Distinction" (*Philosophia*, 2020), is in the same neighborhood.
- **Conceptual engineering proper.** The chapter uses Plunkett & Sundell well. Burgess & Plunkett (2013) and Cappelen's *Fixing Language* (2018), both in the plan, would let the chapter say what the field thinks about the legitimacy of revising a term for non-predictive ends, which is the exact question at issue. Chalmers's "Verbal Disputes" (2011) is the academic form of the 2012 "Contemporary" move (eliminate the term and see whether disagreement remains), and would make §1's frame sharper. Ludlow's *Living Words* (2014) on "meaning litigation" is the closest thing to a theory of the Category War as a linguistic event.
- **Clustering underdetermination.** Kleinberg 2002, as in §1.6.

The chapter cannot carry all of these at 9,900 words. The minimum, in my judgment: Byrne (one paragraph, in "The academic neighbors"); Barsalou (two sentences, as the empirical challenge the plan asks for); Dupré (one sentence in §7); Searle or Ásta (one sentence in the money subsection). Everything is from memory and must be fetched before use.

## 5. Structure, clarity, prose

- **§1 vs. §10 on the 2012/2019 frame.** §1 says the 2019 sentence points "the other way" from the 2012 one; §10 says "The two positions are one." Both are defensible but the reader is told one thing at the start and the other at the end without being warned. Add "apparently" in §1, or make §1 announce that the chapter will show them to be one.
- **§4, "Its plural answers Yudkowsky's singular."** Yudkowsky's title "Where to Draw the Boundary?" was given only as an attribution in §2's data-src and in the works cited; the running text never names it. Name it in §2 so this sentence lands.
- **§6, TAG paragraph.** "(b) and (d) were coordination and self-fulfilling prophecy" arrives after the labels have been used. Introduce Davis's four-way list with the letters the first time, or paraphrase TAG without the letters.
- **§6, Demski paragraph.** The chronology is tangled: a Nov 2019 email, a Dec 2020 LW comment, a Jan 2021 email reply, Feb 2021 LW comment. Split into two paragraphs, one for the public thread and one for the correspondence, with dates in order.
- **§7, cata.** "The remark goes deep, and §8 returns to it" promises more than §8 delivers (one clause). Either expand in §8 (it is a good point: the "common-sense cluster" strategy that makes dolphins fish also makes passing trans women women, so Davis's dolphin stance and his gender stance pull in opposite directions on the perceived/underlying question) or cut the promise.
- **§8, "What holds."** The italic run-in headings work; the "mathematics" item refers to the method note for the third time. Once is enough.
- **§8 → §10 repetition.** The four-clause statement of the norm ("Give the reason… Acknowledge what is lost… Do not present a choice as a discovery") appears in §8 (paragraph beginning "The norm may still be a good one") and again in §10. Keep it once, in §10.
- **Compression casualties.** "Partly a jibe at the course, it is also a fair statement of what Eliezer Yudkowsky had taught" (§1) is fine. "That caption is the whole later dispute in miniature (§8)" is fine. But "The sequence thus admitted that some boundaries are legitimately fixed by what we care about, an admission Davis's 2021 essay would have to narrow" (§2) needs one more sentence saying *how* the 2021 essay narrowed it (the "lawful dependence… not an atomic preference" quotation in §6 is the answer; cross-reference it). And "Davis reached his positions independently, which suggests they are where the reasoning leads" (§7) is a non sequitur as compressed; independent arrival suggests the reasoning is natural, not that it is correct.
- **The lede** is good. "Soundly formalized" could be "correctly calculated," since the chapter's own §1.6 point is that the formalization leaves residual freedom.

### 1. Weak or overstated arguments
- **1.1 Where the "law" lives — adopted.** Confirmed: the 2021 essay already holds (b) ("Depending on your goals, deception can be rational!"; "it helps to notice that what you're doing is deceiving people"; "maybe that's the right thing to do" on meat substitutes; and "notice the difference" appears in the essay itself, not only in the Martínez comment). - **1.2 "A norm has exceptions" asserted — adopted, argued differently from the proposed insertion.** The reviewer's insertion was not used verbatim.
