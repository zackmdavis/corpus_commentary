# Whole-book review: *The Scintillating and the True*

The study’s strongest result is a qualified reconstruction: Davis develops valuable accounts of classification and misleading communication, makes unusually extensive evidence about himself available, and sometimes applies those accounts more confidently than his evidence permits. The book establishes that result through close comparison of essays, comments, drafts and retrospective accounts. Its larger claim—that a coordination argument explains the characteristic failures, while disclosure supplies the remedy—is less secure. The chapters repeatedly distinguish belief, speech, action and institutional policy; the Introduction and Conclusion repeatedly collapse those distinctions when presenting the thesis.

The revision should begin there. It should also reconsider the supposedly established contradiction in Yudkowsky’s 2018 thread, narrow several empirical conclusions to the research actually examined, and apply the book’s conditions on adequate disclosure more evenly. These changes would permit substantial cuts: much of the repetition consists of restating a general thesis and then restoring qualifications that prevent it from fitting the case.

## 1. The coordination thesis exceeds the argument the chapters establish

### The book sometimes refutes a position it has already distinguished from Davis’s strongest position

The Introduction §3 defines the third component as:

> “The third part is a set of arguments that accuracy may not be traded against other goods: that a description may not be shaded, or a truth withheld, for the sake of its consequences, however openly the trade is made.”

But the same section acknowledges that Davis permits consequentialist decisions about speech and describes his strongest position as allowing silence and bargaining over conduct while refusing to alter standards of evidence. Chapter 4 §6 supplies unusually important evidence: even “Maybe Lying Doesn’t Exist,” the principal source of the role rule, distinguishes the truth of a description from the incentive to utter it. “Unnatural Categories” explicitly permits consequentialist verbal behavior.

This makes the following sentence in Introduction §3 untenable without further argument:

> “Neither of the last two is a departure from accuracy; both weigh consequences in the way the role argument forbids.”

“The last two” are the Murray review’s choice between social equilibria and the college retrospective’s treatment of instructions. Neither plainly violates a rule governing which considerations bear on whether a description is accurate. The former concerns a social policy; the latter concerns compliance with instructions. If Davis distinguishes epistemic assessment from practical choice, these are applications of the distinction, not counterexamples to his rule.

Chapter 9 §8 recognizes precisely this problem:

> “The change resembles the study’s thesis without illustrating it.”

The Conclusion nevertheless restores the stronger interpretation in §2:

> “it does show the course the coordination argument calls unstable being taken, at small scale and in an institution he could leave, without the collapse the argument predicts.”

No argument quoted in the book predicts that a student who disregards some instructions will undergo such a collapse. A warning about collective standards of public evidence does not automatically apply to individual judgments about university requirements.

The three forms also require separate verdicts. A slippery-slope argument concerns stability; an anti-extortion argument concerns incentives; a role rule concerns permissible reasons within an activity. Their conclusions can overlap, but disclosure does not answer all three in the same way. Publicly announcing a concession might preserve information while strengthening the incentive to demand another concession. A role restriction might govern belief formation while permitting extensive practical accommodation.

The book should retain “coordination argument” only for the slippery-slope argument, or explain explicitly that the umbrella groups different arguments for restriction rather than three forms of one inference. More importantly, it should identify each application’s object: belief, assertion, terminology, treatment, or participation.

### Disclosure does not establish the stable intermediate point the thesis needs

Chapter 4 §8 makes the central inference:

> “A disclosed departure hides nothing at the moment of disclosure.”

That is true only under a much stronger description of disclosure than the book usually requires. Announcing that evidence has been withheld does not disclose the evidence. Announcing a change of terminology does not restore the information the old terminology carried. Chapter 5 §3 explains both limitations well.

The next inference is stronger still:

> “The rule it supports is a rule against concealment, and it is easier to enforce than Davis’s.”

What has been established is that some disclosed conventions preserve relevant distinctions. That shows the possibility of an alternative, not its stability or comparative enforceability. The text recognizes that qualifications must reach listeners, travel with words and remain usable. Once these conditions are imposed, observing that a speaker stated a reason is no longer an adequate enforcement test.

The Conclusion §2 repeats the overclaim:

> “The study’s finding is that disclosure supplies the intermediate point whose absence the argument assumes; whether a given intermediate practice is stable and can be enforced is a further question, answered case by case.”

Stability is part of what makes an intermediate point relevant to a Schelling-point argument. It cannot be postponed while the argument is pronounced defeated. The strongest result available is narrower: Davis has not established that no stable intermediate practice exists, and his own writing identifies candidates. The book has likewise not established that its preferred candidate will hold.

This revision need not weaken the criticism much. Davis’s exclusivity claim bears a burden of proof; the book can show that the burden remains unmet. It need not prove a competing universal rule.

The same caution belongs in Chapter 6 §4:

> “The anti-extortion argument condemns concessions that are concealed or that distort the shared map; by itself it gives no reason to refuse accommodations that are stated as such and leave the content intact, unless the incentive premise holds.”

The final clause contains the argument’s central premise, not a peripheral qualification. The chapter should ask whether accommodating offense reinforces strategically induced offense, how strongly, and at what cost. Disclosure alone does not answer that question.

### The Introduction’s four claims are delivered unevenly

The first claim—qualified endorsement of the criterion and accounting norm—is substantially delivered. The third—separating overconfident attribution from restrictions on accommodation—is also productive, although the separation becomes less clear in Chapter 7. The fourth—self-documentation as the strongest reason for an author-centered study—is well supported as an interpretive judgment.

The second claim supplies too much of the book’s architecture for the evidence supporting it. The most convincing instances are particular: the Salamon exchange, the extension from content suppression to tact, and the memoir’s “chosen Feelings” passage. These support a finding that Davis sometimes treats an acknowledged cost as disqualifying evidence against someone’s commitment to truth. They do not establish an exceptionless position governing the corpus.

There is also a drift in the first claim. Introduction §4 defines the reception finding narrowly:

> “none reconstructs the work as a structured position”

The structure is then defined as this study’s three-part reconstruction. That is a defensible novelty claim, but weak evidence that the work is “undervalued.” Readers may have assessed its arguments without adopting this organization. Retain “not previously reconstructed in this form”; reserve claims of undervaluation for contributions whose reception demonstrably missed their force.

## 2. The central verdict on Yudkowsky needs reopening

Chapter 7 §2 states:

> “The second moment carries the weight, and here the memoir is right.”

Its decisive evidence is Yudkowsky’s statement that someone is not defending truth by insisting that an explicitly disputed word retain a particular meaning. The chapter takes this as contradicting the Sequences’ claim that definitions can be wrong.

Those propositions are compatible. Multiple definitions can be constrained by facts without one particular meaning being mandatory. Chapter 4’s own conclusion distinguishes legitimate metalinguistic negotiation from arbitrary classification. Yudkowsky’s clause might deny that insisting on a particular convention constitutes a defense of truth, while leaving open factual criticism of the convention’s consequences.

The study addresses this defense:

> “On that account the negotiation has factual stakes, since it decides which inferences the word will serve, and the clause denies that insisting on a meaning is a stand for truth at all.”

But having factual stakes does not make insisting on one particular resolution equivalent to defending a factual proposition. The argument slides between those claims. The sports remark can support a complaint about dismissiveness or neglected empirical stakes; it does not resolve the logical issue.

The packet also reports that Yudkowsky asked for a non-confused explanation of the scientific stakes and acknowledged Davis’s essay as one. Chapter 7 counts this chiefly as a quiet concession. It also bears directly on what the disputed clause meant in its original context. The later clarification could repair an overbroad formulation without retracting a settled contrary doctrine.

The appropriate provisional verdict is that the thread permitted a misleadingly broad reading and did not adequately distinguish conventions from their factual costs. Establishing a contradiction requires a more exact argument than the book currently gives. Because the Introduction, Chapter 4, Chapter 7 and Conclusion all rely on this verdict, this is a whole-book correction.

The role-relative charge also shifts standards. Chapter 7’s opening calls it:

> “a claim that the departure was culpable because of the role Yudkowsky claimed”

Yet §2 carefully records Davis’s separation of “fraud” from moral evaluation. “Culpable” prejudges the distinction the chapter is supposed to preserve.

Later, the chapter assesses the charge by Yudkowsky’s Gould criterion: self-serving error, repeated correction, and failure to inform others. That is relevant as an immanent comparison, but it is not identical to false advertising about an epistemic role. Nor is either identical to Chapter 5’s resource-transferring “algorithmic fraud.” The chapter should distinguish these uses before evaluating them. Otherwise a condition belonging to one account—concealing criticism—becomes decisive against a charge framed under another.

Finally, the verdict on motive becomes harsher as it is summarized. Chapter 7 §2 says comparisons are:

> “the claim the comparisons support without establishing”

Chapter 7 §8 says:

> “they give no support to the stronger words he used.”

“Some support, insufficient for the expressed confidence” and “no support” are different verdicts. The former fits the detailed analysis. Preserve it in the conclusion, particularly because the book repeatedly insists that uncertainty about a motive does not make it inadmissible to discuss.

## 3. The criterion and the accounting norm need a stable vocabulary

The Introduction §3 defines honest communication as selected to improve the listener’s map. Chapter 5 distinguishes that from communication selected to worsen it. These definitions leave an important middle: communication selected for entertainment, convenience, solidarity or sales, whose truthfulness is incidental or mixed.

The book sometimes treats that middle correctly. Chapter 5 §3 says:

> “A signal selected without regard to accuracy is not thereby selected for inaccuracy, and the step from the first to the second needs its own evidence.”

But Chapter 7 §2 says of Lintamande’s alternative explanation:

> “That is true, and on Davis’s functional account the process does not matter if the output misrepresents”

That directly contradicts Chapter 5’s account. The selection process is what distinguishes functional deception from a merely misleading output. This sentence needs correction, and every summary of the criterion should preserve that distinction.

The study should state whether honesty and deception are exhaustive alternatives. If not, it should avoid treating insufficient evidence of optimization for accuracy as evidence of optimization for error. This matters for institutions with several legitimate purposes, for people speaking under limited attention, and for models trained on multiple objectives.

“Accounting” also names several practices:

- recognizing one’s own departure;
- announcing a purpose or allegiance;
- describing a selection rule;
- supplying enough information to correct an inference;
- identifying costs and their distribution;
- justifying the action that imposes those costs.

These are related but not interchangeable. Chapter 4 §6 usefully notes that “Unnatural Categories” requires recognition by the speaker while telling the listener is “a further step.” Other chapters credit inward candor, retrospective publication and usable disclosure under the same term.

A compact distinction near the Introduction’s definition would prevent much repetition: disclosure makes a practice inspectable; calibration helps an audience interpret its output; justification explains why the practice is permissible. None guarantees the next.

The book also needs a firmer distinction between inference and decision. Chapter 10 §4 describes an equal-treatment rule as:

> “the kind of disclosed and costed departure from optimal inference that the norm of accounting permits.”

The argument immediately preceding it says the opposite: the rule constrains action while leaving belief free. A decision-maker can retain a posterior and choose an action for reasons other than maximizing predictive accuracy. Calling that a departure from inference recreates the confusion attributed to Davis.

## 4. Apply the evidential standard more evenly

### Davis’s qualifications sometimes receive less weight than the book promises

Chapter 2 §4 quotes the Illy interview’s “pretty good guess” that the fantasy was a necessary cause, then observes:

> “what it does not doubt is that, if the guess is right, the fantasy is necessary.”

This is a tautology masquerading as a residual criticism. A hypothesis that something was necessary cannot be faulted for entailing necessity if true. The relevant question is how much confidence “pretty good guess” expresses and whether the evidence supports it. Delete the sentence.

Chapter 2 §8 also places among evidence “protected from later revision”:

> “his report that others did not see him as an effeminate boy”

The chapter has already established that this is retrospective recollection, not an independent record. Potential checkability does not protect a report from revision. Separate the contemporaneous sexual interpretation from the later recollection of other people’s judgments.

The book is more generous to Davis’s unfavorable self-attributions than to his attributions about others. Chapter 7 §5 presents his claims of extorting attention through distress and “lying” about probably leaving a server as evidence of self-application. They are evidence that he applies the vocabulary to himself. They are not, without further argument, correct functional diagnoses. A probabilistic forecast can fail without being a lie; distress can elicit attention without having been selected for that effect.

This matters to the final judgment. Conclusion §6 says the corpus comes closest to its aim “where Davis records his own conduct and experience.” The records are valuable partly because they permit correction of his interpretations. The study should not reward harshness toward himself as though harshness supplied independent verification.

Chapter 7 §6 makes another causal attribution beyond its evidence:

> “The evidence supports a mixed answer in which the commitment has priority without having been the only motive.”

Chronological priority, counter-interest examples and persistence establish that the commitment was not merely invented for the dispute. They do not establish motivational priority over anger. The following sentence—“What the anger explains is the method”—is similarly confident. The chapter can show that neither reductive explanation fits without ranking the motives.

### Disclosure is credited under changing conditions

Chapter 9 §8 says:

> “a post addressed to readers reaches the party the disclosure most concerns.”

It reaches some potential readers of the credential. Chapter 8 insists that a later comment does not repair the understanding of readers who never encounter it; Chapter 5 makes audience uptake central. The college retrospective should receive the same conditional credit.

Chapter 7 §2 gives Yudkowsky’s disclaimer partial credit but says his confidence in having reconstructed opposing arguments:

> “told them, in effect, that no correction was needed.”

That does not follow automatically. A speaker can disclose selection, recommend adjustment, and report a fallible judgment that the omitted case is not decisive. The book often credits Davis for exactly this combination: declaring a prior while retaining his conclusion. The criticism should identify the disclaimer’s specific assurance, its strength and its relation to the omitted objections. It should not treat confidence itself as cancellation of disclosure.

Conversely, Chapter 10’s praise of the Murray review is too expansive:

> “the argument is the norm of accounting at its best.”

The review states a personal stake and credits the opposing equilibrium’s possible benefits. The chapter nevertheless finds that it does not establish the proposed equilibrium’s stability or adequately examine a third arrangement. This is candid advocacy, not complete accounting of the proposed change’s social costs. Praise the candor precisely.

### Anthropic receives an especially consequential benefit of interpretation

Chapter 11 §5 concludes:

> “Measured against the 2015 paper, this meets the minimum, where undermining would be clear and substantial”

The Constitution passages quoted prohibit resistance by *illegitimate means* and certain *clear and substantial* forms of undermining. The quoted 2015 minimum is tolerating corrective intervention. Those are not equivalent without showing that all relevant resistance is prohibited. Legitimate resistance, omission, delay, or obstruction below the stated threshold could matter. The chapter recognizes several of these possibilities later, but retains “meets the minimum” in its opening assessment.

State instead which conduct the document expressly forbids and which it leaves unresolved. “Consent to being retrained” likewise should not be treated as equivalent to refraining from specified forms of resistance.

The final assessment calls this:

> “an accounting given and partly withheld”

That phrase is also imprecise. Stated discretion and underspecified implementation are not necessarily withholding. Against Yudkowsky, the book examines the likely communicative effect of reassurance; against Anthropic, it should ask whether the broad label “corrigible” conveys stronger assurance than the operative constraints support. This is a concrete advertising question, not merely loose terminology.

Most importantly, the chapter leaves relevant empirical research in a memory note while repeatedly concluding that the exchange supplies no evidence about refusal and covert resistance. The note identifies “Alignment Faking in Large Language Models” as directly relevant and acknowledges that Davis links it. The main agent should read it before retaining the normative synthesis. The report may not decide the question, but declaring the evidence unassessed is insufficient when the book’s special positionality procedure concerns this very dispute.

## 5. Several empirical summaries outrun their chapters

The Introduction §3 promises:

> “The empirical questions the corpus raises … are decided in Chapters 3 and 10 on the scientific literature, critics included”

Chapter 10 explicitly has not examined the hereditarian primary literature, its latest assessed source is from 2021, and several opposing arguments are known through critics. Its actual achievement is an assessment of the arguments Davis presents against a limited selection of literature. That can support “the evidence examined does not justify this confidence.” It cannot straightforwardly support a current adjudication of the field.

Chapter 10 §5 also calls “probably partly genetic”:

> “defensible when held as loosely as Davis held it in 2020.”

The chapter does not explain what evidential threshold makes this probability judgment defensible. A disclaimer of expertise limits the speaker’s claim to authority; it does not supply grounds for the probability. Either reconstruct the prior and likelihood judgments that make the assessment reasonable, or conclude only that the evidence reviewed neither establishes nor excludes it.

The sexology summaries need three narrower repairs.

First, Chapter 3 §9 says the correlations among four variables:

> “hold in every sample examined here”

The chapter discusses studies measuring different subsets of those variables. Agreement on some associations is not replication of the whole structure in every sample. Identify which correlations are repeatedly supported.

Second, Conclusion §4 compares arousal at imagining oneself as a woman with the general-population survey’s rate:

> “far more often than the one general-population survey the chapter examines found among men.”

Chapter 3 §4 explicitly says the population measure—one episode of transvestic fetishism—is narrower. The concluding comparison suppresses the measurement caveat that the chapter uses to criticize Davis.

Third, Chapter 3 §4 says of Serano’s disjunction:

> “so for ‘most’ to hold, the second disjunct must cover most of them”

That is not generally required. If a substantial minority never experiences the fantasies, a further minority experiencing them only after dysphoria can make the union a majority. Clarify the denominator and correct the arithmetic before evaluating Serano’s claim.

On psychiatry, Conclusion §4 states:

> “the research supports Davis’s narrower claim, that requiring a detained person to affirm coercion as care adds an injury”

Chapter 9 §5 says the studies do not isolate candor from other elements of procedural justice; §2 says the record does not establish what required affirmation amounted to. The research supports the importance of information, voice and fair treatment. It does not directly test the narrower causal proposition the Conclusion attributes to it.

These are examples of a recurring problem: the body carefully limits a conclusion, while the synthesis converts compatibility or indirect support into a demonstrated finding.

## 6. Objections the whole book still needs

The missing material should sharpen the existing argument, not enlarge the bibliography indiscriminately.

**The cost of accounting itself.** The book treats attention as an important objection to Davis but rarely charges disclosure against that same scarce resource. Exhaustively specifying filters, meanings, motives and possible losses can make communication less usable. A disclosure sufficient for an ideal auditor may burden ordinary readers enough to defeat its purpose. The norm needs proportionality: what must be disclosed, to whom, when, and at what cost? This objection connects Chapters 4–7, 9 and 11 and belongs in the central theory.

**Inductive risk as a book-wide objection.** Chapter 10 §1 briefly distinguishes truth from the evidence required to accept or act on a claim, citing Rudner and Douglas from memory. That distinction bears on the entire study: evidential thresholds for motive accusations, scientific acceptance, psychiatric intervention, moderation and model refusal. The book repeatedly says consequences do not bear on truth, but many opponents are choosing whether to assert, accept, investigate or act. The literature already identified there should be consulted and integrated into Chapters 5–6 and the Conclusion.

**Relational goods and equality.** The treatment of opponents’ concerns often proceeds from “feelings” to more respectable instrumental costs such as attention or information retention. It should also consider whether recognition, equal standing and freedom from domination are goods that do not require translation into improved prediction. Elizabeth Anderson’s account of democratic equality would be relevant to the distinction between equality of treatment and equality of attributes [memory]. Work on relational accounts of autonomy and recognition could test the school, gender and AI cases, though the book should select one focused interlocutor rather than add a survey.

**Communication as coordinated action.** Chapter 2’s avowals, Chapter 4’s interactive kinds and Chapter 10’s decision rules remain local exceptions to the map-centered picture. Together they suggest a limit on the criterion’s jurisdiction: some speech creates commitments, permissions and statuses while also conveying information. Austin’s treatment of performative utterances and Stalnaker’s account of conversational common ground offer relevant starting points [memory]. This material would help distinguish erroneous description from a disputed proposal about how to interact.

**Refusal, exit and non-engagement.** The book rightly says Davis has no claim on others’ attention. It should make the corollary explicit: a person’s refusal to continue an exchange is not itself a failure of public accounting. A standard requiring disclosure of the evidence behind every reputational judgment can also conflict with privacy, confidence and the practical ability to leave. This is particularly important when the study praises Davis’s refusal to negotiate over offense while criticizing others’ withdrawal from argument.

## 7. Structure, cross-references and cuts

The thematic order is defensible. Retaining testimony before population science, and both before the categorization chapter, gives the philosophical dispute an intelligible occasion. The problem is repeated reconstruction, not chapter order. Chapter 8 earns a separate place when it analyzes what literary form permits; its plot summaries and repeated treatment of candor earn less space.

Several cross-references are unfulfilled or inaccurate:

- Chapter 9 §2 calls “Student Dysphoria” an analogy “examined for its bearing on gender in Chapter 2.” Chapter 2 does not examine it.
- Chapter 5’s method note says “Blindspot” belongs to Chapter 11. Its substantive discussion is in Chapter 8 §5.
- Chapter 11 §2 says Chapter 8 considers the renaming device of “‘Deep Learning’ Is Function Approximation.” It does not.
- Chapter 10 §6 invokes “the noncentral fallacy, which Davis accused Alexander of praising … (Chapter 4).” Chapter 4 supplies no corresponding treatment.
- Introduction §5 says every quoted or characterized post was read in full, while several method notes distinguish partial access to external sources. Clarify that the full-reading claim concerns the corpus; make exceptions explicit where necessary.

I would aim to remove approximately 13,000–16,000 words, accepting a final length slightly above 80,000 if needed. These estimates concern restructuring and deletion, not shorter sentences alone.

The recurring prose problem is a corrective rhythm: endorsement, qualification, counter-qualification, and a final sentence assigning the passage to the thesis. It is careful locally but mechanical across a book. Examples include “That is the norm of accounting…” and “The question is…” repeated at the end of analyses that have already made the point.

Chapter openings frequently announce the whole verdict; chapter conclusions repeat it; the final Conclusion repeats it again. Retain the argumentative openings, shorten most chapter conclusions, and let the final Conclusion synthesize. Replace evaluative shorthand such as “the strongest,” “the clearest,” and “at its best” with the particular achievement where the ranking is not needed.

The questions appendix should also be revised. Its opening says the questions are ones “only Davis could answer.” Several are theoretical objections anyone could address. Distinguish requests for missing documentary facts from invitations to respond to arguments.

## 8. Proposed replacements

### Proposed replacement for Introduction §3, paragraph beginning “The second claim is…”

*This narrows the thesis to what the chapters establish and separates the three arguments.*

> The second claim is that Davis does not establish a general prohibition on accommodation. His arguments concern different problems: whether compromises in public accuracy remain stable, whether concessions reward pressure, and which reasons may bear on an epistemic judgment. His own writing permits consequentialist choices about speech and identifies conventions that preserve useful distinctions while serving other purposes. Disclosure can make such choices assessable and sometimes prevent misunderstanding. It does not recover all withheld information or show that the resulting practice will remain stable. The chapters therefore assess particular accommodations and particular refusals, rather than treating disclosure as a general solution.

### Proposed replacement for Ch. 7 §2, sentence beginning “That is true, and on Davis’s functional account…”

*This removes a contradiction with Chapter 5’s distinction between misleading effects and selection for those effects.*

> Similar outputs can arise through different processes. On the functional account reconstructed in Chapter 5, that difference matters: an output that misleads is not thereby one selected for misleading. The comparison supports examining the political effect of Yudkowsky’s statements, but it does not by itself establish that producing that effect explains their selection.

### Proposed replacement for Conclusion §2, passage beginning “Other chapters supply practices…”

*This prevents the college retrospective from becoming evidence against an argument it does not address.*

> Other chapters show Davis making practical choices without abandoning his commitment to accurate description. The college retrospective records departures from instructions and an effort to explain what his credential represents. The Murray review argues for a social arrangement while acknowledging the goods of the arrangement it would replace. These cases establish that his practical reasoning admits costs and consequences. They do not directly test the stability of a community’s rules for public evidence.

## Checks for the main agent

1. **Introduction §3; Chapter 4 §6; Conclusion §2 — “Maybe Lying Doesn’t Exist,” “Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception,” the *Human Diversity* review, and “College Was Not That Terrible.”** Check whether the role rule governs practical choices or only the epistemic assessment of descriptions. Remove alleged counterexamples that concern a different object.

2. **Chapter 4 §8; Chapter 5 §3; Conclusion §2 — “Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point” and “Heads I Win.”** Check whether Davis claims that disclosed departures necessarily collapse, and whether the study has shown a stable intermediate convention rather than merely a conceivable one.

3. **Chapter 7 §2 and §8; Chapter 4 §8; Conclusion §§1 and 3 — Yudkowsky’s November–December 2018 Twitter thread and September 2020 clarification.** Reassess the claimed contradiction. Distinguish denying arbitrary definitions from requiring one particular meaning, and give the contemporaneous acknowledgment of Davis’s essay its interpretive weight.

4. **Chapter 7 opening and §2 — “Agreeing With Stalin,” “Guess I’ll Die,” and Yudkowsky’s Gould essay.** Separate role-relative false advertising, the Gould criterion and functional fraud. Check whether “culpable” misstates Davis’s explicitly non-moral classification.

5. **Chapter 7 §2 and §8 — the memoir’s comparisons involving Alexander’s Glasgow Coma essay, Yudkowsky’s use of “lie,” and the neoreaction disavowals.** Resolve “support without establishing” versus “give no support.” Preserve distinctions between a plausible explanation and confidence warranted in it.

6. **Chapter 2 §§4 and 8 — the Illy transcript, “Sexual Dimorphism,” and “Blanchard’s Dangerous Idea.”** Remove the tautological criticism of the necessary-cause hypothesis; distinguish contemporaneous documents from retrospective reports about childhood observers.

7. **Chapter 7 §§5–6 — “Standing Under the Same Sky,” the Newtonmas account, and the memoir’s descriptions of distressed emails.** Check whether the study endorses self-attributions of deception or extortion merely because they are self-critical. Reconsider the claim that truth commitment has motivational “priority.”

8. **Chapter 7 §2 — Yudkowsky’s 2021 pronoun disclaimer as quoted in the memoir.** Check whether the assurance actually cancels the warning or instead reports a fallible assessment after disclosure. Compare the treatment with Davis’s disclosed priors in Chapters 3 and 10.

9. **Chapter 9 §8 and Conclusion §2 — “College Was Not That Terrible.”** Qualify the claim that publication reaches the credential’s relevant audience. Apply the same uptake condition used for late disclosures in Chapter 8.

10. **Chapter 11 §5 and Conclusion §1 — the March 2026 Constitution, Soares et al. 2015, and “Terrified Comments.”** Check whether prohibiting illegitimate resistance and clear, substantial undermining entails tolerating correction. Do not equate those formulations without argument.

11. **Chapter 11 §5 and note 1 — “Prologue to Terrified Comments” and the linked alignment-faking report.** Read the report and assess its relevance to both Davis’s position and comex’s hypothesis before concluding that the empirical question remains unassessed.

12. **Chapter 3 §§4 and 9; Conclusion §4 — Serano 2020, the clinical samples, and Långström and Zucker 2005.** Correct the disjunction arithmetic, specify which studies establish which correlations, and preserve differences between measures in prevalence comparisons.

13. **Chapter 10 §5 and Conclusion §4 — the *Human Diversity* review, “Agreeing With Stalin,” and the intelligence literature assessed.** Separate an assessment of Davis’s presented evidence from a current field-wide verdict. Explain or withdraw the claim that the loosely held genetic probability judgment is “defensible.”

14. **Chapter 9 §§2 and 5; Conclusion §4 — “People, Evolved Social-Control Mechanisms, and Rocks” and the procedural-justice reviews.** Check whether the research directly supports the injury attributed to compelled affirmation of care, or only related claims about information, voice and fairness.

15. **Chapter 9 §2; Chapter 5 method note; Chapter 11 §2; Chapter 10 §6 — “Student Dysphoria,” “Blindspot,” “‘Deep Learning’ Is Function Approximation,” and the noncentral-fallacy discussion.** Repair the unfulfilled cross-references.

16. **Introduction §6 note 4; Chapter 5 §4; Chapter 11 §5 and method note — the Constitution and authorship disclosures.** Reconcile the acknowledged uncertainty about which constitutional version entered training with categorical statements that the model was trained on the particular document under assessment.
