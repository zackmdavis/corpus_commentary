# Review of Chapter 9: Obedience Tests: School, Psychiatry, and the Honesty of Coercion

The chapter’s strongest contribution is its distinction between Davis’s demand for candor about coercion and his broader account of psychiatric institutions as instruments of social control. It also gives proper weight to his revisions: accepting the medication, reconsidering the paperwork, and describing his own conduct unfavorably. These passages make the chapter an assessment of arguments rather than an explanation of them through illness.

The main revisions should concern the chapter’s own evidential standards. It repeatedly moves from evidence of danger to vindication of detention, from patients’ retrospective approval to institutional purpose, and from relief mixed with guilt to the refutation of responsibility laundering. None of those transitions is secure. Its concluding connection to the book’s coordination thesis is also stronger than the evidence presented. The chapter can preserve its central distinction while reaching narrower, better-supported judgments.

## 1. Separate coercion, care, and compelled assent

The opening says Davis’s complaint is that institutions “describe their compulsion as something else, education in one case and care in the other.” This formulation assumes that those descriptions compete. An intervention can be coercive and educational or therapeutic. The relevant questions are whether the coercion is acknowledged, whether its justification is adequate, and whether the recipient must affirm its value.

Section 2 initially makes this distinction well, then loses it in the sentence:

> “The ward that calls detention care and the movement that calls a student a researcher make the same error in opposite directions.”

Calling detention care does not by itself conceal detention, and calling someone a researcher need not leave that person’s status unchanged. The latter issue belongs principally to Chapter 2; here, attribute the analogy to Davis and identify the additional premises it requires.

The chapter also needs to establish what “required to affirm” means in the psychiatric case. Was agreement with the institution’s account a condition of release, an expectation conveyed by staff, or Davis’s interpretation of therapeutic language? These possibilities differ. The medication threat is concrete evidence of reported pressure; it does not independently establish compelled affirmation that detention was beneficial. Check “People, Evolved Social-Control Mechanisms, and Rocks” and “Memoirs of My Recent Madness, Part II” for the strongest specific example.

Conversely, the chapter is too soft on Davis’s proposed replacement vocabulary. Calling the institution a jail and its intervention punishment could be inaccurate even when detention is coercive. Institutional candor requires an account of the actual reasons and constraints, including uncertainty. It does not require adopting the detainee’s most hostile interpretation.

**Proposed replacement for §2, paragraph beginning “Two claims are folded together…”**

*This separates the demand for candor from claims about purpose and leaves room for coercive care.*

> The passage makes two claims that require different evidence. An institution may add to the injury of confinement by requiring a person to endorse its account of his welfare. Davis’s objection to that demand does not depend on showing that treatment is useless. His proposed description of the ward as punishment makes a further claim: that its governing purpose is to secure compliance for other people’s convenience. Coercion alone does not establish that purpose. A truthful account would have to acknowledge the restriction of liberty while explaining why it was imposed, what benefit was expected, and what alternatives were considered.

## 2. Danger does not establish the necessity of detention

The opening concludes:

> “On the record Davis himself supplies, the institutions in his own case were answering a real danger.”

Section 4 establishes credible grounds for concern. It does not establish the degree or immediacy of danger, the comparative merits of available responses, or the necessity of confinement. Those distinctions matter because the chapter itself presents Davis’s objection as a comparison between confinement among strangers and help obtaining sleep from trusted people.

The qualification “That argument may be right about what would have served him best” acknowledges this objection and then effectively discards it. Section 5 concludes that his case “is one of the examples that does not fall apart.” But an example offered to justify institutionalization could fail because institutionalization was unnecessary or counterproductive, even if the person was distressed and potentially endangered.

The earlier episode supplies still less support. Asking to go to hospital in 2013 establishes that Davis initially sought help. His later statement that he was physically safe establishes his retrospective assessment of one dimension of the experience. Neither establishes that admission was necessary, that the treatment was proportionate, or that psychological injuries were negligible.

Fairness to institutions also requires a stronger reconstruction of their predicament. On Davis’s account, relatives and clinicians faced alarming statements, lost sleep, and uncertainty about his whereabouts. A hypothetical less coercive response is not automatically an available response: someone must be able and willing to provide it. The chapter should ask what alternatives the record actually identifies, without inventing either professional negligence or clinical necessity.

**Proposed replacement for §4, paragraph beginning “On this account…”**

*This preserves the evidence of concern without treating it as a retrospective clinical adjudication.*

> Davis’s account gives substantial grounds for concern about his safety. It also records an initial decision against involuntary detention, followed by a change in circumstances and a different decision. The available record does not establish whether confinement was necessary or whether a less coercive response could have addressed the danger. Davis’s acknowledgment that he was unwell therefore matters without settling his objection to the treatment he received. The dispute concerns both the grounds for intervention and the choice of intervention.

The historical statement about §5150 should be verified against the law applicable in 2017. An emergency time limit alone does not explain the threshold for detention or the relationship between detention and medication consent.

## 3. The research does less adjudicative work than the chapter claims

Section 5 is useful, but it shifts among perceived coercion, harm, therapeutic benefit, justified intervention, and institutional purpose.

> “The literature treats information, voice and candor as part of what determines whether coercion harms…”

The preceding quotations establish associations with **perceived coercion**. They do not, as presented, establish effects on harm. Nor does a correlation with procedural justice isolate candor as the effective component. Being heard, receiving explanations, participating in decisions, and encountering benevolent staff need not have identical effects.

The chapter should therefore say that the research supports the relevance of Davis’s concerns, rather than directly confirming his proposed language or establishing its therapeutic effects.

The largest inferential problem follows:

> “An institution that described its holds as punishment for antics would be honest only if that were its purpose, and the evidence on purpose does not favor Davis.”

The evidence immediately supplied is patients’ later judgment that admission was right. That bears on retrospective endorsement. It does not identify institutional purpose. A system with mixed purposes could produce beneficial outcomes, while a benevolent system could produce harmful ones. The perception that treatment was punitive likewise cannot establish that it was punitive—or that it was not.

The prospective study’s percentages need their denominators and follow-up limitations. The cited range also shows substantial variation that the chapter leaves unexplained. Because three important sources were read only as abstracts, the chapter should not let their findings carry a verdict on institutional function.

“Davis … belongs to the ambivalent group” is too categorical. His account resembles the position described by that group, but endorsing medication while rejecting admission is compatible with judging detention unequivocally wrong. This is an interpretive comparison, not a classification of a research participant.

The Stockholm-syndrome discussion creates another false symmetry. Later approval and continuing resentment both require interpretation, but that does not make them equally unreliable. The proper response is to compare the reasons supplied for each judgment. Davis’s objection to medication pressure does not cease to be reasoned because he was frightened when the pressure occurred.

Finally:

> “He is wrong that the transfer comes with a clean conscience…”

Reported guilt challenges a universal claim about relatives’ psychological relief. It does not refute responsibility laundering as a transfer of authority or social accountability. People could feel guilty while treating a decision as professionally authorized. The chapter must distinguish the draft’s claim about motives, its claim about institutional function, and its claim about subjective conscience.

The unsupported addition that respite is “a fact institutions rarely state” should be removed or documented.

## 4. Do not turn acknowledgment of treatment into wholesale institutional vindication

The chapter handles Davis’s medication concession carefully until it concludes:

> “The same draft’s claim that psychiatry has no procedures to treat mental illness does not survive it, since the drug he now endorses was the treatment.”

This may identify a contradiction, but the scope of “procedures” needs checking in “People, Evolved Social-Control Mechanisms, and Rocks.” Does Davis mean interventions of any kind, reliably effective interventions, or procedures analogous to those of other hospital specialties? The quoted assertion is sweeping and deserves criticism. Nevertheless, endorsing a sedating medication for insomnia is not automatically an endorsement of the institution’s entire treatment model or of coercive administration.

The later claim that “[t]he 2023 draft made no comparable concession about the ward” also needs narrowing. It has already been credited with endorsing medication and correcting an accusation against staff. Perhaps it does not concede the ward’s institutional necessity or the legitimacy of detention. Say that.

The paperwork analysis should remain firmly within Chapter 5’s distinction between error and functionally deceptive communication. A false form copied without intent may still mislead; it is not thereby evidence that the process was selected to mislead. “A false statement produced by a process, with no one deceiving” risks suggesting both that the copying explanation is established and that lack of intention resolves the functional question. Davis’s later explanation is plausible on the account given, not independently verified.

## 5. The school argument needs institutional analysis, not only a recovery narrative

Section 3 says:

> “The coercion Davis describes there was in large part his own.”

His acknowledgment that “The obedience test was all in my head” is significant, but its scope matters. The career college’s pragmatic waiver and the generous course grade show that particular administrators demanded less than he expected. They do not establish how much of his experience of schooling was self-imposed. “What made school unbearable was…” then offers an exhaustive explanation that the available examples cannot sustain.

Distinguish compulsory schooling, credential dependence, classroom authority, and Davis’s interpretation of assignments. The chapter’s university cases involve different constraints from childhood compulsory schooling and involuntary psychiatric detention. The differences should structure the comparison.

The institutional side deserves more than “signaling.” A requirement to use a recently taught method could test mastery of that method rather than merely obedience. A word count might serve an educational aim even when badly designed or inflexibly applied. These are possible justifications to test against “College Was Not That Terrible,” not facts to assume. Equally, the fact that a credential is useful or difficult to bypass does not establish that its requirements are educationally defensible.

Section 8 is also too quick to treat disclosure as the solution:

> “What replaced the exceptionless reading of instructions was disclosure.”

On the quoted evidence, what changed first was Davis’s judgment about which instructions had moral force. Disclosure accompanied that judgment. It does not itself establish that skipping training was permissible, that an audience received adequate notice, or that affected parties accepted the departure. Publishing a retrospective account for readers is different from disclosing noncompliance to the institution when it matters.

The extra-credit protest episode needs context before it can count as “the old escalation intact.” Threatening to approach a department chair may have been proportionate if academic credit was being tied to political participation. Check the event, alternatives, and communication in the 2026 retrospective. “Escalation” is not yet an objection.

Most importantly:

> “The student of 2007 lived that argument with respect to instructions…”

The chapter supplies no evidence that the student’s scrupulosity rested on a coordination argument about exceptions. This conflicts with Chapter 1’s summary, which places the coordination argument’s arrival later. An analogy between inflexible obedience and an exceptionless norm is possible; a shared argumentative basis has not been shown.

**Proposed replacement for §8, paragraph beginning “This is the study’s thesis at small scale.”**

*This preserves the connection to the thesis while avoiding a retrospective attribution.*

> The retrospective offers a practical counterpart to the norm of accounting. Davis describes departures from instructions and makes the resulting qualification available for scrutiny. It does not show that his earlier obedience rested on the coordination argument examined elsewhere in this study, or that disclosure by itself justified each departure. Its contribution is narrower: integrity became compatible with judging instructions rather than treating every instruction as morally binding.

## 6. The Vassar section needs firmer causal and rhetorical distinctions

Reporting Alexander’s retraction prominently is fair and necessary. The chapter nevertheless converts a correction in the public dispute into a causal finding:

> “On the specific claim about February 2017, the record supports Davis.”

A previous episode without recent Vassar contact weakens a simple attribution to Vassar. It does not rule out a contributing influence on a later episode. Likewise, a precipitating public dispute does not exhaust the possible causes. Alexander’s revised explanation resembles Davis’s selection account; the chapter cannot establish the causal graph from their agreement.

The paragraph ending with “the Roberta emails and the April relapse” then introduces temporal proximity suggestively, before disclaiming causal proof. Either explain the relevance of the relapse or omit it from this sequence. The stronger, supportable point concerns Vassar’s recorded encouragement of an adversarial institutional interpretation during recovery.

The treatment of Davis’s 2021 comment also risks demanding excessive disclosure. A person rebutting a particular allegation about his psychiatric history need not publish every adjacent vulnerability to make the correction honest. The question is whether his broader statements about Vassar invited an inference that the omitted episodes would materially change. Quote and assess those statements precisely. The “defense lawyer” admission helps, but does not settle that question.

In §6, refusal to confirm a patient’s presence or pass messages should be assessed against possible confidentiality and consent constraints, without assuming their application. Even “an undelivered message is evidence of negligence” is too strong without knowing what staff could disclose, what the patient authorized, and what was promised. The 20:1 wager also requires competing predictions: a successful prediction does not establish the “lying kidnappers” theory if mundane alternatives predict the same result.

## 7. Scholarship to add selectively

These are research leads from memory, not verified sources or conclusions:

- **Erving Goffman, *Asylums*.** [memory] Its account of total institutions, institutional routines, and the management of inmates’ identities could sharpen Davis’s school–ward comparison. Use it to identify mechanisms and limits, not to declare universities and wards equivalent.
- **Bennett W. Helm and Robert Paul Wolff are less immediately useful here than a focused account of educational authority; R. S. Peters’s “Education as Initiation” is one candidate.** [memory] Peters can supply an opposing account of initiation into practices whose value learners may not initially recognize. This would make the educational case more than a dispute over credentials.
- **Ivan Illich, *Deschooling Society*.** [memory] Illich distinguishes learning from institutional certification and challenges the identification of education with schooling. This would contextualize Davis’s objection without treating personal dysfunction as its explanation.
- **Randall Collins, *The Credential Society*, alongside Caplan.** [memory] Collins treats credential expansion through status competition and occupational closure; Caplan emphasizes signaling. Their differences would prevent “the institution persists” from doing duty as an explanation of its legitimacy.
- **Paul S. Appelbaum, *Almost a Revolution*, and Appelbaum and Thomas Grisso, “Assessing Patients’ Capacities to Consent to Treatment.”** [memory] These are leads for distinguishing commitment, treatment consent, and decision-making capacity. The chapter currently needs those distinctions more than another general verdict on psychiatry.
- **Alan Wertheimer, *Coercion*.** [memory] This offers resources for distinguishing threats, constrained choices, and justified coercion. It could clarify why perceived coercion and wrongful coercion are different questions.
- **Szasz requires fuller treatment or omission.** [memory] His position concerns the status of psychiatric illness claims and the legitimacy of coercive psychiatry; “denied that mental illness was illness” is too compressed to establish the proposed contrast with Davis.

The current scholarship is strongest on patient experience and weakest on educational authority, consent, and institutional structure. Add a few sources that repair those gaps rather than a broad literature survey.

## 8. Structure, overlap, and prose

The chapter is already over its ceiling. Make room through consolidation:

- Combine the opening, §2’s two-claim distinction, and §9’s repeated verdict into one carefully qualified framing.
- Keep the institutional consequences of the Vassar exchange here; leave the extended advocacy and disclosure analysis to Chapter 7, which already owns that problem.
- Reduce the “Charles Goodhart” coda. Its second reading is clearly labeled, but linking the fictional boy’s collapse to Davis’s psychiatric experience adds speculation. The distinction between institutional approval and correctness is sufficient.
- Replace “The plan for this study asked…” with the substantive question. Remove “This is the study’s thesis at small scale.”
- Cut formulaic verdicts such as “The concession is real, and so is the distinction” and “The prediction is the right kind of claim to make.” The preceding analysis should establish their content.
- Fulfil or remove the promise in §1 to examine Davis’s reasons for declining diagnostic categories.
- Add the required unnumbered Note on method, distinguishing Davis’s reports, clinical material reproduced by him, independently read public comments, and abstract-only research.

The chapter should end with a limited finding: Davis’s accounts support his demand for intelligible, contestable authority and undermine some of his sweeping accusations. They do not settle whether his detention was necessary, what psychiatric institutions principally serve, or whether disclosure solves the problem of legitimate disobedience.
