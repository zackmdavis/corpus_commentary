The chapter is clear, the reconstructions are economical, and Davis comes across as a philosopher with a position rather than a polemicist with a grievance. That is the right register. The main problems are these, ranked:

1. The verdict "the functional account is right" is asserted, not argued. The chapter never states the strongest reasons the intentional tradition keeps intent, and one of those reasons comes from Davis himself.
2. The Ch. 4 handoff is unfulfilled. Ch. 4 leaves "general deception theory, Lewis–Skyrms, Martínez at length" to this chapter, and this chapter only cross-refers back to Ch. 4 §6. 3. The Williams section contradicts itself, and its claim that Davis's account "has trouble saying what trust is for" is unfair to an account in which trust is the receiver's model of the sender's filter.
4. The attribution problem is treated as a problem of false positives only. Davis's side of it, that too high an evidential bar protects optimized deceivers, is missing. Without it the chapter is unfair to him and leaves Ch. 6 with nothing to pick up.
5. The chapter states the "Higher Innocence" counterfactual test as the standard and then declines to apply it to the one case in the chapter where it obviously applies ("Contra Piper", which postdates the draft). It also half-applies the test to the memoirs while saying it leaves that question to Ch. 7.
6. The prose has a recurring grader's cadence: report, then a one-line stamp ("The post is correct", "That criterion is right", "The argument is sound as far as it goes"). The plan forbids scorecard tone, and this is it at sentence scale.

## 1. Weak or overstated arguments

**1.1 "The functional account is right" (§1) and "It is correct in its central claim" (§6).** No section argues this. §2 reconstructs the account and reports one convergent paper. It then treats convergence as confirmation (see 1.2) and moves on to applications. A reader from the deception literature will ask at least three questions, and the chapter needs answers to them before it can give a verdict:

- *Is the disagreement with the intentional tradition substantive or verbal?* The chapter itself reports Mahon's point that the tradition already has an intent-free word, "mislead" (§2, "The academic convergence"). If "algorithmic deception" just means "functional misleading", Davis and the tradition disagree about which word should carry the weight. By Davis's own Ch. 4 framework, that is a question about which boundary serves which predictive purposes. It is a respectable question, but it is not "the account is right". The advertising-pipeline case, which the chapter says the functional account "plainly" gets right, is exactly the case the intentional theorist re-describes as misleading without deceiving, or as deception whose intent lies with whoever chose "sells best" as the objective. "Plainly" is unearned.
- *Receiver-relativity.* The chapter notes, correctly, that Davis's definition makes deception relative to a listener's inference algorithm (§2, "Two features of this definition matter"). Then it drops the point. It has large consequences. A filtered report is deceptive relative to a naive listener and not deceptive relative to one who knows the filter. That is why "blatant cherry-picking is the best kind!" works: disclosure changes the receiver's algorithm, not the signal. It also means that whether a given utterance is deceptive depends on a choice of reference receiver, and Davis's "Conflict Theory of Bounded Distrust" turns precisely on savvy and clueless receivers drawing different conclusions from one signal. The chapter should say which receiver Davis's definition presupposes and whether he is consistent about it. This is where the theory meets the norm of accounting (thesis part (b)), so it is the most thesis-relevant point in §2.
- *The excluded middle.* As the chapter quotes "Algorithmic Intent", honesty is "signals selected on the criterion of making receivers have more accurate beliefs" and deception is "signals selected for producing less accurate beliefs". Most speech fits neither: it is selected for pleasing, for status, or for brevity, and its effect on accuracy is incidental. This is Frankfurt's territory, which the chapter meets only through a commenter (1.6). The gap matters for the attribution problem. The inference Davis's critics object to typically moves from "not optimized for accuracy" to "optimized against it". The chapter should say whether the definitions as written license that move. As quoted they do not, and the chapter could credit that.

**1.2 "Since neither author draws on the other, the convergence is independent evidence for the functional account" (§2).** Two theorists who draw on the same biological signaling literature (Krstić through Artiga & Paternotte; Davis through Skyrms, as Ch. 4 reports) and reach a similar definition are not independent in the way two measurements are. Their agreement is evidence that the definition is a natural extension of the biology. It is not evidence that the extension is correct for persons, which is what is disputed. It is also unclear what "evidence for" a definition means here. Suggest: "Krstić reached a similar position by a different route, which suggests the position is not an idiosyncrasy of Davis's" and no more. The §1 word "independently" has the same problem.

**1.3 "Krstić's vocabulary would also have helped Davis: the distinction between deceptive and deceitful could have spared many of his comment threads their arguments over whether 'lying' was the right word" (§2).** This is counterfactual and probably false to the texts. "Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist" is *about* whether the word should move. Davis had a qualifier ("algorithmic") that did Krstić's job, and the arguments happened anyway because he was making a claim about the folk concept. Cut the sentence or reframe it as a difference: Krstić keeps "deceitful" for intent, while Davis's "Lying Alignment Chart" puts his own position in a cell where an intent-free utterance *is* a lie (see Check 3). That difference is worth stating. The chapter also reports Davis saying he "frowns on the expanded sense of 'lying'" and then reports a chart on which his position is the expanded sense. These need to be reconciled or the tension named.

**1.4 "Its structural claim fares no better" ("The slippery slope", §3).** This overstates Ch. 4. As summarized, Ch. 4 §8 argues that the coordination argument has a defensible rule-consequentialist structure and supports an exceptionless rule against *concealment*. That is a partial vindication of the structure, redirected, not a failure on the order of the example's being "rigged". Also, Kaas's quip ("how come we've been standing on it for over a decade?") is not, by itself, an objection to a claim about instability over time. Vaniver's free-speech-law equilibrium is the real counterexample. Say so, and say whether Davis answered either (Check 7).

**1.5 "He need only deny that it is an adequate account of honesty, and Alexander's distinction concedes that it is not" (§3, "Not lying is not enough").** This lets Davis off too lightly. The post's title says firming up not-lying is "less broadly useful than one might initially think", and its central hypothesis, as quoted, is that adopting the wizard's code *trains* people to mislead. That is a claim that the rule has a cost, not just that it is incomplete. Alexander's reply (a bright-line rule is valuable as the enforceable shadow of an ideal) meets that claim head on, because a floor can be worth having even if some people learn to stand exactly on it. The two positions conflict over whether the rule's net effect on honesty is positive, which is empirical. The chapter already calls Davis's dynamic claim "empirical and unverified". It should then say that Alexander's objection survives where it bears. The sentence "This is the strongest objection to the post, and it is an objection to an inference the post does not quite make" names an objection and then dismisses it in one breath.

**1.6 Stupidity and dishonesty (§3).** "Davis's interlocutors were mostly very intelligent people, so the graph tilts his explanations of their errors toward dishonesty." This is a claim about Davis's practice, supported in this chapter by no instance. It is also not a tilt in the sense of a bias. With a low prior on stupidity, a higher posterior on dishonesty is the graph working correctly. The real weakness is the one the commenters named: the graph has two causes where there are at least four (Frankfurtian indifference, simple slips, and, unmentioned, differences in premises or values, which produce "errors" that are not errors from the other side). Restate the criticism as an omitted-variable problem. Drop the claim about Davis's interlocutors or move it to Ch. 7 with instances. As written the paragraph also says the same thing three times ("The post is correct, but…"; "The inference is valid; its strength depends…"; "a question the post does not ask"). And if Davis replied to Leong or Najt, "a question the post does not ask" may be unfair (Check 8).

**1.7 "Davis often writes as though they were" (end of "Communication and its maintenance").** "Often" with no citation. Either give two or three dated instances, or drop the sentence. Note also that "That is a fair description of Davis's demand, and an important one for judging it" adopts Adele Lopez's characterization ("liquid helium") without testing it against the post.

**1.8 The MDL reply (§5).** The chapter's critique of the role rule is the chapter's best idea and should stay: Davis names an epistemic cost ("not-readily-falsifiable") and answers it as if it were a social one ("socially harmful"). But the follow-up sentence is muddled: "A description that 'shortens the message' for the accuser need not be true, and a practice of freely attributing hidden motives produces many such descriptions that are false." Under the MDL criterion Davis invokes, a false description of someone's motives does *not*, in expectation, shorten the encoding of observations of their behavior. That is the criterion's point. Davis therefore has a reply: his criterion already excludes the false attributions. The real problem is small samples and motivated choice of hypothesis. With a handful of observations, many motive hypotheses compress equally well, and the accuser picks the one he wanted. That is overfitting, and Davis's own "Heads I Win" footnote about his "(subconsciously) selected" assumptions describes the mechanism. Proposed replacement in §4 of this review (P2).

**1.9 The Alexander exchange (§5).** "The attribution of motivated obtuseness was not obviously right, and the conversation that followed it went better than the attribution predicted." It is unclear what the attribution "predicted", and a good conversation the next day bears on neither its truth nor its falsity. Fairness also runs the other way. Alexander amended his essay within the week, which a reader could take as evidence that he *had* been missing something. Not motivatedly, perhaps, but Davis was not simply wrong that the exchange was stuck. The honest description: the exchange shows that a motive attribution can be made, denied, and left undecidable on the evidence, and that the dispute got unstuck by a conversation that changed the subject from Alexander's motives to the argument. Did Davis later retract or qualify "motivatedly"? If so, say so (Check 9).

**1.10 "Contra Piper" (§5).** "This is the right answer to the unfalsifiability worry" overstates. The cookie-spreadsheet example works because the alleged motive (favoritism in purchasing) has a paper trail. Motives alleged about speech, such as selective emphasis or pandering, rarely leave comparable records, and those are the cases the attribution problem is about. The duty on the accuser ("A motive claim is admissible to the extent that it is tied to behavior others can inspect") is the chapter's own inference. That is fine, but mark it as the chapter's, not Davis's. Then the Piper reading: the chapter says the offer to talk "is what keeps the post on the right side of the line." An offer to be corrected does not make a public attribution warranted. On the chapter's own standard, the question is whether Davis would have read the same warning from an ally as a "veiled negotiating threat". See 1.11.

**1.11 The "Higher Innocence" test applied selectively (§5).** The chapter says the draft's counterfactual test is "the standard the attribution problem needs", then defers its application to Ch. 7. But "Contra Piper" (Sept 2026) postdates the draft (July 2026) and contains a published motive attribution. It is the obvious test case for whether Davis applies his own standard once he has stated it, and it is already in the chapter. Apply the test there, or say why it cannot be applied (no ally case to compare). Conversely, the chapter half-applies the test to the memoirs while saying it will not: "He judges the episode by what it shows about policy and makes no claim about the journalist's soul, which is what the earlier attributions of algorithmic dishonesty did not do." That prejudges Ch. 7. It is also confused. *Algorithmic* dishonesty is by definition not a claim about the soul, so the contrast is not soul against policy. The contrast is between claims about what a person's behavior is optimized for, which need counterfactual evidence, and claims about their policies, which can be inspected. Rewrite the sentence along those lines and leave the memoir verdict to Ch. 7.

**1.12 Weight on an unpublished draft.** The chapter's climax is a passage Davis has not published. The brief puts drafts in scope, but the chapter should say what that status does and does not license. It shows what Davis was thinking in July 2026. It does not show a position he stands behind publicly, and a draft can be abandoned. "Stated by the person who created the problem and applied first to himself" is also misleading on chronology: the attributions to others came first (2019–2024) and the self-application came in 2026. The opening's "applies the standard first to himself" invites the same misreading.

**1.13 The test itself is not examined.** A counterfactual test about one's own behavior ("would I have read six pages for an ally?") is itself hard to know, and the ally case is rarely observed. In practice the test is applied through *patterns* across cases, which the chapter says ("patterns sometimes do"). But a pattern shows that some filter correlated with allegiance exists, not which motive drives it. Loyalty, fear, fatigue, and pandering all produce the same asymmetry. This matters for Ch. 7, since thesis 3(ii) concerns specific motive words ("pander", "optimized stupidity"). The test can establish selection. Naming the motive takes a further step, and the chapter should say so.

**1.14 "Social Punishment and Shared Maps" praised without testing (§3).** The chapter calls this post "the account at its best". But its central claim, as summarized, is that limiting social punishment "cannot avoid degrading the shared map". That is exposed to the chapter's own disclosure remedy. A norm such as "don't broadcast true negative facts except in these circumstances" is a disclosed filter, and by "Heads I Win" a disclosed filter leaves a Bayesian's map intact. If the post concedes this (the "one distinction available" between publishing evidence and announcing a trade policy may be it), then the praise should say how far the concession reaches. If it does not, the chapter has found a gap and should say so. Either way, "Here the account is at its best, precise about mechanism and frank that its conclusion will not be socially accepted" is a verdict stamp in place of an assessment.

**1.15 The "Fake Deeply" close (§6).** "That is the account's deepest claim, that deception spreads and honesty is cheaper in the long run." The functional account makes no claim about cost. That is a separate empirical thesis, and §3 reported Adele Lopez's objection against something close to it. The chapter's last move therefore attributes to the theory a claim the chapter has just put in doubt. The pun that follows ("evidence deep enough to bear it") is a decorative closer. Ch. 11 owns the story, and this chapter could end without it.

**1.16 "The theory's single serious weakness" (§6).** The chapter itself identifies others: the trust problem (§4), Lopez's room-temperature objection, Ninety-Three's counterexample, and (if 1.1 is adopted) receiver-relativity. "Single" contradicts the body.

## 2. Fairness to Davis, in both directions

**Too harsh.**

- *Trust (§4).* "A theory of honesty that defines deception without reference to trust and so has trouble saying what trust is for." Davis's account has trust in it. In "Heads I Win" the decisive variable is whether the reporter is one "whom you trust to be impartial", and trust is the receiver's model of the sender's selection function. "Communication Requires Common Interests or Differential Signal Costs" and "So You Don't Trust Me?" are both about how that model is maintained. Sabien's objection, as the plan describes it, is a different one: whether to extend trust *beyond* what the evidence warrants, so as to lower verification costs. That is a real disagreement, and it should be stated as such. Proposed replacement in §4 of this review (P1).
- *"He disclosed the strategy, as his theory requires, but only after using it" (§3).* A sharp sentence resting on one comment. Check the qualifier "mostly" in "mostly a strategic political concession" (Check 6). If the disclaimer was partly sincere, the gotcha weakens. If it stands, write it as an observation, not a closing jab.
- *Stupidity/dishonesty and "often writes as though" (1.6, 1.7).* Both make claims about Davis's practice without instances.
- *The role rule.* The chapter calls the 2020 answer "a poor answer". It should credit, before criticizing, what Davis's rule was trying to protect: a description's truth is not to be decided by its social convenience. That is the same principle Ch. 4 defends in its narrow form. The criticism (1.8) is that the rule is applied to a cost that is not social. Say that cleanly and drop "poor".

**Too generous.**

- "Among the best things he wrote" (§1), "plainly gives the right answer" (§2), "the account at its best" (§3), and the offer to talk as what keeps "Contra Piper" "on the right side of the line" (§5). See 1.1, 1.10, 1.14.
- The hero framing of §5's ending ("stated by the person who created the problem and applied first to himself") turns an unpublished draft into a redemption arc (1.12).

**Concessions acknowledged and then ignored.**

- The chapter reports Davis's 2016 preference for "more lies and fewer delusions on the margin", then says he "built a theory around that preference". But the functional theory *erases* the distinction the preference relies on. The preference also suggests a reason intent matters on Davis's own cartographic terms: a conscious liar keeps an accurate map somewhere, which can in principle be recovered, and a self-deceiver does not. Wei Dai's four reasons (§5) include the converse, that conscious lies are harder to detect and draw on more intelligence. Between them they show that intent bears on *epistemic* repair, not only on blame. This is the strongest objection to the intent-free account, and it comes partly from Davis himself. Put it in §2 before any verdict (Check 1).
- Davis's statement in "Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist" that he frowns on loose uses of "lie" and "fraud" "where intent is hard to establish" is reported in §2 and never brought back in §5. It is Davis's own early statement of the attribution problem, a year before "Algorithmic Intent", and the chapter should connect them. It also complicates the claim that he "dismissed" the danger. He held both positions, and the chapter should say how they fit.
- The "Heads I Win" footnote is reported as the theory applied to its author, but the chapter never asks whether the corpus's own advocacy discloses its filter. That question may belong to Ch. 7, but the chapter should mention it.

## 3. Missing objections

**From Davis's side.**

- *The other error.* The attribution problem has two kinds of error. A demanding evidential standard for motive attributions protects the falsely accused. It also protects optimized deceivers, whose behavior is by hypothesis shaped to be deniable. That asymmetry is the core of "Assume Bad Faith" and of the "Algorithmic Intent" examples (the father who "loses control" only when it pays; the baker who mishears on cue). Those are cases where demanding intent-level evidence guarantees that the pattern is never named. The chapter needs at least a paragraph on this, with a pointer to Ch. 6. Proposed paragraph P3.
- *Algorithmic attribution as a de-escalation.* Davis can argue that saying a behavior is optimized to mislead is *less* accusatory than calling someone a liar, because it makes no claim about conscious bad faith. The chapter reports Viliam's reply that readers will hear "intent" in the usual sense. The exchange should be set out as a disagreement in which each side has a point.
- *MDL as an epistemic criterion.* See 1.8.

**From the critics' side.**

- *Blame.* If "algorithmic liar" is heard as blame, as Viliam warns, the functional account brings in moral conclusions it has not argued for. The chapter does not ask whether Davis's account of honesty is meant to bear moral weight (the "fraud" qualifier suggests it is) or only descriptive weight.
- *Advocacy.* Every advocate selects arguments. Is an attorney's brief deceptive? On Davis's account it is not, because the filter is common knowledge in that setting. That answer is good, and it again shows receiver-relativity doing the work (1.1). It is also a test case for his own one-sided writing.
- *Self-sealing.* The chapter makes the point well for "So You Don't Trust Me?" (every response to the accusation confirms it). It is a general property of motive theories, and the chapter could say so once and cite the literature below.

## 4. Proposed prose

These follow the study's voice rule. Quotations inside them are copied from the chapter's own text unless marked.

**P1. Replacement for the second paragraph of §4 ("The comparison clarifies…").**

> The comparison locates Davis's account more exactly than the plan's labels did. Williams's Accuracy concerns the care a speaker takes over what he believes; Davis's criterion concerns the effect of a speaker's signals on what others believe, and so belongs with Sincerity, broadened in the way Williams himself broadens it when he says that "in relying on what someone said, one inevitably relies on more than what he said." On that point the two agree, and the clever not-liar fails both. Where they part is over trust. For Williams trust is the end that truthfulness serves, and a speaker's sincerity is something a hearer may rely on without checking. For Davis trust is the hearer's model of the speaker's filter: the reporter in "Heads I Win" misleads because she is trusted "to be impartial (but isn't)," and the remedy is to make the filter known, so that reliance becomes calibrated. Davis's account can say what trust is for. It is less clear that it can say when trust should outrun the evidence, which is the question Duncan Sabien presses, and which [Chapter 6] takes up.

(The Williams quotation and "to be impartial (but isn't)" are from the chapter. "Heads I Win" has "a reporter whom you trust to be impartial (but isn't)", as the chapter quotes it. Check that the shortened form matches the source exactly.)

**P2. Replacement for the sentences after the "not allowed to care" block quote, up to the gjm citation (§5).**

> The second paragraph is part (c) in its role-constitutive form, and it answers the wrong objection. Davis's first paragraph names two costs: accusations of algorithmic dishonesty will be acrimonious, and they will be "not-readily-falsifiable." The rule about what a rationalist may care about disposes of the first, and properly, since the social convenience of a description is no evidence of its truth. It does not touch the second, which is an epistemic cost. Davis's criterion might seem to cover it. A false account of someone's motives does not, over enough observations, shorten the description of his behavior. But an attribution of motive is usually made on few observations. Several hypotheses then compress them about equally well, and the accuser chooses among them. Davis described this mechanism in his own "Heads I Win" footnote, warning that his modeling assumptions might have been "(subconsciously) selected for the resulting conclusions."

(The final quoted fragment is from the chapter's quotation of the footnote. Check that the fragment is exact in the source.)

**P3. New paragraph, to follow the Wei Dai / Alexander paragraph in §5.**

> The problem has a second side, which Davis states more forcefully than his critics. A high evidential standard for attributing motive protects people falsely accused. It also protects behavior that is optimized to be deniable, and the examples in "Algorithmic Intent" are chosen to show this: the father who "loses control" only when calm conversation is failing him, and the baker who mishears only the questions he would rather not answer. A standard that demands evidence of conscious intent will never convict either of them, and that is the point of their behavior. Any adequate standard has to weigh both errors, and the disagreement between Davis and his critics is partly over their relative cost. His argument that the cost of false negatives is the greater one, in "Assume Bad Faith," is assessed in [Chapter 6].

(Unverified: that "Assume Bad Faith" makes this argument about relative cost. Check 12.)

**P4. Replacement for the first three sentences of §6.**

> Davis's account of deception is intent-free and receiver-relative: a signal deceives when it is selected to worsen the predictions of a listener who decodes it in good faith. Recent philosophy has moved toward a similar view, and Davis's applications of it hold up: selective reporting misleads without false sentences, disclosure of the filter restores the listener's map, and a rule against literal falsehood leaves the rest of honesty unregulated. Two difficulties remain. The account depends on a choice of reference listener, which Davis does not always make explicit. And it makes attributions about people depend on evidence of how they would have behaved otherwise, which is rarely available.

## 5. Prose that reads as AI-generated

The chapter is better than average on this, but some patterns recur.

- **Verdict stamps after reports.** "The counterexample is correct, and it shows…"; "That criterion is right, and it is an evidential standard."; "The post is correct, but its use in practice depends…"; "The inference is valid; its strength depends…"; "The argument is sound as far as it goes."; "This is the right answer to the unfalsifiability worry"; "The remedy is real but partial, and the post says so"; "That is a fair description of Davis's demand, and an important one for judging it."; "Its structural claim fares no better."; "The test is demanding."; "Here the account is at its best". Most of these could be removed with no loss. Where a verdict is needed, attach it to the reason in the same sentence.
- **"Two X" openers announcing structure.** "Two features of this definition matter."; "Two qualifications should be made in fairness to Yudkowsky."; "Two exchanges in the record show the problem in practice."; "Two later posts extend the account…". Four in one chapter is a tic.
- **Signposting and self-reference.** "The joke contains the problem this chapter is about."; "His later posts supply something better (§5)."; "Call that difficulty the attribution problem."; "the one this chapter has called the attribution problem"; "What can be said here is that…"; "The second example matters more than the first." The thesis is stated three times before §2: in the abstract paragraph, in "The functional account is right…", and in the seams paragraph. Keep one statement, preferably the second, and fold the seams into a sentence or a note.
- **"Not X but Y" and its variants.** "a property of communication, not of the communicator's conscious state"; "The claim is about the theory behind the frown, not about the frown itself."; "The objection is not only that … It is that…"; "embarrassment, not defensiveness"; "from inner states, which observers cannot see, to policies, which they can." Each is defensible alone. Together they make a rhythm.
- **Triads and anaphora.** "selective reporting, not-technically-lying, and the parasitism of deception on shared meaning"; "functional, behavioral, and indifferent to the deceiver's conscious state"; "the same care withheld from a rival and extended to an ally, the same question answered when it is convenient and misheard when it is not, the same standard invoked against one faction and waived for another" (three-part anaphora at the chapter's rhetorical peak); "precise about mechanism and frank".
- **Epigrammatic closers.** "The theory's own discipline points the same way for accusations: to hold an attribution of deception, you need evidence deep enough to bear it."; "The functional account holds that minds can be read from behavior, which licenses reading only as much as the behavior that has been gathered."; "He disclosed the strategy, as his theory requires, but only after using it."
- **Intensifiers.** "Most important, the standard applies to the accuser."; "plainly gives the right answer"; "unusually candid"; "took it up at once".
- **Machinery.** "(These figures reproduce.)" in running text belongs in the method note. "every quotation has been checked mechanically against its source" in the method note comes close to "by script". "The theory is applied to its author within its own apparatus" is jargon.

## 6. Relevant scholarship left out

The plan's external list for this chapter is mostly unused: Skyrms, Fallis & Lewis 2019, Martínez 2019, Artiga & Paternotte 2018 (used only through Krstić), Millikan, Grice (used only through Martin's review of Williams), Shiffrin, Carson, von Hippel & Trivers, and Zahavi/costly signaling. Ch. 4's handoff makes Skyrms and Martínez obligatory here. In order of relevance:

- **Signaling-game definitions of deception.** Skyrms, *Signals* (2010), defines deception in a signaling game roughly as a signal that raises the probability of a false state, to the sender's benefit and the receiver's cost **[memory]**. Fallis & Lewis, "Toward a formal analysis of deceptive signaling," *Synthese* (2019), criticize it **[memory; on the plan's fetch list]**. Martínez, "Deception as cooperation" (2019), argues that some deceptive signals are maintained by partial common interest **[memory]**. Davis wrote a comment on it ("Comment on 'Deception as Cooperation'", 2021, a core text in the plan that the chapter never cites). Comparing Davis's "systematically results in less accurate predictions" with Skyrms's benefit condition matters: Davis drops the sender-benefit condition for "deception" and keeps it only for "fraud". That is a substantive choice worth a paragraph. "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!" (2020) is also a plan core text and is presumably where Davis engages Skyrms directly. It is uncited here.
- **Function attribution.** Millikan's teleosemantics, and the debate between etiological functions (selection history) and Cummins-style causal-role or dispositional functions **[memory]**, map exactly onto the chapter's §5 distinction between "selection history" and "pattern of responses across counterfactual situations". The chapter uses both senses of "optimized for" without naming the choice. Krstić picks the etiological sense (as the chapter reports). Davis's robustness-to-perturbation criterion in "Algorithmic Intent" sounds dispositional. If so, the convergence is weaker than claimed, and the attribution problem looks different under each sense: selection histories are mostly unobservable, while dispositions can sometimes be tested.
- **Dennett directly.** "Real Patterns" (1991) states the compression criterion for the intentional stance explicitly **[memory]**. The chapter credits it to Achmiz's comment. A primary citation would strengthen §5 and connect it to Davis's MDL language.
- **Self-deception.** Mele, *Self-Deception Unmasked* (2001), gives a deflationary account of self-deception as motivated bias without an intention to deceive oneself **[memory]**. It is the philosophical position closest to Davis's for the "Lies and Delusion" problem. Von Hippel & Trivers, "The evolution and psychology of self-deception," *BBS* (2011), argue that self-deception serves the deception of others **[memory; on the plan's list]**. It is the empirical backbone of the Hansonian premise. Simler & Hanson, *The Elephant in the Brain* (2018), is the source of Davis's "elephant in my brain" **[memory]**. The chapter mentions "Hanson's hidden motives" without citing it.
- **Lying and misleading.** Carson, *Lying and Deception* (2010); Fallis, "What Is Lying?" (2009); Stokke, *Lying and Insincerity* (2018); Lackey, "Lies and deception: an unhappy divorce" (2013) **[all memory]**. Mahon's SEP entry, already fetched, surveys most of these. Shiffrin, *Speech Matters* (2014), on lying and the "justified suspended context" **[memory]**. Shiffrin is useful because she defends the moral distinctiveness of lying on grounds about the role of sincere assertion, which is the strongest version of Alexander's bright-line reply.
- **Frankfurt, *On Bullshit* (1986/2005)** **[memory]**, cited directly, not only through Chris Leong. See 1.1, the excluded middle.
- **Costly signaling and its revision.** Zahavi's handicap principle; Grafen 1990; Lachmann, Számadó & Bergstrom, "Cost and conflict in animal signals and human language," *PNAS* (2001) **[memory]**. The last argues that honest signaling is often maintained by the costs of *deviation* (punishment, lost credibility in repeated play), not by costs paid in equilibrium. That bears directly on "Communication Requires Common Interests or Differential Signal Costs" (whose title reads like a two-option version of the older view) and gives Adele Lopez's room-temperature point its theoretical basis.
- **Epistemic vigilance.** Sperber et al., "Epistemic Vigilance," *Mind & Language* (2010); Mercier, *Not Born Yesterday* (2020) **[memory]**. Receiver-side mechanisms that keep communication stable under divergent interests. This is the scholarly form of Lopez's objection, and it bears on the receiver-relativity point.
- **The psychology of motive attribution.** Pronin, Lin & Ross, "The bias blind spot" (2002); Pronin, Gilovich & Ross, "Objectivity in the eye of the beholder" (2004), which finds that people attribute disagreement to the other party's bias; Ross's fundamental attribution error (1977) **[all memory]**. This is the empirical literature on the attribution problem itself, and it is symmetric: it predicts that both Davis and his interlocutors will overattribute motive to each other. Using it would make the attribution problem a general human problem that Davis's theory inherits, rather than a defect peculiar to Davis.
- **Law as an evidential model for intent.** The "Higher Innocence" six-pages case closely parallels the doctrine of *willful blindness*, which treats deliberate avoidance of knowledge as knowledge (in U.S. law, *Global-Tech Appliances v. SEB*, 2011) **[memory]**. Flexman's "should-have-been-very-easy-to-tell" standard resembles negligent misrepresentation as distinct from fraud, which requires scienter **[memory]**. Disparate-impact doctrine is an intent-free standard established by statistical pattern rather than single cases **[memory]**, which matches the chapter's "single texts rarely meet it, but patterns sometimes do". One paragraph on how courts infer intent from conduct would give the attribution problem a mature comparison class. Davis's own "fair trial" language in "Liability" invites it.
