# Review: Algorithms of Deception

The chapter’s strongest achievement is its separation of a functional account of deception from the evidence needed to apply that account to a person. Its treatment of “Firming Up” also gives Davis’s critics a substantive answer: a rule can leave misleading speech unregulated and still improve conduct. These distinctions deserve to organize the chapter.

The principal weakness is that the chapter does not consistently distinguish misleading effects, selection for misleading effects, and selection for some other outcome that happens to mislead. It sometimes grants the functional attribution before examining the evidence that would establish it. Its remedy—disclosing the filter—is also stronger in the exposition than either the quoted qualifications or the chapter’s own objections warrant. These problems affect the book’s thesis, since later chapters rely on disclosure to reconcile accuracy with permissible departures from other norms.

The comments below assess the packet’s presentation. Characterizations of the underlying posts and threads require checking against those sources.

**1. Establish which functional account the chapter defends**

The opening defines deception as communication “optimized to make a receiver’s predictions worse.” The first quotation from “Algorithms of Deception!” instead concerns communication that “systematically results in less accurate predictions.” Later, the chapter distinguishes selected effects from dispositions and says sender benefit has been dropped.

These are substantial differences. A process can reliably produce misleading reports without having been selected because they mislead. A reporting system might favor vivid examples, simplify instructions, or maximize sales; whether its misleadingness explains its selection remains a further question. The chapter recognizes this in §3—“A signal selected without regard to accuracy is not thereby selected for inaccuracy”—but does not apply that distinction consistently to its own reconstruction.

The advertising example in §2 is especially vulnerable:

> “So is a company whose advertisement was selected from machine-generated drafts for the version that sold best, even though no human formed an intent to mislead.”

Selection for sales does not by itself establish selection for false impressions. The example needs a stipulated mechanism: the misleading impression contributes to sales, and the selection process favors advertisements because they produce that impression. Without it, the chapter treats an incidental effect as a function.

Likewise, “maps optimized to reflect decision-relevant aspects of territory” and “maps optimized to control other agents’ decisions” are not mutually exclusive. An accurate warning can influence conduct precisely by improving the recipient’s understanding. Explain what additional condition makes influence deceptive.

The sentence “Taken together the posts make a substantive choice” conceals an interpretive synthesis. Does Davis explicitly abandon sender benefit, or is the study reconstructing a position from formulations with different purposes? The 2020 “Algorithmic Intent” passage already separates deception from resource-moving fraud, before the 2021 Martínez comment. The chronology needs precision.

**2. Specify the receiver, the benchmark, and the kind of error**

The receiver-relative reconstruction is promising, but “less accurate predictions” needs a comparison. Less accurate than before receiving the message? Than after direct observation? Than after an unfiltered report? A message can improve a listener’s beliefs while withholding information that would improve them further. That alone cannot make every useful summary deceptive.

The category-merging example particularly needs explanation. Combining “one” and “four” might destroy information while accurately communicating what remains. Check “Algorithms of Deception!” for how Audience interprets the merged category and where distortion enters. The chapter presently lets loss of resolution stand in for induced error.

Similarly, the reference listener develops from someone applying the same inference procedure as for direct observation into someone using “conventional meaning.” These are not evidently the same. Convention includes expectations about selection, exaggeration, context, and genre. State whether the reference listener represents an actual audience’s justified expectations, an ideal decoder, or a theoretical baseline fixed by a common-interest game. Otherwise the account can classify communication as deceptive by choosing an unusually credulous listener.

This also matters for counteradapted mimicry. If a signal remains deceptive after actual receivers cease being misled, the definition cannot require actual worsening of their predictions without qualification. Distinguish deceptive function, attempted deception, and successful deception.

**3. Disclosure does less work than the thesis requires**

The chapter repeatedly concludes that disclosure converts distortion into missing information:

> “By the same reasoning a community could limit social punishment by a known policy of leaving certain negative facts unpublished. Its map would lose information without being biased.”

This is true under demanding assumptions about the policy, its implementation, and the audience’s knowledge. Knowing that unfavorable facts are withheld may permit a rational adjustment without revealing their prevalence, severity, or distribution. Different underlying populations can produce the same published record. A listener can recognize that the evidence is selected while remaining unable to estimate the relevant facts.

The distinction is between knowing that a filter exists and knowing enough about it to interpret its output. The chapter acknowledges cata’s objection but then returns to categorical formulations such as “disclosing the filter restores the listener’s map” and “a disclosed departure leaves [accuracy] intact.” Those conclusions exceed “At least one partial remedy,” the qualification attributed to Davis.

There is also a practical objection from Davis’s side: a disclaimer may fail to reach the audience that encounters the selected evidence, or may be less salient than that evidence. Disclosure in a later comment cannot automatically repair the impression made by an article. Conversely, a critic could object that disclosed selection remains misleading when the speaker can predict that ordinary recipients will not compensate adequately.

This correction should propagate to Chapters 4, 6, and 7 and the Conclusion. Their summaries make disclosure the central answer to part (c). That answer needs conditions of adequacy, not merely the presence of a disclaimer.

**4. Two formal claims need correction**

In §3, the discussion of “Stupidity and Dishonesty Explain Each Other Away” says:

> “Stupidity and dishonesty are independent causes of the common effect of someone’s being wrong, so conditioning on the effect makes them anticorrelated.”

The graph alone does not entail negative association. It permits dependence after conditioning; the sign and strength depend on the conditional probabilities. Explaining away is appropriate where either cause can substantially account for the observed effect and the interaction has the relevant form. Causes that operate synergistically need not behave that way.

For the same reason, “with only two parents, ruling out stupidity forces the inference to dishonesty” overstates matters unless the model makes those causes exhaustive. A conditional-probability specification can allow errors with neither parent present. The defensible criticism is that the framing encourages readers to treat two causes as exhaustive, not that the graph necessarily does so.

The reply to Ninety-Three also fails:

> “the claim holds for lies that are systematically biased, not random ones.”

Systematic bias need not destroy communication. If a speaker always exchanges two color names, a listener who knows the exchange can recover the information exactly. Other biased channels retain partial information. The relevant question is whether signals retain usable dependence on what they concern and whether receivers can learn that dependence. Neither the proportion of false sentences nor systematic bias alone settles it.

These are local corrections with wider importance: the chapter should not endorse formal conclusions more strongly than the models establish.

**5. Strengthen the attribution objection without making it an impossibility claim**

The chapter is right that incentive-compatible behavior does not establish the motive producing it. But its repeated appeal to “a text or a few exchanges” risks making sample size the decisive criterion. A single exchange can contain unusually discriminating evidence; a large archive can remain ambiguous. Selection history can also leave records, while dispositions may be hard to test without changing the situation being investigated.

A better standard asks which competing explanations the evidence discriminates among. Distinguish confidence that speech misled, confidence that the process favors misleading outputs, and confidence in a particular motive such as pandering. Those claims can warrant different confidence levels on the same evidence.

The “Higher Innocence” passage does not quite supply the counterfactual test attributed to it:

> “A functional attribution of motive is warranted, on this test, to the extent that one can show the behavior would have differed had the motive been absent.”

Replacing a rival with an ally does not remove a motive while holding everything else fixed. It can also change prior evidence about reliability, familiarity with the argument, or the expected value of checking. The asymmetry is a reason for scrutiny; its interpretation needs those alternatives examined. Moreover, some deceptive dispositions might operate equally toward allies and rivals. The test is neither necessary nor sufficient for deception generally.

The chapter is too soft on Davis when it says:

> “A standard that demands evidence of conscious intent will never convict either of them, and that is the point of their behavior.”

Repeatedly selective loss of control or convenient mishearing might provide circumstantial evidence of conscious intent. More fundamentally, the sentence assumes both optimized deniability and its purpose before demonstrating either. It reproduces the inference under review. The fair point is that requiring conscious intent excludes genuinely unconscious strategic behavior and may make some harmful patterns harder to challenge.

**6. Separate truth, vocabulary, and permission to accuse**

The analysis of “Algorithmic Intent” says the role rule disposes of acrimony “properly, since the social convenience of a description is no evidence of its truth.” That is only part of the argument.

Whether a particular attribution is true differs from whether a community should adopt a vocabulary, encourage public accusations, or impose sanctions. Social effects can bear on those institutional choices without becoming evidence about the truth of the attribution. The quotation itself concerns descriptions “socially harmful for a human community to adopt,” which leaves room for a policy question the chapter treats as merely epistemic.

The strongest charitable reading is that Davis refuses to falsify his beliefs for social convenience while leaving open constraints on publication and enforcement. The strongest criticism is that he moves from that defensible refusal to permission to deploy accusations under conditions where their epistemic and social effects are foreseeable. Determine which move “Algorithmic Intent” actually makes.

The Piper discussion should likewise distinguish weak evidence from no evidence. “Maximum a posteriori” identifies the highest-ranked hypothesis; it does not entail high confidence. One reply and its context can supply some evidence of a negotiating threat while failing to distinguish that hypothesis from a sincere warning. Also, two responses that fit an accusation do not establish that “every response” confirms it. The sharper question is what evidence Davis would accept as counting against his interpretation.

Because Piper’s position is available here only through Davis’s quotation, the chapter should state that limitation alongside its account of her claim.

**7. Do not discount concessions after recording them**

Several passages recognize qualifications and then recover a cleaner contrast than the evidence supports.

The opening says Davis named the account’s cost in 2020 and “declined to weigh” it. Yet §2 records evidential caution in “Maybe Lying Doesn’t Exist” in 2019, including reluctance to use “lie,” “fraud,” and “scam” where intent is hard to establish. The later conclusion that he answered the problem in 2020 with a role rule needs to acknowledge that earlier caution. The pattern may be inconsistent application of an existing standard, rather than a late discovery of the need for one.

Similarly:

> “A conscious liar keeps an accurate map that can in principle be recovered … a self-deceiver keeps no accurate map, and will often fold when shown the error.”

A liar need only believe the asserted claim false; that does not guarantee an accurate alternative account. Self-deception also need not yield readily to correction. The draft gives a useful possible contrast as a general psychological difference without evidence. Present the repair advantage as a hypothesis, conditional on mechanism.

The final paragraph on “Higher Innocence” is unfairly categorical:

> “It is unpublished … not a position he has stood behind in public.”

The brief expressly treats these drafts as publicly accessible. Draft status warrants caution about completion and endorsement; it does not establish privacy or absence of public commitment. Nor does the 2026 retrospective date establish that self-application began then. The chapter’s own 2019 examples already show self-suspicion. Check whether the browser incident received contemporary acknowledgment before claiming a seven-year delay in self-application.

**8. Scholarship should clarify the disputed distinctions**

The principal problem is insufficient direct engagement with work already central to the chapter. Before expanding the bibliography, read Artiga and Paternotte, Fallis and Lewis, Martínez, and the relevant Williams passages. “Philosophy of deception has moved toward a similar view” is too broad for a position represented here chiefly through one paper and Davis’s summaries.

The following are research suggestions from memory, not verified characterizations:

- **Lying and deception.** [memory] Roy Sorensen’s “Bald-Faced Lies! Lying Without the Intent to Deceive” and Don Fallis’s “What Is Lying?” challenge intent-to-deceive requirements within theories of lying. They would complicate the chapter’s identification of the ordinary concept with conscious deceptive intent. [memory] Thomas Carson’s *Lying and Deception* develops an account involving warranting truth; Seana Shiffrin’s *Speech Matters* connects truthfulness with the conditions of interpersonal communication. These provide alternatives to treating an accuracy function as the whole normative account.
- **Function attribution.** [memory] Larry Wright’s “Functions,” Robert Cummins’s “Functional Analysis,” and Ruth Millikan’s *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* distinguish explanatory projects currently grouped under “functional.” Millikan would be especially useful for asking how a signal can malfunction or misrepresent when its current effects differ from those explaining its historical function.
- **Evolution and signaling.** [memory] John Maynard Smith and David Harper’s *Animal Signals* distinguishes evolved signals from other informative features. This bears on whether every selected influence on a receiver counts as communication. [memory] William von Hippel and Robert Trivers’s “The Evolution and Psychology of Self-Deception” offers an evolutionary account of self-deception’s interpersonal benefits, directly relevant to the claim that maintaining two maps is costly. [memory] Lachmann, Számadó, and Bergstrom’s “Cost and Conflict in Animal Signals and Human Language” challenges simple identification of honest signaling with costly signals. Read it directly before using touchiness as a separating signal.
- **Motive attribution.** [memory] Dennett’s *The Intentional Stance* concerns a predictive explanatory strategy, not a straightforward inference to consciously represented motives. [memory] Davidson’s “Actions, Reasons, and Causes” and Anscombe’s *Intention* offer different approaches to explaining action through reasons. They would help distinguish predicting a pattern from identifying the agent’s reason for acting.

Williams particularly needs restraint. The current claim that Davis’s criterion “belongs with Sincerity” overlooks the possibility of sincerely transmitting a badly formed belief. A functional standard can assess both belief formation and expression. The contrast between trust as Williams’s “end” and trust as Davis’s “model of the filter” also compares a purpose with a mechanism; both descriptions could hold together.

**9. Structure, overlap, and prose**

The chapter largely respects its planned scope, but §3 becomes a sequence of short post reviews. Organize the consequences around three questions: how true statements mislead, what rules against lying accomplish, and what sustains informative communication. “Bounded Distrust” currently receives too little analysis to justify its place.

Move most of the Constitution paragraph to Chapter 11. Its detailed wording and the compassionate-framing example are duplicated there according to the summary. Retain a brief positionality acknowledgment if needed. Keep only the conceptual relevance of “Speaking Truth to Power”; Chapter 4 owns the coordination argument and Chapter 6 owns community policy.

Several sentences produce the verdict-heavy cadence the brief cautions against: “The theory is defensible”; “The graph is correct as far as it goes”; “Alexander’s objection stands”; “Davis’s partial answers.” Replace broad endorsements with the precise proposition established. “The best reply,” “the strongest objection,” and “the best criterion anyone offered” also need stated grounds or less competitive phrasing.

The opening paragraph and §1 repeat almost the same assessment. The conclusion repeats it again without resolving the distinctions introduced between them. Use the saved space for the functional account’s conditions. Explain “part (c)” once in ordinary terms before using the label.

**Proposed replacement for §3, paragraph beginning “The post’s remedy follows from its first half”**

*Problem addressed: disclosure is treated as sufficient repair despite acknowledged limits.*

> Disclosure changes how a report should be interpreted. In Davis’s coin example, specifying the reporting rule supplies enough information to reverse the apparent evidential force of the report. A general disclaimer about avoiding controversial subjects supplies much less. Readers may recognize that the published record is selected without knowing what the omitted evidence would show. Davis calls disclosure a partial remedy, and cata’s objection identifies its limit: knowledge of selection does not necessarily provide the information needed to compensate for it.

**Proposed replacement for §5, paragraph beginning “What a behavior is optimized for”**

*Problem addressed: the draft conflates levels of attribution and overemphasizes sample size.*

> Evidence that a statement misleads does not by itself establish why it was produced. Further evidence may show that a reporting process favors misleading outputs, or that a speaker changes standards when the interests at stake change. Identifying a particular motive requires another comparison: does the evidence support that explanation more strongly than confusion, competing commitments, or a different understanding of the question? An account that dispenses with conscious intent still needs evidence for the function it attributes.

**Proposed replacement for the closing paragraph on “Higher Innocence”**

*Problem addressed: draft status and retrospective chronology are made to carry unsupported conclusions.*

> The publicly accessible draft states the counterfactual comparison with unusual clarity and applies it to Davis’s own conduct. Its unfinished status limits what can be inferred about his settled formulation. Its retrospective date also leaves open when he first accepted the criticism. Earlier passages already show him questioning whether his examples and assumptions favored conclusions he wanted. The issue is how consistently he applies that scrutiny when interpreting another person’s speech.
