## Overall judgment

This is a careful and mostly fair chapter, and its best work is in the checking: the four-figure paragraph from "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea," with the Nuttbrock slip caught and the androphilic rates restored; the Lawrence Table VI point; the observation that Davis's 2019 "~5% (?)" figure links to a source measuring something else; the USTS arithmetic. The five-claim structure works, and the separation of Davis's stated position from his polemics is the right organizing judgment. Four problems need fixing. (1) The verdict on (d) as "the best supported of the five" is stronger than the evidence the chapter itself presents. That evidence is one structural study, one review coauthored by a proponent, two pooled studies that do not split by orientation, and a Haskell datum whose reading is reversed without the calculation that would justify it. The chapter also says the imaging evidence "finds no feminization" a paragraph after reporting two functional studies that found female-like responses. (2) The chapter never asks whether the gap it finds, between a defensible stated position and an indefensible polemical one, is the motte-and-bailey pattern tailcalled charged Davis with in 2021. It is the obvious objection to the chapter's own conclusion, and the chapter calls the gap "to his credit" without addressing it. (3) Several arguments are logically loose: the dependence claims in §1 contradict §2's account of the 2022 model; the "one-sided" measurement-invariance charge treats a within-group validity problem as an invariance problem; the "under modern conditions" argument in §6 claims Davis's demand-curve model predicts a falling autogynephilic share when it plausibly predicts the reverse. (4) Several core texts in the plan are missing, among them "Revised Taxonomy" (2017), "Reply to Ozymandias on Two-Type MtF Taxonomy," "Falsifiability and What We Expect Not to See," "Book Endorsement: *Autoheterosexual*" and "Janet Mock on Late Transitioners." So are several external sources the plan required (Moser 2010, Veale et al. 2008, the referral-shift papers, the short ROGD note). Without "Revised Taxonomy" the chapter's trajectory, which jumps from 2016 to 2018 to 2021, may misdate when Davis first revised his typology. The prose is plain and mostly good. Its main habit is a verdict scorecard stated three times, in the lede, at the end of each section, and in the conclusion, plus a scattering of short verdict sentences.

## 1. Weak or overstated arguments

### 1.1 The dependence structure in §1 is wrong, and contradicts §2

§1 says: "The claims are logically independent in one direction only. (c) requires (a) and (b); (e) requires (a), (b) and some version of (c) or (d); but (d) can be true while (c) is false."

- **(c) does not require (a)** in the sense §1 defines (a), as two groups. Autogynephilia can cause dysphoria in a population that is continuous on every variable. §2 shows Davis taking exactly this position in 2022: "Useful Approximation" *replaces the two types* with a threshold model and keeps the causal arrow. If (c) required (a), the 2022 model would be incoherent, and the chapter calls it "an improvement in kind." What (c) requires is (b), within-group prevalence, plus a causal premise.
- **(c) can be true while (d) is false.** Suppose some feminized neural feature makes autogynephilic arousal more likely, which is one way of reading the Guillamon body-perception finding. Then autogynephilia could be a proximate cause of the wish while the group still has a mild "intersex" feature upstream. So (c) and (d) are independent in *both* directions, and "independent in one direction only" is false.
- **(e) does not require (d)** on the weak reading the chapter adopts in §6 (non-androphilic and with some history of autogynephilic arousal). (d) is needed only for the categorization argument of Ch. 4. §1 should say that the negative claim is "load-bearing" *for the argument about the category woman*. As written, "load-bearing" is unexplained.

This matters beyond tidiness. The chapter's later handling of the 2022 model, and its ranking of (d), both depend on getting the dependencies right. See Proposed prose P2.

### 1.2 The verdict on (d) is overstated

The chapter ranks (d) "the best supported of the five" (§5, §9, and implicitly the lede). Take the evidence it presents for the distinctive part of the claim, that the *non-androphilic* group is not feminized:

- Zucker, Lawrence & Kreukels 2016: a review whose coauthor is the theory's leading proponent. The chapter flags the authorship but uses the review as primary support anyway.
- Guillamon et al. 2016: found "only one study of untreated non-homosexual trans women" (Savic & Arver 2011, per the summary), and said "more independent studies on nonhomosexual MtFs are needed."
- Frigerio 2021 and ENIGMA 2021: "Neither separated trans women by sexual orientation, so neither can confirm the distinction Davis cares about."
- tailcalled's summary: small studies, many comparisons, and again not split by orientation in the quoted part.
- The Haskell datum (see 1.3).

On the claim as distinctive to the late-onset group, this is one primary structural study plus a proponent-coauthored review. That is thinner than the support for (a) (several clinic samples across decades, conceded by Serano) or for within-group (b) ("high in every sample that has measured it"). The ranking seems to come from two things other than evidence:

- **The claim's weakness.** "Not an intersex condition, or at most, a very mild one" is hard to refute because it is hedged. "Best supported" and "least falsifiable" are being run together. The chapter half-notices this ("negative claims are hard to establish"; "What would make late-onset dysphoria an intersex condition is not obvious") but does not draw the consequence: a claim whose falsifying condition is unspecified cannot be well supported in the ordinary sense. It can only be *not contradicted*.
- **Its distance from the polemics.** The chapter likes it because it is the stated rather than the polemical claim. That is a judgment about Davis's conduct, not about evidence.

There is also an internal contradiction. §5 paragraph 2 reports that the Zucker review "noted exceptions, female-like responses in nonhomosexual trans women in two functional studies." §5's verdict paragraph then says "the little imaging evidence that separates the groups finds no feminization." Both cannot stand. Either the functional studies are dismissed with a reason (small, task-specific, hormone status unclear) or the verdict says "mostly finds no feminization in structure, with two functional exceptions."

There is also an ambiguity the chapter should name. "Intersex" in medicine means a difference of sex development (DSD), and in that sense nobody informed claims trans women of either type are intersex. In that sense (d) is trivially true. In the "brain-sex" sense (Swaab's BSTc work, the "female brain in a male body" account), it is contested, and the evidence for and against concerns mostly mixed or androphilic samples. The chapter should say which sense it ranks. I suspect the honest verdict is: **(d) is not contradicted by the evidence that separates the groups, which is very little, and is consistent with the broader finding that trans women's brains before hormones are mostly male-typical with some distinctive features. It is not better supported than (a).** Proposed prose P5.

The ranking matters to the book. Ch. 4 (§8) handed the psychological strand of the category argument to this chapter and needs (d) in the form "male-typical in the conjunction of psychological traits." Ch. 3 answers with imaging plus the Haskell datum and does not cite any study of trans women's pre-transition *interests or personality*. Imaging is not psychology. See 4.1 and 5.3.

### 1.3 The Haskell datum is reversed without the calculation

"Spencer meant it as a criticism, but it is the evidence: trans women in a male-dominated field appear at rates set by the male population, which is what one expects if their occupational interests are male-typical."

To say trans women appear "at rates set by the male population," one has to compare the share of trans women among male-born Haskell respondents with their share among male-born people generally. The chapter gives neither. On the figures it does give (about 3% of respondents women, half of them trans), trans women are roughly 1.5% of respondents and perhaps 1.5% of male-born respondents. Population estimates of trans women among natal males in the US are, from memory, well under 1% (low confidence on the exact figure; it would need a source). If so, trans women are *over-represented* among male-born Haskell programmers by a multiple. That is a known pattern in software and online communities, and it needs explaining by *some* theory: selection into online surveys, autism comorbidity (a literature the study does not touch), community acceptance, or Davis's own reading. Over-representation is not "rates set by the male population." What the datum does show is that trans women are not under-represented in the way cis women are. That is weak evidence against female-typical occupational interests, and it is confounded by socialization and opportunity before transition, which every side expects to be male-typical. Spencer's point, that the 51% figure is uninformative as stated, is closer to right than the chapter allows. Proposed prose P4.

### 1.4 The one-sided measurement-invariance charge conflates two problems

§3 credits Davis's argument against Alexander's survey as a measurement-invariance argument: the same item may mean different things in different groups (women vs. autogynephilic men). It then says he "applied it to one side," because he took the r/MtF poll ("(or similar)") literally.

The r/MtF poll is a *within-group prevalence estimate*. Its problem is construct validity (does "gender/body swap/transformation 'fetish' (or similar)" measure autogynephilia as Blanchard defined it?) and sample selection. It is not measurement invariance, which concerns comparisons *across* groups. Davis's exemption sentence ("my skepticism is specifically about these mass survey questions where we haven't done the extra work to try to figure out whether the question means the same thing to everyone") is phrased in invariance terms, so there is a real tension. But a defender can say that the item's meaning only needs to be stable within one population for a prevalence estimate, and that a loose item *over*-includes in a predictable direction. The charge of one-sidedness survives in a narrower form, and the narrower form is still damaging:

(i) the poll's loose wording and self-selected sample are exactly the validity problems Davis would raise against a poll that went the other way; and
(ii) when Davis uses the poll to support "guys like me" (same phenomenon as mine), he *is* making a cross-group comparison of meaning, between his experience and theirs, and the invariance worry applies in full.

The chapter should make the charge in that form. Proposed prose P3.

The sentence "a prior cannot be offered to readers who do not share it as a reason to read one side's survey literally and the other's skeptically" is also slightly off. In Bayesian terms a prior does license weighting ambiguous evidence differently. What it cannot do is make the evidence *persuade* someone without the prior, and Davis's disclosure ("sounding weirdly overconfident on non-empirical grounds") concedes exactly that. The fault, if any, is presenting a prior-weighted reading in a document aimed at persuading people who lack the prior. Say that.

### 1.5 The Serano order-of-onset argument via Lawrence Table VI is weaker than presented

The chapter says the second half of Serano's objection (fantasies come *after* dysphoria) "has support" in Lawrence's Table VI: mean age of first wish to change sex 9.8 and 8.0 in the gynephilic groups. It then compares this with Davis's own account of puberty onset ("If the erotic fantasy begins near puberty, as it did in Davis's own account ... a wish reported from age eight or ten precedes it").

- **A sample mean is compared with one individual.** To show the wish precedes the fantasy *in this sample*, one needs the same sample's age of first autogynephilic arousal, or at least of first erotic cross-dressing. Does Lawrence 2005 report that? If it does, use it. If it does not, the inference is invalid as stated.
- **Means do not establish order within persons.** A mean of 9.8 is compatible with most individuals' wish following arousal at 12–13 if the distribution is skewed by a minority of early reports.
- **Recall.** These are retrospective reports from a post-surgical sample. Ch. 2 (per its summary) made a point of how *Davis's* order-of-onset account is a 2021 recollection and protected from revision less than his early documents. The same discount applies here, and proponents (Blanchard, from memory) have argued that such recollections shift toward the expected narrative. The chapter should apply its Ch. 2 standard symmetrically.
- **Inconsistency with Ch. 2.** Ch. 2 holds that Davis's order of onset is weak evidence (a 2021 recollection). Ch. 3 uses his puberty onset as the benchmark for when autogynephilic fantasy begins. Choose one.

Lower the conclusion to: Table VI shows that childhood wishes to change sex are reported in the gynephilic groups at ages that, *if accurate and if the fantasy typically begins near puberty*, would precede it; this is the strongest piece of evidence on Serano's side in Davis's own chosen sources, and it is retrospective. Also, the closing antithesis ("harder to test, not easier to believe") reads as a debating point. The proponents' reply (a childhood precursor of the orientation) is the same kind of move as Serano's (gender identity as precursor). Both make their theory harder to test, and the chapter should say so of both.

### 1.6 The 2019 "~5% (?)" comparison cuts both ways and the chapter implies it cuts one

The chapter says Långström & Zucker's 2.8% is "a measure narrower in kind, arousal from cross-dressing, and set at a far lower threshold than intensity, a single episode." These two differences push in *opposite* directions. A narrower kind makes the true autogynephilia rate higher than 2.8%; a lower threshold makes the "intense" rate lower than 2.8%. The source therefore does not show Davis's figure is too high, only that it does not support it. The Choir draft's "about 3%" is "closer to the source" numerically but says "to some extent," a different threshold again. Say plainly that the source neither supports nor refutes "~5% of men with intense AGP," and that the figure Davis gave was unsupported by its link. That is the real finding, and it is enough.

### 1.7 §6's "under modern conditions" argument misreads Davis's model

"Davis's own model predicts that the composition should change ... If culture contributes more to transition when transition is common, the share of trans women for whom autogynephilia is the main cause should fall."

The demand-curve post, as the chapter summarizes it, says that as transition becomes cheaper, more of the *mildly dysphoric* transition on the margin. Who are the marginal transitioners? On the theory Davis holds, androphilic feminine males face low costs of transition (they pass well, per Smith's appearance finding) and transitioned even when it was costly. Non-androphilic males face high costs (they pass less well, are often married, transition later). Lowering costs should therefore bring in *disproportionately more* of the non-androphilic group, raising the autogynephilic share. That is, from memory, a common argument among the theory's proponents (Blanchard, Bailey) for why the share of non-homosexual transitioners rose over the 20th century. Low-moderate confidence on attribution; it should be checked or presented as the study's inference. The cultural arrow pushes the other way. So Davis's model predicts *change* with an ambiguous sign, and the chapter's inference to a *fall* needs the further premise that the cultural arrow dominates.

The empirical evidence the chapter has, Nuttbrock's cohort finding (younger participants more often androphilic and less often reporting transvestic fetishism), does point to a fall, at least in a New York community sample. Present it as the evidence and drop the claim that Davis's model predicts it. Proposed prose P6. Also check: the USTS sample is overwhelmingly young and *non*-androphilic (§6's own figures), which is in tension with Nuttbrock's "younger = more androphilic" in a different population. The chapter does not notice this tension and should.

### 1.8 The lede and §4 overstate the 2021 concession

The lede says: "The causal claim has not been shown, and Davis said so in 2021 before asserting it again." On the chapter's own account, the 2021 concession was that the *inference from correlation to taxonomy* was wrong ("this is just wrong!"; "lazy and wrong"). Davis kept his conclusion on stated grounds ("my own parsimony intuitions applied to everything I've read and seen"). Withdrawing one argument while holding the conclusion on disclosed grounds is not "saying the causal claim has not been shown." It is closer to "saying this argument doesn't show it." Rephrase. This also bears on §8's charge against "Point Man" (see 2.3).

### 1.9 §8's "Mostly they did not" rests on two texts

The section asks whether the polemics tracked the concessions. Its evidence: "Point Man" (2021, five weeks after the concession), "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea" (2023), "Social Construction" (2019), and the Murray review (2020). The 2019 and 2020 texts *predate* the concessions and cannot show failure to track them. The Murray item is in any case praise ("held a popularizer to the standard"), sitting under a topic sentence about overstatement. That leaves two texts, and the conclusion generalizes to "The polemics kept the confidence of 2016" and "his wider argument was usually conducted as if the strong reading were established." "Usually" and "kept" need more than two texts. Check the post-2022 polemical writing in the plan's core list ("Janet Mock on Late Transitioners," the *Autoheterosexual* endorsement, the Illy transcript's polemical passages, anything from 2024–25 in the gender blog). Then either widen the evidence or narrow the claim to "the two major polemical texts after the concession."

The "−18.012 (just worse than chance)" result in the ITT post is more useful than the chapter makes it. With 11 of 18 correct but a log score worse than chance, Davis's *confident* predictions about which impersonator was which were wrong. That is direct, self-published evidence that he modeled his opponents worse than he thought he did, in 2017. It belongs in §8 as evidence on the polemics' confidence about opponents' minds, more than as a footnote on the Bailey controversy.

### 1.10 Smaller points

- **"Nobody informed disputes that sexual orientation, childhood femininity, age at transition and a history of erotic cross-dressing are correlated among trans women."** Too strong as worded. Nuttbrock's cohort result (the association weakening in younger cohorts) and, from memory, Veale et al. 2008 complicate it. Say "the correlations are not seriously disputed in the samples that established them" or similar.
- **"In every clinical, surgical and community sample that has asked, a large majority of non-androphilic natal males ... report some history."** Smith's 64% is a majority but "large majority" is a stretch for it. The Veale 2014 online sample is not reported at all. Did it measure autogynephilia frequency? If so, give it; if the chapter's claim excludes it, say why.
- **The (b) verdict depends on the measure.** "Some history" (Lawrence's 170/189) is a lenient criterion. The chapter notes intensity and centrality are unmeasured, which is good, but the lede's "high in every sample" should carry the qualifier.
- **"The error runs against Davis's own case and changes nothing."** Fine, but say why it runs against him (73.2% is higher than 69%) for a reader who has not done the arithmetic.
- **The unpublished 2025 draft is counted as a "public concession" in §9** ("His writing reached that position through public concessions, several prompted ... and one, the unpublished 2025 draft"). An unpublished, possibly unsent draft is not a public concession. The draft is publicly *visible* via Gitweb, but it was not published as a concession. Fix the wording, and weigh private self-criticism less than published self-criticism when assessing his conduct.

## 2. Fairness to Davis

### 2.1 Places the chapter is unfair or ungenerous

- **Mahalanobis D.** "The blog reports the larger one without saying that it chose." Del Giudice et al. headlined the latent D ≈ 2.7 themselves. More to the point, Davis's 2019 effect-size post argues that observed effect sizes *underestimate* true ones because of measurement error and irrelevant variance. The latent estimate is the error-corrected one. So his choice follows from a methodological position he published, one the chapter calls "elementary and correct" in the very next paragraph. One can criticize the latent estimate on other grounds: from memory, critics argued that multigroup latent modeling and aggregating many small differences inflate D, and that D is sensitive to which traits are included. Moderate confidence on the existence of these critiques; check before citing. But the "without saying that it chose" charge is weak when the reasoning for the choice is on the same blog. Proposed prose P9.
- **Priority on the univariate fallacy.** "Replies by Marco Del Giudice and colleagues, and by another group ... made Davis's point in the same journal." The replies were 2016; Davis's post is 2019. They made the point first. Phrase it as Davis making their point, and check whether "The Univariate Fallacy" cites them. If it does, credit that; if not, note it neutrally.
- **"Point Man" as failure to track.** Given 1.8, telling the causal story in a historical-narrative post after withdrawing *one argument* while keeping the conclusion is consistent with his stated beliefs. The fair complaint is that it presents a belief held on parsimony intuitions without signaling that status. That is a failure of the norm of accounting (the reader is not told what the conclusion rests on). It is not inconsistency. The chapter's own thesis frame gives it the right word; use it.
- **"How dumb do you ... think we ... are?"** The chapter reads it as "an inference from disagreement to bad faith." It may be a complaint about *implausible public messaging* (that gender-identity theories ask the public to disbelieve what it can see). That is an accusation of bad argument, not of knowing pretense. Check the surrounding paragraph before filing it under Ch. 5's attribution problem. The T-shirt's "YOU LYING BASTARDS" (2016) is a clearer case of the move, and the chapter quotes it in §1 without using it in §8.

### 2.2 Concession acknowledged and then partly ignored

§6 lists the qualifications inside "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea" itself: the "not otherwise specified" residual, the internalized-misandry pathway, "I don't have any proof with which to accuse her of lying," "I believe her." §6 concludes "The essay states the strong reading and supports the weak one." §9 then says the claim "he stated most confidently in polemic" is "that most trans women are men like him," and that "his wider argument was usually conducted as if the strong reading were established." The essay that states the strong reading *contains its own qualifications*. That cuts against the conclusion's picture of a decade of polemics conducted as if the strong reading held. At minimum the conclusion should say that the main polemical essay carries its qualifications on its face. The question is then whether readers were entitled to take the headline sentence as the claim. Also, "essentially" in "essentially, guys like me" is a hedge the chapter does not weigh: does it mean "same type" (weak) or "same inner life" (strong)? Ch. 2 argued the latter (per summary). Ch. 3 should cite that argument back explicitly rather than assuming it.

### 2.3 Places the chapter is too soft on Davis

- **The motte-and-bailey question is not asked.** This is the largest omission. The chapter's own summary of Davis's position has this structure: a narrow, hedged, hard-to-refute claim (the "Empirical Claim," "or at most, a very mild one") put forward when commenters ask for his object-level thesis, and a broad, confident claim ("guys like me"; "yes, obviously"; "LYING BASTARDS") in the polemics. tailcalled's 2021 complaint, which the chapter reports and which Davis conceded, was *exactly* a motte-and-bailey charge, against the 2016 reply. The chapter's conclusion says "That the stated position is the most defensible one is to his credit." A critic will say the stated position is the motte, so of course it is the most defensible, and that the credit is misplaced. The chapter must engage this. There is evidence on both sides, all already in the chapter: the qualifications sit inside the same polemical documents (against a strict motte-and-bailey, which requires the bailey to be advanced *without* the motte's qualifications); the concessions are public and the 2022 model is a genuine revision (against); the 2024 Empirical Claim was produced on request in a thread about something else, and Davis did not revise "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea" or other polemics to match it (for). The verdict may well be that it is not a motte-and-bailey in the strict sense but has the same *effect* on readers. That needs arguing, not assuming. Proposed prose P7.
- **"The concession and the reinterpretation are both characteristic."** The KartoffelElf exchange is: Davis concedes he should have searched, then argues the women meant something else. That is a pattern worth naming critically. The concession is about method, the conclusion is retained by reinterpreting the counter-evidence, and the reinterpretation is not tested either. The chapter should say whether the reinterpretation was any better grounded than the original "I'm inclined to doubt it." As written, "characteristic" sounds like praise for both halves.
- **The 2025 draft is called among "the best things in the sexological writing."** It may be. But it is unpublished, its sending is unrecorded, and the objection it makes is the one Serano, Veale and tailcalled had made publicly two or more years earlier. Credit is due for applying it to his own side. It is not originality, and the chapter's phrasing ("makes, against the researchers he most admires, the objection Serano, Veale and tailcalled made") already shows this. The conclusion's weight on it should drop accordingly.
- **The Lawrence 2005 Table VI point is used to fault Davis ("Davis did not discuss in the table he himself chose")**, which is fair. But the chapter does not apply the same test to the critics' sources. Did Serano 2020 discuss the §3 frequencies that contradict her first half? If not, say so in the same breath. That is a fairness-to-Davis point by way of symmetry.

## 3. Fairness to the critics and to the literature

- **Serano.** The concession quotation is accurate as far as the chapter shows. But her *positive* model, female embodiment fantasies as a consequence of gender identity together with sexual embodiment, is named only in passing ("her 'female embodiment fantasies'"). The chapter's verdict on (c) is "not established, nor its rival." The reader cannot judge the second half without being told what the rival claims and what evidence Serano offers for it. A paragraph is needed, along with Veale's identity-defense model (Veale, Clarke & Lomax 2010, from memory, moderate confidence), which is the other developed rival. Without them, "nor its rival" is an assertion.
- **Veale.** The taxometric study gets one sentence of result. Lawrence's reply gets a longer paraphrase plus a quotation and in effect the last word. Did Veale respond to Lawrence 2014? I am not sure (low confidence either way). Check, and if there is a reply, give it. More substantively, Lawrence's objection has a corollary the chapter misses. If Veale's online sample was ≥94% non-androphilic, the dimensional finding is evidence about structure *within* the non-androphilic group: autogynephilia there looks continuous, not a taxon. That bears on whether "the autogynephilic type" is a kind, which is part of (a). The chapter uses the same sample for the population estimate in §6, so it should use it consistently.
- **Moser.** Only Moser 2009 appears, through its numbers and Lawrence's reply. Moser 2010, "Blanchard's Autogynephilia Theory: A Critique" (*Journal of Homosexuality* 57, from memory, high confidence), is, with Serano's papers, the main academic critique of the theory as a whole, and the plan lists it. It should be represented. Also, Lawrence's objection to Moser 2009 is not only an instrument complaint. It raises a *construct* problem: "arousal at the thought of being a woman" has no clear analogue for someone who is a woman. That makes the women-comparison half of (b) partly conceptual, not merely an instrument dispute. "Each side's instruments are disputed by the other" understates this. The comparison cannot be settled by better sampling alone until the construct is defined for women, and both Davis's "No Way No Chance" prior and Moser's adaptation take a side on that definition. This is a missing objection to *both* sides and the most interesting point available in that section.
- **tailcalled.** Treated well and at length. One caution: the chapter calls him "the most careful sympathetic critic the theory has had," "the best criticism of the causal claim from inside the camp," and has him draw "the right moral." Three approving characterizations of one commenter, when he is the chapter's main source of criticism, risk making him the chapter's proxy. State what he argued and let the reader see it is good.
- **Alexander.** The 2020 survey is fairly described, and the chapter notes he listed its weaknesses himself, which is good. The chapter should also say whether Alexander answered Davis's 2023 reply. If he did not, say so in a phrase: the brief asks that reception be described accurately, including absence.
- **Bailey and Lawrence.** The chapter applies an authorship discount to the Zucker review (Lawrence coauthor; Zucker chaired the DSM work group, from memory). It then calls Guillamon's group "independent" and applies no comparable discount to Serano or Veale, who are also invested parties. Either authorship is relevant or it is not. The better course, consistent with the brief, is to evaluate the reviews by the studies they summarize. On the brain question that means saying which primary studies the Zucker review relied on for "not in nonhomosexual MtFs" (likely the same Savic & Arver 2011, which would make Zucker and Guillamon one study counted twice). Check this. If true, it is the most important fact about the evidence for (d).
- **Dreger.** Dreger is quoted directly on the activists ("worked to try to ruin Bailey") and her conclusion is reported. The other side is represented only by "contested in the many commentaries ... (this from memory; the commentaries were not read for this study)." That is uneven, even with the label. The brief requires seeking the other parties' accounts where a dispute is characterized, and Conway's and James's public accounts exist online, as do several of the 2008 *Archives* commentaries (some open access, from memory, low confidence). Either fetch one or two, or cut the Dreger quotation to a neutral description of what she investigated and concluded. Also, "A literature produced under that pressure has an obvious incentive structure on both sides" is too vague to carry weight. Say what the incentive is (proponents to publish confirming studies with convenient samples; critics likewise) or drop it.

### 4.1 Sexology and adjacent

- **Moser 2010**, "Blanchard's Autogynephilia Theory: A Critique," *J. Homosexuality* 57(6). High confidence it exists and is a general critique. Plan-required.
- **Veale, Clarke & Lomax 2008**, "Sexuality of Male-to-Female Transsexuals," *Archives of Sexual Behavior* 37. Moderate confidence on venue and year. Found autogynephilia-related measures among trans women in a pattern not fully matching the typology. Plan-required.

- **Blanchard's work on misreporting**: Blanchard, Clemmensen & Steiner 1985, on social desirability and distorted self-report in gender patients. Moderate confidence. Proponents argue non-homosexual patients misreport as androphilic or non-autogynephilic. This bears on §6's use of USTS self-labels (it would push toward Davis's first quantity) and on §3's androphilic rates (it would make them partly misclassified non-androphiles). The chapter should state the proponents' best response to the "exceptions" before declaring the strict theory refuted by the 15–40% androphilic rates. Fairness to Davis's side.
- **Stone 1987**, "The Empire Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto." High confidence it exists and argues that patients learned to present the narrative clinicians required. Self-report under gatekeeping cuts *both* ways: patients concealing erotic cross-dressing to qualify, which supports proponents, and narratives shaped to fit the clinic's model, which supports critics. A paragraph on this would strengthen §3's "None is a random sample." Hacking's looping effects (Ch. 4 uses Hacking) apply to the typology itself: once a typology is used for gatekeeping, it shapes the reports that test it.
- **Brain-sex literature that (d) denies**: Zhou et al. 1995 and Kruijver et al. 2000 (BSTc, postmortem, mostly hormone-treated); Rametti et al. 2011 (DTI, untreated MtF, white matter partly feminized); Kranz et al. 2014. High confidence on Zhou/Kruijver, moderate on Rametti and Kranz. The chapter should say what the "intersex" hypothesis rested on historically and that most of it came from samples not split by orientation, or hormone-treated ones. Otherwise the reader does not know what (d) is denying.
- **Twin and genetic studies**: Heylens et al. 2012 (twin concordance for gender identity disorder); Foreman et al. 2019 (sex-hormone-signaling gene variants in trans women). Moderate confidence on both. These bear on (c)/(d) as biological-cause evidence, mostly not split by orientation, and their absence is noticeable in a chapter assessing a claim about biological cause.
- **Studies of trans women's personality and interests**: Lippa 2001 (gender diagnosticity in transsexuals; *Archives of Sexual Behavior*). Moderate-low confidence on details. This is what Ch. 4 needed for the psychological strand, and imaging cannot supply it.
- **Proponents' later work**: Lawrence 2013, *Men Trapped in Men's Bodies* (Springer). High confidence. The book-length statement of the theory; strange to omit in a chapter on the theory's literature, even if only to say what it adds. Hsu, Rosenthal & Bailey 2015 on the psychometric structure of autogynephilia items. Moderate confidence. Relevant to (b)'s instrument dispute.
- **Referral-shift papers** (Aitken et al. 2015; de Graaf et al. 2018; also Wiepjes et al. 2018 on the Amsterdam cohort, moderate confidence). The plan required them for (e). They mostly concern adolescents and the sex ratio, so their bearing on adult MtF composition is limited, but the chapter should say so rather than silently drop them. The plan's "short note" on ROGD is also missing.
- **Illy's *Autoheterosexual*** (2023). Davis endorsed it (a core text not used). It is the most recent popular statement of the position and bears on what "the theory" now is.

### 4.2 Psychometrics

- **Taxometrics.** Meehl's method; Ruscio, Haslam & Ruscio 2006 (textbook); Haslam, Holland & Kuppens 2012, quantitative review finding most psychological latent variables dimensional (*Psychological Medicine*). High confidence on Haslam et al. 2012. This gives a *prior* on discreteness that the chapter needs: dimensional results are the norm, so Veale's finding is unsurprising, and the burden is on the taxon claim. The methodological guidance on minimum taxon base rates (around 10%, from memory, moderate confidence) supports Lawrence's objection to Veale. Cite it on both counts. Latent class or finite-mixture modeling is the obvious alternative test, and the chapter could note that nobody has run it on a sample adequate to the question (if true; check tailcalled's work).
- **Measurement invariance.** Meredith 1993; Vandenberg & Lance 2000. High confidence. The chapter names the concept and should cite a standard source. Also DIF in item response theory, since Bailey & Hsu used scale items.
- **Collider / selection bias.** The chapter credits Davis's collider caveat. Berkson's paradox is the standard name, and Elwert & Winship 2014 a standard reference (moderate confidence). One sentence would place Davis's point in its literature, as the chapter does for invariance.

### 4.3 Philosophy of science of taxonomy

- Ch. 4 owns natural kinds, but this chapter makes a claim it should ground: that "separability in the aggregate is a fact about classification, not about whether the groups are discrete kinds." This is right, and it is the distinction between *discriminability* and *taxonicity* familiar from the taxometrics literature. Cite it there rather than leaving it as the chapter's own aside.
- **Psychiatric-kinds literature**: Kendler, Zachar & Craver 2011, "What kinds of things are psychiatric disorders?" (*Psychological Medicine*). Moderate confidence. Proposes mechanistic property-cluster kinds, which fits Davis's 2022 move from types to a causal model exactly. One sentence would show that his revision converges with a mainstream position in philosophy of psychiatry. That is credit to Davis, and it is in the plan's spirit (convergence with academic parallels).

## 6. Prose

The prose is mostly plain and exact. The habits that read as machine-made are concentrated in verdict sentences and closing antitheses.

**Scorecard structure.** The lede lists five verdicts in five sentences, then §9 lists them again. "The plan warns: no scorecard tone." Examples:
- Lede: "The causal claim has not been shown, and Davis said so in 2021 before asserting it again. The negative claim about brains is the best supported of the five and the one he put at the center when asked to state his position. The population claim goes beyond its evidence."
- §9: "The clusters exist and the theory's best-known critic concedes them. The arousal is common in one cluster in every sample that has looked."
- §5 end: "Of the five claims, (d) is the best supported ..."

**Short verdict sentences**, often closing a paragraph:
- "The pattern is real."
- "The model is an improvement in kind."
- "The selection deserves more notice."
- "Davis applied it to one side."
- "The datum is small, but it is a real one."
- "The polemics are where it lapses."
- "Mostly they did not."
- "That is a researcher's guess, offered as such."
- "The concession and the reinterpretation are both characteristic."

Not all need cutting. "Davis applied it to one side" works as a paragraph opener after a paragraph of credit. But the density is high enough to read as a tic. Fold most of them into the sentence before or after.

**Antithetical closers**, the "not X but Y" family:
- "The reply is available, but it makes the causal claim harder to test, not easier to believe."
- "The late-onset group is late in transitioning, not necessarily in wishing." (This one earns its place; keep.)
- "Those findings are neither an intersex condition in the sense the slogan means nor the absence of any biological difference."
- "separability in the aggregate is a fact about classification, not about whether the groups are discrete kinds" (substantive; keep, but cite; see 4.3)
- "Spencer meant it as a criticism, but it is the evidence" (also wrong on substance; see 1.3)

**Announcing**:
- "This chapter takes them one at a time, against the literature Davis cites and against sources outside his collection."
- "Whether he wrote as if it were is a separate question, taken up in §8."
- "The objections from the other side are of two kinds."
- "The causal claim, then, has not been established ..." ("then" as recap)

**Self-description and evaluative framing**:
- "The honest summary is that ..." (§2). Implies other summaries would be dishonest; say "The evidence supports ..." or just state it.
- "The most notable document here is unpublished." "Most notable" is an intensifier. "One document bearing on this is unpublished" or just begin with the draft.
- "which the T-shirt did not anticipate." A wry closer. It is in the chapter's voice and harmless, but it is the kind of quip that accumulates. Keep or cut by taste.

**Machinery**: "The figures Davis cites were checked against the papers; the results are reported where they bear on the argument." This is acceptable disclosure in a scholarly note, but it edges toward describing the project's procedure. A human scholar would write "I have checked..." or simply report the discrepancies as found. Suggest: "Where the figures Davis cites differ from the papers, the differences are noted." "as the texts could not be retrieved" is necessary disclosure; keep it.

**Unattributed commenter**: "A commenter pointed out that a web search finds the phrase mostly in cis women's writing." The works cited names KartoffelElf. The brief requires the display name in text.

**Sweeping opener**: the lede's first sentence, "which most trans writers reject," is unsourced. Either cite (Serano and Veale represent it) or soften to "which is rejected by most of the trans writers who have engaged it, Julia Serano foremost."

**Tension with Ch. 2 in the first sentence**: "Davis's gender writing rests on a body of sexology." Ch. 2 argues that the first-person record is "the evidential core of the gender writing." Say "draws on" or "is argued partly from" to avoid contradicting Ch. 2's thesis in the book's next chapter.

## 7. Proposed prose

Quotations below are copied from the chapter. The main agent should add the `data-src` markup and citations as in the existing text.

### P1. Replacement lede

> Davis's gender writing draws on a body of sexology associated with Ray Blanchard, J. Michael Bailey and Anne Lawrence, which he defended for a decade and which most of the trans writers who have engaged it, Julia Serano foremost, reject. The position he defended bundles five claims of different strength and different kinds of evidence: that male-to-female transsexuals fall into two clusters; that erotic arousal at the thought of being female is common in one of them and rare in women; that this arousal causes the wish to transition; that the late-onset cluster has no feminized brain; and that most trans women in Western countries today belong to that cluster. Davis's own position moved over the decade, through concessions made in public and mostly in reply to one sympathetic critic, toward a narrower statement than the one his polemics carried. The chapter assesses the five claims against the literature he cites and against sources outside his collection, and then asks what the distance between the narrow statement and the polemics amounts to.

(This keeps the question and structure and moves verdicts to §9. It still ends with a sentence about what the chapter does; the main agent may cut that last sentence if it reads as announcing, but a lede in this book's format seems to require something of the kind.)

### P2. Replacement for the first half of §1 paragraph 3 ("The claims are logically independent...")

> The claims depend on one another less than the polemics suggest. The causal claim needs the second, since a paraphilia cannot explain dysphoria in people who lack it, but it does not need two discrete types: autogynephilia could contribute to transition across a population that is continuous on every variable, and Davis's 2022 model, discussed in §2, says exactly that. The negative claim about brains is independent of the causal claim in both directions. The late-onset group's dysphoria might have some cause that is neither the paraphilia nor a feminized brain. Or a mild neural difference might be the reason the paraphilia arises, so that both a cause of the kind Davis believes in and an "intersex" feature of the kind he denies would be present. The population claim, on its weaker reading, needs only the first two. What the negative claim carries is the argument about the category *woman* that Chapter 4 examined, whose psychological strand requires that the late-onset group's traits be male-typical. Davis saw part of this early.

(Then continue with the existing 2018 quotations, and end the paragraph: "The structure of the position, with the causal claim as optional and the negative claim carrying the argument about categories, was in place by 2018.")

### P3. Replacement for §3 paragraph beginning "Davis applied it to one side."

> Davis did not apply it evenly. The same reply cites a Reddit poll in which 82 percent of respondents on a trans women's forum answered yes to a forced-choice question about a gender/body swap/transformation "fetish" (or similar), and concludes, "that makes me think it's a majority." Strictly, the poll raises a different problem from the one Davis had pressed against Alexander. It estimates a rate within one population, and measurement invariance concerns comparisons between populations. Its problems are validity and sampling: a loosely worded item, answered by the self-selected members of one forum. Those are the problems Davis would raise, and in the same reply does raise, against surveys whose results he doubts, and the exemption he gives himself is worded so as not to reach them: "my skepticism is specifically about these mass survey questions where we haven't done the extra work to try to figure out whether the question means the same thing to everyone." Once the poll is used to support the further claim that these women are, in his phrase, guys like him, it becomes a comparison between populations after all, between his experience and theirs, and his own objection applies to it in full.

(Then the existing material on the "No Way No Chance" prior, with the last sentence revised to:)

> A prior legitimately affects how its holder weighs ambiguous evidence. It cannot make that evidence persuasive to someone who does not hold it, which is what the disclosure concedes, and an essay addressed to such readers owes them the evidence without the prior.

### P4. Replacement for the Haskell paragraph (§5)

> The behavioral evidence is thin. Smith's Dutch study found large differences between the orientation groups in childhood cross-gender behavior and judged appearance and none in height. Davis's own contribution is a tally of the State of Haskell surveys, in which about 3 percent of respondents were women, cis and trans in roughly equal numbers. A commenter, Spencer, corrected his handling of non-responses, which Davis fixed and credited in the post, and objected that "observing that 51% of women are trans is not meaningful since trans people are 91% male." The objection is right about the figure as stated. What the survey does show is that trans women are not under-represented in this male-dominated field in the way cis women are. That is weakly consistent with male-typical occupational interests, though it is equally expected from male-typical socialization and opportunity before transition, which no party disputes. If anything, trans women appear to be over-represented among the male-born respondents relative to their share of the general population. That calls for an explanation of its own, whether selection into an online survey or something about the population, and none of the theories at issue was built to supply one.

(The main agent must compute the over-representation from the post's own figures and a sourced population estimate before keeping the last two sentences. If the calculation does not show over-representation, cut them.)

### P5. Replacement for the §5 verdict paragraph ("Of the five claims, (d) is the best supported...")

> In the form Davis gave it in 2018, the negative claim is not contradicted by the evidence that separates the groups, and that evidence is very little: one structural study of untreated non-androphilic trans women, summarized in two reviews, one of them coauthored by the theory's leading proponent. Two functional studies cited in that review found female-like responses in the group, and the review's own summary does not dismiss them. The pooled imaging studies that do not separate the groups describe trans women before hormones as mostly male-typical with features of their own. The qualification in Davis's 2018 sentence, "or at most, a very mild one," has turned out to be needed. The qualification also makes the claim hard to test, since Davis has not said what finding would count as more than mild. When adrusi, a late-onset trans woman without autogynephilic fantasies, pressed him in 2023, he drew the line at "a bone fide intersex condition that has no particular symptoms other than a mysterious desire to be female." No study has found such a condition, and none has been designed to look for it. The claim is better described as unrefuted than as established. It is more secure than the causal claim, because it asks for less, and less secure than the clustering, which several samples across several decades confirm.

(If Ch. 2 already quotes the adrusi sentence, replace the quotation with a cross-reference.)

### P6. Replacement for the second half of §6 paragraph 3 ("Davis's own model predicts that the composition should change...")

> What follows for the present is unclear, and Davis's own models do not settle it. "Lesser-Known Demand Curves" argued that as transition becomes less costly, more of the mildly dysphoric will transition on the margin. On the theory Davis holds, the costs of transition have historically fallen hardest on the late-onset group, which passes less easily and is more often married, so cheaper transition should bring in proportionally more of them. The 2022 graph's arrow for cultural factors points the other way, toward transitions for which autogynephilia is not the main reason. Which effect dominates is an empirical question. The one direct piece of evidence in the chapter's sources favors a fall in the autogynephilic share: Nuttbrock found that "Older participants were roughly twice as likely to report all measurements of transvestic fetishism," and that younger participants were more often androphilic. But that is a New York community sample recruited partly from streets and clubs, and the 2015 national survey, whose respondents were mostly young, shows a non-androphilic majority. The qualifier *under modern conditions* thus marks the period the old samples describe worst, and nothing measured since tells us which way they are wrong.

### P7. New paragraph for §8, after the thesis-frame paragraph (or at the start of §9)

> A critic could describe this result less kindly. The narrow statement Davis gave when asked for his object-level claim, that late-onset gender dysphoria in males is not an intersex condition, "or at most, a very mild one," is the easiest of the five to defend because it says the least. The polemical claims, that the taxonomy is approximately true ("yes, obviously") and that most trans women are guys like him, say much more. A position defended at its narrowest when challenged and advanced at its broadest when not is the pattern tailcalled named in 2021, and Davis conceded that charge then. The record does not fit the pattern exactly. The polemical essay carries its own qualifications (the residual category, the honest exception, "I believe her"), the concessions were made in public, and the 2022 model is a real revision, not a retreat to a slogan. But the effect on a reader is much the same. Someone who meets the headline sentence and the "How dumb ..." question is not told that the author's considered position is narrower. When the considered position narrowed, Davis did not go back and narrow the essays. The norm of accounting asks a writer to say what a conclusion rests on. The gender polemics met it in their qualifications and failed it in their emphasis.

### P8. Replacement for §9 paragraph 2

> The claim Davis put forward in 2024 as his empirical thesis is the narrowest of the five, and it is unrefuted, mostly because so little evidence bears on it. The claim he pressed hardest in polemic, that most trans women are men like him, is the least supported, because it rests on a causal story that has not been shown and on a resemblance of inner life that no survey has measured. Between the two lie the claims the literature does support: the clusters, and the frequency of autogynephilic arousal among non-androphilic trans women in every sample that has asked. That Davis's considered position ended close to what a reader of the literature, critics included, could defend is to his credit, and so are the concessions that brought it there. That he left his polemics where they were explains much of the hostility the theory met in his hands. The next chapter asks which parts of his philosophy of categories need these claims at all. It finds that only the psychological strand of the argument about *woman* does, and that strand needs exactly the question about male-typical traits this chapter could not close.

(The last sentence must be checked against Ch. 4 as written; I am relying on its summary.)

### P9. Replacement for the Mahalanobis sentences in §7

> Davis's use of Mahalanobis *D* shows how much turns on choices of this kind. He cited Del Giudice's personality figure of about 2.7, the estimate the paper itself headlines, computed on latent factors. The same paper reports that "The uncorrected multivariate effect size for observed scores was D = 1.49." The choice of the latent figure follows from the position Davis defended in his 2019 post on effect sizes, that observed scores understate true differences because measurement error inflates the variance. But a reader of "The Univariate Fallacy" is not told that a second figure exists or why the first was preferred. The latent estimate also depends on which traits are included and how the factors are modeled, which is a further reason to report both.

(The sentence about the effect-size post must come after that post has been introduced, or be reordered. Currently the effect-size post is discussed in the paragraph *after* the Mahalanobis sentences; move the Mahalanobis material below it.)
