# Review: Where to Draw the Boundaries

The chapter’s strongest achievement is its reconstruction of a position more qualified than Davis’s polemics suggest. The concessions are substantial, and the distinction between choosing predictive interests and assessing performance against them gives the chapter a coherent subject. Its principal weakness is that it does not apply the same scrutiny to its own proposed resolution. Disclosure becomes an almost costless remedy; two admissible categories become an exhaustive account of gender; and several mathematical examples acquire conclusions their assumptions do not establish.

The revision should preserve the reconstruction while narrowing these verdicts. This review assesses the packet alone. Suggestions about the contents of particular posts require checking against those sources; proposed scholarly connections from memory are marked accordingly.

## 1. The central argument needs further qualification

### Disclosure does not establish an exceptionless rule

The decisive passage in §8 is:

> “A disclosed departure hides nothing.”

This is too strong, and much of the book’s thesis now depends on it. Disclosing that a category has changed does not necessarily disclose its extension, its predictive losses, or the circumstances in which earlier inferences become unreliable. Even an exact disclosure may be forgotten, detached from subsequent uses, or too expensive for listeners to incorporate. A community can preserve information in an explanatory document while making that information harder to recover in ordinary communication.

Davis’s strongest answer is already available in the chapter: “More Schelling” concerns the effects on *all* the downstream uses of a word. An announced change may still impose those costs. Saying that message length measures the price does not establish that the price is negligible, that the relevant information remains accessible, or that the new convention is stable.

The inference to an exceptionless prohibition on concealment is also unsupported. Showing that disclosed departures are possible defeats the claim that every departure must be deceptive. It does not show that disclosure is sufficient for honesty in every case, or that disclosure must have no exceptions. Moreover, “whether a speaker has stated his reason is observable” substitutes the presence of an explanation for its adequacy. A strategic speaker can state an incomplete reason.

The chapter should present accounting as a strong candidate for an intermediate norm, then specify what successful accounting requires. That is enough to challenge Davis’s dichotomy. It need not claim to have solved the coordination problem.

**Proposed replacement for §8, paragraph beginning “The question is which rule…”**

*This addresses the unsupported passage from disclosure to an exceptionless norm.*

> Davis’s argument does not establish that every change of convention moves a community toward less accurate belief. Speakers can distinguish a revised use from the inferences licensed by an earlier one: a regulation can count dolphins among aquatic animals while retaining their classification as mammals. Whether such a distinction succeeds depends on its uptake. Listeners must understand which inferences remain warranted, and the qualifications must remain available when the expression circulates beyond its initial setting. Disclosure therefore supplies a possible intermediate norm between refusing revision and permitting concealed distortion. Its success is a question about communication and coordination, of the kind Davis’s own discussion of Schelling categories makes unavoidable.

### The redefinition dilemma is invalid as presented

In §4, someone who merges Foo and Quux but continues to predict coordinates 4 and 6 after observing a first coordinate of 2 supposedly “is not using the new concept.” That does not follow. A broad category can retain internal conditional structure. The merged category’s unconditional mean differs from its expected value conditional on an observed coordinate.

Davis’s quoted claim that the cluster knowledge must be represented “somewhere” is defensible. The chapter’s stronger claim that using the merged concept requires predicting the merged means is not. It assumes that the category label replaces all other available information.

The assessment repeats the problem:

> “Either a redefinition changes the redefiner’s predictions, for the worse, or it changes only his words while the old concept keeps working unnamed.”

There are further possibilities: a broader category supports different predictions; its internal structure remains explicitly represented; or it improves some predictions while worsening others. Check whether “Where to Draw the Boundaries?” expressly restricts the example to a particular decoder. If so, restore that restriction.

**Proposed replacement for §8, “The dilemma of redefinition”**

*This preserves the valid information-preservation argument without endorsing a false dilemma.*

> A broader label does not remove the need to represent distinctions relevant to prediction. If Foo includes both former groups, an observer can still use the first coordinate to predict the others, provided the model preserves that conditional information. Davis’s example establishes a burden on redefinition: the useful distinctions must remain recoverable somewhere. It does not establish that they must retain their previous names or remain separate categories at every level of description.

### Mathematical correctness is not adequacy of the model

“The distinction is correct and correctly calculated” conflates a philosophical distinction with computations illustrating it. The numerical verification is valuable, but the opening should distinguish verified arithmetic from the assumptions governing its interpretation.

In particular:

- The title-inflation example establishes reduced information about **pay** under its stipulated distribution. It does not establish that “all” the change accomplishes is making language less useful. Duties, organizational authority, recruitment, and social standing are excluded from the calculation. These are possible omitted purposes, not claims about the actual example.
- Squared-error loss rewards proximity in a specified representation. If the task were predicting parity, odd/even would become the relevant partition. The chapter acknowledges metric dependence but repeatedly returns to “compactness” as though it were an independent virtue.
- A deterministic reassignment of a fixed subset and random lying at the same aggregate rate need not generate the same conditional distribution of messages. Equal error rates are insufficient for equivalence. In §8, “behave identically” should require matching error patterns and receiver interpretations, not merely the same percentage.

The last point matters to signaling theory as well as to the arithmetic. “Unnatural Categories” may establish equivalence under a carefully specified construction; the chapter must reproduce that construction rather than generalize from its error rate.

## 2. Fairness to Davis and his interlocutors

### Alexander’s clarification is not clearly the concession claimed

After the 2019 editorial note, §2 says:

> “That is a long way from ‘not an error.’”

But the chapter has already credited Alexander with distinguishing badly drawn boundaries from false ones and rejecting plainly useless categories. His clarification may strengthen an existing qualification without conceding that definitions are epistemically false in Davis’s sense. The note says definitions have costs; that is compatible with his original border analogy.

Similarly, Alexander need not establish that *woman* is constituted by agreement to argue that the choice of a word’s extension should consider consequences. A convention can designate independently existing properties while its adoption remains a practical decision. The chapter needs to identify the exact invalid inference in “The Categories Were Made For Man, Not Man For The Categories,” rather than treating the difference between epistemic and other purposes as itself sufficient to establish invalidity.

“Everything Davis wrote on the subject can be read as an attempt…” is correspondingly too totalizing. The later program has independent concerns about compression, inference, causation, and coordination.

### Davis’s concessions should change the verdict

The chapter repeatedly acknowledges that Davis permits multiple legitimate classifications, then treats each renewed acknowledgment as a concession to his opponents. This culminates in:

> “The pattern recurs throughout the program. A critic says that several categories are legitimate and use picks among them, and Davis replies that this was already in the post. It usually was.”

If it usually was, the substantive disagreement needs sharper identification. Is it about unacknowledged costs, the existing meaning of a word, the preferred default, or whether a particular proposed revision actually preserves useful information? The chapter should not continue to adjudicate a generic pluralism dispute Davis has already granted.

Likewise, §6 says the capstone’s “theoretical conclusion is already the norm of accounting.” Its permission to deceive when consequences warrant it does not establish the study’s preferred remedy of disclosure to the listener. Recognizing oneself as deceiving is different from telling one’s audience how to correct for it. The chapter needs to separate:

1. A functional classification of conduct as deceptive.
2. Permission to engage in that conduct.
3. A requirement to disclose the conduct or its costs.

The supplied quotations support the first two more directly than the third.

### The chapter is too soft on the pronoun argument

Section 8 endorses:

> “The redefinition is wanted for the meaning it says it lacks.”

This trades on “meaning.” Someone can deny that a pronoun asserts developmental sex while valuing it as recognition, politeness, affiliation, or compliance with an agreed practice. Caring about a linguistic form does not establish the particular descriptive content under dispute. The chapter itself supplies the corrective when it quotes Davis on meaningful but non-denotative pronouns.

The closing “sucks to be you” passage is also credited too readily as exemplary accounting. It casts the dispute as competing feelings and attributes that ordering to the opposing coalition. An advocate might instead offer reasons concerning equal standing, predictable interaction, or the burdens of publicly contesting someone’s status. Those reasons might fail, but Davis’s formulation does not state them fairly merely because it states a cost.

His own reported public pronoun practice deserves credit as evidence that he distinguishes conduct from philosophical assent. It does not establish that his proposed description of the opposing policy is adequate.

### Stealth replies are not automatically failures of honesty

Section 9 calls withheld political context “a failure to disclose a filter.” Abstracting an argument from its motivating dispute can be legitimate. The relevant question is whether the presentation created a false impression about the argument’s target, the interlocutor’s actual commitments, or what an endorsement would subsequently be taken to endorse.

Davis’s acknowledgment of possible unfairness is important, but the chapter should identify the misleading implication rather than infer dishonesty from undisclosed motivation alone. Check the “Containment Thread” and “A Hill of Validity” for how endorsements were sought and used. Without that evidence, the verdict is stronger than its basis.

## 3. Categories, biology, and the scope of the gender conclusion

### “Two real categories” should become “at least two relevant distinctions”

The opening verdict and §8 present developmental sex and social gender as an exhaustive pair. The discussion then stipulates:

> “Perceived sex is not a third category…”

Why not? A perception can estimate another property while also being a variable worth studying in its own right. Social treatment can diverge from perceived sex when an observer knowingly follows an institutional rule or a person’s requested form of address. The chapter’s own subject includes precisely such cases.

“Social gender” also gathers together appearance, treatment, identity, pronoun use, and social expectations without showing that they form one category. Ozy’s proposed convention and Davis’s concession about successful passing do not necessarily concern the same extension. Before declaring the dispute legitimate by Davis’s criteria, specify the candidate classification and what it predicts.

The treatment of medical purposes is similarly overbroad:

> “For a physician, a sexologist, or a question about reproduction, that is plainly true.”

No medical conclusion is needed here. The logically relevant point is that different questions may require developmental history, current bodily characteristics, or particular capacities. The chapter should avoid awarding an entire profession to a single variable.

### The causal argument is oversold

Section 7 says:

> “The genome sits at the root of the causal graph of an organism’s traits, so conditioning on ancestry renders many downstream traits conditionally independent…”

That “so” carries more than the chapter establishes. Conditioning on a common cause screens off its effects only under suitable causal assumptions; the existence of a common ancestor or a genome does not by itself establish the proposed conditional independences. The argument needs an explicit model and an account of its approximation.

The analogy to Boyd is also misleading:

> “‘the root of the causal graph’ is a sharper name for what Boyd calls homeostasis.”

[memory] Boyd’s homeostatic property-cluster account permits mechanisms maintaining clusters without requiring one root cause or a naïve Bayes structure. The chapter should describe a resemblance, not claim that Davis has supplied a more precise equivalent.

Vaniver’s phylogenetic objection warrants more attention to the argument itself. Does Davis’s preferred grouping mean mammals, a clade containing familiar fish, or an ecologically useful grouping? “Phylogeny is not an aesthetic preference, because evolution happened” establishes that ancestry is real; it does not select the relevant taxonomic rank or lexical boundary.

### The sexology dependency is narrower and less exclusive than stated

“For the psychological dimensions… it needs the two-type hypothesis” is too strong. Evidence about psychological distributions could support or undermine a classification without establishing that particular taxonomy. Conversely, the taxonomy would not alone determine the appropriate weights, population, metric, or decision threshold.

The stronger objection behind Ozy’s lesbian example is also normative and semantic: even an atypical conjunction of traits need not determine membership if the category is organized around a different criterion. Davis’s multivariate reply answers a statistical simplification; it does not yet settle which properties constitute or govern membership.

“Beyond the Binary” is especially important here. The quoted admission about being less “real” needs analysis distinguishing typicality, membership, and social standing. At present the chapter records the admission and moves on.

## 4. Scholarship that would change the assessment

These additions should support specific revisions, not enlarge the “academic neighbors” catalogue.

- **Conceptual ethics and engineering.** [memory] Alexis Burgess and David Plunkett’s “Conceptual Ethics I” and “Conceptual Ethics II” distinguish questions about which concepts to use from descriptive questions about existing usage. Herman Cappelen’s *Fixing Language* examines conceptual engineering without making successful reform depend simply on announcing a new definition. These would sharpen the chapter’s account of adoption, uptake, and accountability.
- **Meaning and social significance.** [memory] Grice’s work on implicature and Sally McConnell-Ginet’s *Gender, Sexuality, and Meaning* offer resources for distinguishing descriptive content from what an expression conveys or accomplishes socially. This is directly relevant to the pronoun inference.
- **Reference and causal explanation.** [memory] Putnam’s “The Meaning of ‘Meaning’” and Kripke’s *Naming and Necessity* distinguish reference from speakers’ identifying descriptions. They could strengthen Davis’s objection to treating appearances as constitutive, while also exposing how much the chapter leaves unsettled about word meaning.
- **Projectibility and pluralism.** [memory] Goodman’s *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* makes the choice of projectible predicates a substantive problem. It belongs beside the metric discussion: regularity in a representation cannot alone vindicate that representation. Dupré and Boyd should then be engaged as competing accounts, rather than brief parallels.
- **Biological classification.** [memory] Elliott Sober’s *Reconstructing the Past* treats the relation between phylogenetic inference and explanatory evidence. It would help test the move from ancestry to conditional independence. No extensive philosophy-of-biology excursus is needed.
- **Cognitive categorization.** [memory] Rosch’s “Principles of Categorization,” Barsalou’s “Ad Hoc Categories,” and Medin and Schaffer’s “Context Theory of Classification Learning” provide contrasting approaches to prototype structure, goal-dependent grouping, and exemplar-based classification. They matter because the chapter repeatedly treats a category as a single probabilistic summary without distinguishing a normative model from a psychological claim.
- **Social ontology and the missing opposing text.** [memory] Ásta’s *Categories We Live By* distinguishes social conferral from straightforward self-identification. Robin Dembroff’s “Escaping the Natural Attitude About Gender” directly contests Byrne’s approach. Reading Byrne’s reply without Dembroff’s argument leaves the academic exchange unevenly represented.

Kleinberg needs correction rather than supplementation. [memory] His impossibility theorem concerns jointly unsatisfiable requirements on a clustering function. It does not establish that a particular dataset has several equally good partitions. The sentence equating his theorem with gjm’s point should be removed.

## 5. Structure, overlap, and prose

The chapter exceeds its planned ceiling while repeating its central verdict in the opening, §1, §6, §8, and §10. Consolidation would create room for the missing objections.

A more effective sequence would retain the chronological reconstruction through §7, shorten “academic neighbors,” and organize §8 around three questions: what the models establish; when altered classifications mislead; and what follows for the disputed uses of *woman*. The coordination argument belongs here, but in its qualified form. Chapter 5 should own the general receiver-relative account and attribution problem.

Section 9 substantially overlaps Chapter 7’s account of Soares, the withheld draft, the apology, and motive attribution. Retain only what bears on the presentation of philosophical arguments and cross-reference the fuller assessment. The early “Contemporary” passage is already central to Chapter 1; a short reference would suffice.

Several formulations sound like verdicts supplied in advance of analysis: “correct and correctly calculated,” “the whole later dispute in miniature,” “few and good,” “the sharpest counterexample,” and “the most philosophically generous essays in the corpus.” Replace these with the property being praised or the objection being answered. “Davis reached these positions without that literature” also requires evidence about his reading; absence of citation establishes less.

The chapter’s first-person “I borrow the title” violates the standing voice rule. The method note’s account of November 2018 tweets conflicts with the Introduction and Chapter 7 summaries, which report successful archive verification. Finally, the summary’s claimed absence of a retreat narrative is not fully realized: “In private Davis had already moved further” and “what Davis came to think” still imply development that the chapter elsewhere locates in the original qualified position.

**Proposed replacement for the opening paragraph**

*This gives the chapter a defensible thesis without anticipating stronger conclusions than it establishes.*

> Davis’s categorization essays ask what makes a classification answerable to the world. Their recurring distinction separates the purposes a category serves from its success in serving them: interests select what matters, while evidence constrains which distinctions support the relevant inferences. The formal examples make those constraints explicit. They do not by themselves determine which purposes should govern a shared word, or when changing its use amounts to deception. Davis often acknowledges these limits. The unresolved question is how his qualified account of classification supports the broader accusations made in its name.
