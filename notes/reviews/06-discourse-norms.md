## A. Weak or overstated arguments (ranked)

### A1. The individualism/institution crux is contradicted by the chapter's own material

The thesis paragraph says: "Davis treats discourse as a contest among individuals each responsible only for his own accuracy, while his critics, and much of the philosophy of science, treat it as an institution whose output depends on keeping fallible contributors in the conversation. On that question his opponents had the better of it."

The evidence for "individualist" is two sentences from one 2023 exchange with Raymond Arnold ("individualist conflict-theoretic rather than a collective mistake-theoretic perspective"; "that's not my problem"). Both are about an *author's* responsibility for how a post's style affects readers. The chapter then generalizes them to a view about forums ("'not my problem' is not available to whoever runs it"), as though Davis had claimed that a forum's operators may also disregard retention. Nothing quoted shows that he did. The rest of the chapter shows him arguing the opposite way:

- "Hazards of Selection Effects" (§5) is an argument about communities: the third filter fails "because norms depend on collective enforcement," and the essay asks organizers to acknowledge a problem at the community level. The chapter calls it "Davis's strongest essay."
- The Achmiz reply's "stated-values" argument (§6), which the chapter judges the strongest part of the reply, is an institutional-design argument: the 2017 aim of a "single locus," and the claim that author-level bans meet legitimate interests at lower cost than a site ban.
- The Achmiz reply is, at bottom, a retention argument: the forum lost a valuable contributor (the "wisest person I know").
- The chapter itself says Longino's norms "partly favor Davis" (venues, uptake, public standards).

So both sides in 2025–26 argue that a forum's output depends on who stays. They disagree about *which* contributors' departure costs more (critics who are unpleasant to answer vs. authors who find answering them unrewarding), and about who should bear the cost of filtering (each author, through post-level bans, or the site, through a site ban). That is a substantive and interesting disagreement, and the chapter's material is well suited to it, but it is not individualism vs. institution. As written, the thesis acknowledges the institutional character of Davis's best work in §5 and §6 and then ignores it in the verdict (a concession acknowledged and then ignored, in Davis's favor).

The 2023 "individualist" statement is still worth discussing: it is a real position about authors, and it may well be in tension with his 2025–26 institutional arguments. If so, the tension (an author owes the commons nothing, but a moderator owes it the retention of critics) is the chapter's best critical point, and it is sharper than the current one. A proposed thesis paragraph is in §F1.

### A2. "His own theory of disguised conflict supports them": the argument does not show what it claims

§4 argues: Davis says correct epistemology involves no conflicting interests; but "Assume Bad Faith" says most real disagreements are disguised conflicts with mixed motives; so actual discourse has the mixed payoffs of life and work, "and Sabien's analysis of trust equilibria applies to it"; negotiation "among parties who suspect each other of hidden motives is the setting in which trust lowers costs."

Three problems.

1. Showing that discourse is a setting where trust *matters* does not show that trust should be *extended*. Sabien's own model, as the chapter summarizes it, has two self-reinforcing equilibria, each "well supported by evidence." If Davis believes he is in the low-trust one (that is the content of "Assume Bad Faith"), then Sabien's model tells him that unilateral trust is exploited. The disguised-conflict premise makes the case for verification stronger, not weaker. The chapter needs a further premise, such as that the actual environment is closer to high-trust than Davis thinks, or that unilateral trust can move a population between equilibria, and it argues neither.
2. "Negotiation ... needs [trust]" is asserted. A large part of negotiation theory and arms-control practice concerns agreements designed to work without trust (verification, enforceable commitments, procedures that do not depend on good faith); from memory, Fisher and Ury's *Getting to Yes* explicitly advises proceeding "independent of trust." Davis's own "So You Don't Trust Me?" (costly signals, per the Ch. 5 summary) is a theory of how to cooperate without presumed trust. The chapter should engage that rather than assert the opposite.
3. The chapter does not distinguish what is being trusted. Sabien's essay is about trust in others' goodwill (whether a clumsy remark is read as malice). Davis's "I don't want people to trust me" is, as quoted, about the evaluation of arguments. The philosophy of testimony draws exactly this line (trusting reports one cannot check vs. presuming benign motives in one's interlocutor). The chapter's strongest form of the institutional objection is the testimony one: science depends on accepting reports one cannot verify (Hardwig, "The Role of Trust in Knowledge," 1991, from memory). Davis's position may survive that objection better than the goodwill one: arguments posted on a forum are inspectable in a way that lab reports are not, which is the point of "Critic Contributions" ("the text screens off the process"). Or it may not, if most of what a forum transmits is testimony. Either way, the chapter's §4 currently runs the two together. A proposed replacement paragraph is in §F2.

The Williams sentence ("A practice that refuses to extend trust must verify everything, and verification is costly") inherits this problem: it is a dilemma with a missing middle (calibrated trust, which is plausibly Davis's actual position, given his "minimal amount of strategic bad faith" and "effective rhetoric" concessions).

### A3. The Wentworth exchange may be partly a dispute over a term, and the chapter scores it as a substantive loss

Davis: managing emotions is not "part of the subject matter of epistemic rationality, even if managing people's emotions is a good idea and useful for having good discussions in practice." Wentworth: "managing them is a very central issue for human epistemic rationality in practice." Davis grants the practical point in the same sentence. What remains is whether "epistemic rationality" names the theory of what makes beliefs accurate or the practical craft of getting humans to accurate beliefs. Ch. 4's own apparatus (the first kind of value-dependence: which features a category must help predict is a choice) applies directly, and using it here would connect the chapters. The conclusion lists Wentworth among those "closer to right," which overstates what the exchange shows. The chapter should say how much of the disagreement is substantive (whether Davis's practice actually neglects the emotional rate-limiter, which the "Listening Disorders" accounting suggests he knowingly accepts) and how much is about the scope of the term.

### A4. "Never answered" and "did not answer them"

- §2: "That sentence holds, and it is the part of Davis's critique that his critics never answered." This is a universal negative over a reception the chapter did not read in full (the Note on Method says the 400-comment ban thread was read only in part). At most: "no answer was found in the threads read."
- Conclusion: "These are the norm of accounting applied to rules and institutions, and LessWrong's moderators did not answer them." But §6 says "Habryka's announcement is candid about where its authority lies," and the chapter reports Habryka stating plainly that moderators police "vibe" without a mechanical account and that he decides on personal responsibility. That candor is, on the chapter's own definition, an *accounting*: it says what the rule serves (authors' experience) and on whose authority. The chapter cannot both credit Habryka with candor and say the moderators did not answer the demand for accounting. The defensible claim is narrower: Habryka stated what the ban serves but not what it costs in the terms "Hazards" asks for (the critics filtered out). That would be fair to both sides. (Concession acknowledged and then ignored, against the moderators.)

### A5. The Aumann section slightly undersells Davis's argument

"It does not support the inference from persistence alone." The chapter's own summary of "Assume Bad Faith" includes a second premise: "hidden motives are ubiquitous." So Davis's inference is not from persistence alone; it is persistence plus an independent claim about human motivation (presumably drawing on Hanson, possibly Hanson and Simler's *The Elephant in the Brain*; check). And Davis's definition in "Dark World" ("at least one of the participants is behaving as if they have some other goal than getting to the truth") is close to Cowen and Hanson's own dishonesty charge, which the chapter grants "may well be right" and "supports a conclusion of the kind Davis wants." The gap between Davis and his source may be smaller than "outruns" suggests: the difference is in the confidence and generality of the conclusion ("probably just fake"; "typical human disagreements"). The section would be stronger if it located the overreach precisely there, in "typical" and "probably," instead of in the logical form.

On Ben Pace: from memory, Scott Aaronson's "The Complexity of Agreement" (2005) found that the *communication* needed to reach approximate agreement is small and independent of the size of the agents' information, while the *computation* (simulating each other's reasoning) can be infeasible. If that is right, the result partly supports Davis (agreement need not take long conversations) and partly Pace. The chapter quotes Pace's summary and lets it stand as the last word; it should either fetch Aaronson or mark that the characterization is Pace's.

### A6. The survivorship argument against the Vassar story may be stronger than Davis's use of the story warrants

"A culture's survivors are the worst witnesses to how well it retains contributors." This is right as a general point, but it lands only if Davis used his own case as evidence about retention in general. As quoted, he says of himself: "In reality, that wasn't actually a risk." If he then generalized (harsh feedback rarely drives people away), the critique holds; if he offered the story only to show that harsh feedback can be valuable to the person who receives it, the critique attacks a claim he did not make. Check the June 2026 comment. Also, the same "Hazards" logic applies to the moderators' evidence: the authors who complained to Habryka are a selected sample, and the critics who stopped commenting under moderation are as invisible as the newcomers who fled harsh comments. Turning the selection argument only on Davis is one-sided; applying it to both sides is the more interesting result and fits A1.

### A7. "That answers the question of legitimacy, if not in the way Davis wanted"

This is glib, and it conflates two senses of legitimacy. Habryka's statement settles *authority* (who may ban). Davis's section title, "LessWrong 2.0 Has Forfeited Its Intellectual Legitimacy," as described, makes an *epistemic* claim: a forum that removes certain critics can no longer certify its own conclusions, which is the "Hazards" argument applied. The plan calls this section "the culmination of the norms argument"; the chapter gives it one sentence. It deserves a paragraph that asks whether one ban, with author-level bans and other critics remaining, plausibly produces the selection effect "Hazards" describes, which is an empirical and quantitative question (how much filtering, of whom). Proposed paragraph in §F3.

### A8. The Achmiz section does not test the ban's own rationale

The chapter checks Davis's fact-checks against Habryka, and Habryka's correction against Davis (as the plan asks). It does not assess the ban's central empirical claim: that Achmiz's commenting cost the site more (in authors discouraged, in effort ratios of "five to one") than his presence gained. That is precisely the institutional question the thesis says the critics had right. If the chapter believes the institutional view, it should say whether the institutional case for this ban was made well; if the evidence is not available (private complaints, unverifiable ratios), it should say that, since it bears on whether the moderators met the accounting standard.

### A9. Smaller overstatements

- "The argument is sound and the analogy exact" ("Hazards"/RLHF). "Exact" is too strong for an analogy: a reward model is trained on human ratings, while a community's filters are partly chosen by the people filtered. Say what the analogy captures.
- "a parallel with Ayn Rand's view of altruists that the essay states with relish and does not argue." "With relish" is an attribution of affect. Check whether the "collaborative truth-seeking doesn't exist ... lying" line is in Davis's own voice or a dialogue/paraphrase of Rand.
- "Listening Disorders" premise "asserted rather than supports": fair, but a literature exists (information avoidance in behavioral economics; from memory, Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein, "Information Avoidance," *Journal of Economic Literature* 2017; Sweeny et al. 2010). Citing it would let the chapter say whether the premise is plausible, instead of only that it is unsupported.

## B. Fairness

### B1. To Davis

- A1 and A3 above: concessions recorded (goodwill "a necessary part of many discourse algorithms"; "minimal amount of strategic bad faith needed to keep the discussion going"; "Is that bad faith, or just effective rhetoric? I think it's both!"; emotions "useful for having good discussions in practice") and then ignored in the verdict that he "never defended against the view that a forum's output includes who keeps contributing." The concessions show he grants retention instrumental value. The verdict should engage what he grants, not what the 2023 "not my problem" sentence alone implies.
- "Davis's own published concessions ... They are specific, and they do not reach the premise." Which premise? If A1 is right and the premise is not what the chapter says, this closing line misfires.
- The "Lady Justice" paragraph (§6). A role-play with a language model is a weak basis for "evidence that his account of the dispute was not purely epistemic in private." Lines written in a role-play may be exploratory or adversarial; the chapter does not say whether the draft frames them as Davis's views. Also, "goes further than anything published" needs checking: if UUT has published claims about sex differences in discourse norms (it may; Ch. 3/Ch. 7 material), the sentence is false and the draft adds less than claimed. The dating is also muddled: "of the dispute's period" but the header date is 2022 and the content is "2023 or later"; the 2023 rate limit and the 2025 ban are different disputes. Consider moving it to a footnote, stating the role-play framing exactly, or cutting it. It currently costs more in fairness than it adds in evidence.

### B2. To Sabien

- The paragraph on the "Truth or Dare" passage argues that Sabien's claim about Davis "does not report having applied" the counterfactual check. Fair as far as it goes, but Sabien and Davis had years of public interaction (the plan's core texts include "Reply to Duncan Sabien on Strawmanning," 2023, which the chapter does not discuss; and the chapter mentions Davis's apology for a 2021 "This is insane" comment). Sabien's judgment may rest on a long record. An essay paragraph not reporting a check is not evidence that none was run. The chapter should say what the public record of their interactions contains, or soften to "the passage gives no evidence of the check."
- "The critics were not uniformly fair either": the "cowards" point. The chapter grants that Achmiz used the word and found no instance from Davis. But the chapter itself quotes Davis calling intellectuals who block critics "ineffective and boring people whose pretensions to intellectualism are morally fraudulent" ("Listening Disorders"), and the "Critic Contributions" heading saying such people do not "care about maximizing correctness." These postdate or predate Sabien's 1 June 2026 comment (check; "Listening Disorders" is September 2026, after; "Critic Contributions" is July 2025, before). If pre-June-2026 Davis texts make the substance of the charge (that blocking critics is a moral failing), Sabien was wrong on the word and roughly right on the substance, and the paragraph as written is misleading by literal accuracy, which is the fault Davis's own theory of deception (Ch. 5) is designed to catch. The paragraph should report this.

### B3. To Habryka and the moderators

- A4 (candor credited, then "did not answer") and A8 (ban rationale not assessed) are the main points.
- The use of Longino's third norm against "police the vibe" is too quick. "Public accessibility of the standards" does not obviously require mechanical standards; a standard stated publicly as a judgment standard, with published case law (which the chapter says Habryka set out), is publicly accessible in one sense, as legal standards like "reasonableness" are. The objection Davis makes in "Just Make a New Rule!" (people respond to stated rules without needing judgment calls) is about rules vs. standards, a well-studied trade-off in legal theory (from memory: Kaplow, "Rules versus Standards: An Economic Analysis," *Duke Law Journal* 1992; Schauer, *Playing by the Rules*). The chapter calls Davis's essay "the right objection"; it would be fairer to say it is one side of a known trade-off and to say which way it cuts in a small forum with a few moderators, where standards are cheap to apply and rules are easy to game (the "five to one" effort-ratio complaint is a complaint about a commenter gaming the absence of rules).
- The 2023 background. The chapter mentions "a three-month rate limit in 2023" without saying that (from memory) it followed a public conflict between Achmiz and Sabien that the moderators adjudicated. That context bears on Sabien's role in the 2026 thread and on how long the moderators had been trying lesser measures, which is relevant to whether the ban was proportionate.

### B4. To Achmiz

The Reception section of the brief asks for the other parties' own accounts. Achmiz is the subject of the ban and is characterized throughout (by Habryka, by Davis, by Sabien), but the chapter cites only one sentence of his ("Banning critics from your posts is a cowardly act.") and the debate dialogue. Did Achmiz respond publicly to the ban (in the thread, on his own site, elsewhere)? If so, it should be read and represented; if he did not, say so. Also check the description "built the reader site GreaterWrong": from memory GreaterWrong was a joint project (with the user known as clone of saturn); if so the sentence over-credits him.

### B5. To Ngo

Fair. One detail: "Richard Ngo, asked by Davis whether he vouched for the ban's reasoning, judged the exchange." The word "judged" makes Ngo sound like an appointed arbiter; check whether Ngo presented himself that way or simply gave his view.

## C. Missing objections

From Davis's side:

1. **The anti-extortion argument.** The plan's thesis (c) includes "Don't Negotiate With Terrorist Memeplexes": conceding to hurt feelings rewards being hurt. This is plausibly Davis's real reason for refusing tone accommodation, and it is an institutional argument (incentives created by a policy, not individual accuracy). The chapter does not mention it. Its absence is why Davis looks like a mere individualist. The chapter should state it at full strength (accommodating complaints about tone selects for complaints) and then assess it, which also lands thesis 2 (see E1).
2. **Inspectable arguments vs. testimony** (A2.3): Davis's best reply to the trust objection, available from "Critic Contributions."
3. **Nullius in verba.** The Royal Society's motto (from memory; check) is a direct counterweight to the Shapin point and on Davis's side. The comet anecdote can also be read Davis's way: a society that smooths over a factual conflict between two observers to keep both reporting is protecting reputations, which is the "LinkedIn attractor" Habryka himself names. The chapter presents the anecdote only as support for the critics.
4. **Mill.** *On Liberty* ch. 2 is the classical source for Davis's view that truth needs the collision of adverse opinions and that a received opinion not contested becomes "dead dogma" (from memory). It is surprising the chapter does not mention it. But Mill also says (from memory) that the worst offense in polemic is to stigmatize opponents as bad and immoral people, which bears directly against "Assume Bad Faith." Mill thus supports Davis on §2 and §5 and opposes him on §3, which would give the chapter a natural classical frame.
5. **Mercier and Sperber** (2011, in the plan's external list, not used): the argumentative theory of reasoning holds that individual reasoning is biased toward one's own side and that groups arguing adversarially reach better answers. This is close to Davis's "debate converges on truth ... because debaters are trying to prove each other wrong," and the chapter's conclusion lists "Debate works because it sets biased searchers against each other" as part of the durable core without evaluating it. Mercier and Sperber's evidence (from memory) is mostly from small groups with a shared interest in the answer, which is the condition Davis's critics say is needed. Fetch and use; it may decide the localdeity/"Aiming for Convergence" dispute better than the chapter currently does.
6. **AI safety via debate** (Irving, Christiano and Amodei 2018; from memory): debate as a truth-tracking mechanism is a research program in Davis's own milieu, with mixed empirical results. Possibly Ch. 11's material; at least a cross-reference.

From the critics' side:

7. **The cost to the person answering.** Habryka's effort ratio is an objection the chapter reports but never weighs against "Critic Contributions." A comment's value lies in its text, but the cost of answering it does not, and a forum allocates authors' attention. Davis's text-screens-off principle answers the question "is this comment valuable?" but not "is this commenter's pattern of comments worth what it costs the people who answer?" The chapter should put this to "Critic Contributions" directly.
8. **Exit, voice, and loyalty.** Hirschman (from memory) is the obvious frame for the whole affair: authors who leave (exit), critics who object (voice), and a site whose operators must decide whose exit to prevent. It would also make A1's reframing precise.
9. **Empirical moderation literature.** Kraut and Resnick, *Building Successful Online Communities* (2012, from memory), summarize evidence on newcomer retention, the effects of harsh first responses, and sanctions. If the evidence shows harsh replies reduce retention of newcomers, it supports Habryka; if it shows that clear rules and graduated sanctions matter more than tone, it supports parts of "Just Make a New Rule!" Ostrom's design principles for commons governance (from memory: clearly defined rules, participation in rule-making, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution) are also relevant to whether a three-year ban after a rate limit, decided by one person, fits good governance practice. Grimmelmann, "The Virtues of Moderation" (2015, from memory), is a legal-scholarly taxonomy of moderation that would situate both sides.

## D. Prose that reads as AI-generated

The chapter's prose is generally plain and exact, and better than average on this point. The main recurring habit is the short demonstrative verdict sentence after a quotation. Instances:

- "That is right, and it means 'rationalist discourse' can name a real subset."
- "The demand for derivation is sound."
- "That is a good observation about who may ask for charity."
- "That sentence holds, and it is the part of Davis's critique that his critics never answered."
- "That is honest, and it concedes that ..."
- "The accounting is honest. It does not change the policy, ..."
- "The argument is sound and the analogy exact."
- "For him it was not."
- "That is the real disagreement, and it is not between logic and feelings." (also a "not X but Y")
- "The record supports the first kind of argument most."
- "They are specific, and they do not reach the premise."
- "Its weakness lies elsewhere." (thesis paragraph)

Several of these could be merged into the sentence that introduces the quotation, or replaced by the reason (e.g., instead of "The demand for derivation is sound," say what makes it sound).

Announcing and scaffolding:

- §1, second paragraph: "He made the case in three ways: that ... (§2); that ... (§3); and ... (§5). His critics ... (§4). The ban ... (§6)." This is a table of contents in prose, with a triad. A short orienting sentence would do.
- §6: "Its arguments are of three kinds. The first concerns stated values. ... The second is a set of factual checks. ... The third, under the heading ..." followed by "The record supports the first kind of argument most," "The factual checks were partly vindicated," and "The third kind of argument is where ...". Stacked first/second/third scaffolding across four paragraphs.
- Conclusion: "In the Achmiz affair the critique of stated values was strong, the factual checks partly vindicated, and the explanation of motive the attribution problem in a live case." A triadic scorecard, which the plan's §5 ("no scorecard tone") rules out. The conclusion's first paragraph is also a list of verdicts.

Aphoristic closers: "A culture's survivors are the worst witnesses to how well it retains contributors." "Davis's essays rarely ask them." Both are fine once; the chapter uses this cadence at the end of most paragraphs.

Project machinery (the brief forbids "references to the project's own machinery ... 'by script'"):

- Note on Method: "every quotation has been checked mechanically against its source." Cut or move to the study's general method statement in the Introduction.
- "the chapter records it only as evidence ..." and "this study found no instance of Davis using the word in the posts and threads it surveyed" and "this study has not read Shapin" (in running text). Scholarly disclosure is fine, but put the Shapin disclaimer in a note and phrase the "cowards" search as a fact about the record ("I have found no instance" is not available in this voice; "no instance appears in the posts and threads cited here" is closer).

Minor: "Hanson and Cowen's argument" in the conclusion vs. "Cowen and Hanson" elsewhere.

## E. Drift and overlap

### E1. Drift from the plan's thesis

The plan's thesis 2 says that where part (c) operates unqualified it produces "the treatment of tact as bad faith, and the refusal to weigh costs that are not 'feelings' (attention, trust, the commons)." Ch. 6 is where that claim should be tested, since those are its subjects. The chapter never mentions part (c) and substitutes a different diagnosis (individualism). Either the chapter should argue that the plan's diagnosis is wrong for this material (the plan explicitly allows "If ... a chapter finds Davis's practice there is not driven by (c), the chapter says so"), or it should connect its findings to (c). The likely connection is the anti-extortion form (C1), and "Listening Disorders" (a costed policy: "making people's days worse for only a small probability...") looks like (b) displacing (c) in the way the plan describes for Ch. 9's 2026 material. Saying so would tie the chapter to the book's argument.

Thesis 4 (self-application) appears only in the conclusion's last sentence. The chapter's material (the Feynman concession to philh, the "That's my bad," "Listening Disorders") supports it and could be drawn together in one paragraph.

### E2. Plan items not covered

Core texts in the plan but absent from the chapter: "Reply to Duncan Sabien on Strawmanning" (2023), "We Are Less Wrong than E. T. Jaynes…" (2023), "Comment on 'Four Layers…'" (2025), "'Yes, and—'…" (2025), the draft "The Archipelago of Crybullying," "Speculative Rules of Engagement" (2015), "I Have the Honor to Be Your Obedient Servant" (2016). The two early posts matter most: the chapter dates the position "From 2019," and if the 2015–16 posts show earlier, perhaps more cooperative, rules of engagement, the chapter is missing the development of the position, which is what secondary literature on an author is expected to trace. The Sabien "Strawmanning" reply matters for B2. Reception names in the plan not used: romeostevensit, SaidAchmiz (beyond one line), ricraz is used as Ngo, Benquo, Viliam, Nesov, sil-ver. The chapter is ~6,400 words against a 7,000 target, so there is room.

### E3. Overlap with Ch. 5

The chapter invokes Ch. 5's attribution test three times (§3 "You'll Never Persuade," §4 on Sabien, §6 "What I Think Is Actually Going On"). That is legitimate application, but the third use is the one Ch. 7 will need as precedent; keep the §6 treatment as the full application and shorten the other two to a clause each, so the device does not become a refrain. The Williams sentence in §4 is thin and partly duplicates Ch. 5 §4; either develop the handoff Ch. 5 promised (Williams's Sincerity and trust, applied to Sabien) or cut it to a cross-reference.

### E4. Overlap with Ch. 4

The Wentworth exchange is a case for Ch. 4's two kinds of value-dependence (A3). A cross-reference would help and would show the book's tools working across chapters.

- The Vassar 2008 story: fine as used. Do not characterize Vassar further; Ch. 7 (community) and Ch. 9 (Vassar–psychosis) own him.

## F. Proposed prose

Quotations below are copied from the chapter unless marked [unverified].

### F1. Replacement for the thesis paragraph (opening of the chapter)

> From 2019 to 2026 Davis argued that the norms of rationalist discussion (charity, the ideological Turing test, aiming for convergence, the assumption of good faith, moderation against unpleasant critics) must answer to the laws of inference, and that many of them fail the test. Much of the critique is right, and its strongest parts apply the norm of accounting to rules: a norm that serves comfort or retention rather than accuracy should say so, and a community that filters its critics should count what the filter costs. Where it goes wrong, it goes wrong in two places. The inference from Aumann's theorem to the dishonesty of most disagreement claims more than its sources support. And Davis holds two views about the commons of a forum that do not fit together: as an author, he owes it nothing ("that's not my problem"), but its moderators owe it the retention of critics like Said Achmiz. The second view is the better one, and it is an argument about institutions of the same kind his critics make. The dispute over the ban of Achmiz in 2025 was therefore not between individual reason and collective comfort. Both sides held that a forum's output depends on who stays; they disagreed about whose departure costs more and who should pay for the filtering, and on that question the record supports parts of each side's case.

(This changes the verdict, which is the main agent's call; it is offered because A1 suggests the current verdict does not follow from the chapter's own §§5–6.)

### F2. Replacement for the "deeper difficulty" paragraph in §4

> The deeper difficulty lies in what Davis declines to trust. His reply treats trust as a single thing, but Sabien's essay concerns trust in goodwill, the willingness to read an ambiguous remark as clumsiness rather than malice, while Davis's sentence concerns the evaluation of arguments. On the second, his position is strong. An argument posted to a forum can be inspected by anyone, which is the point of "Critic Contributions": the text screens off its author. On the first, his own premises cut both ways. If most disagreements that matter are conflicts of interest in disguise, as "Assume Bad Faith" holds, then the payoffs of actual discourse are the mixed ones of life and work, and Sabien's model of trust equilibria applies. But that model has two stable outcomes, and it does not say that a reasoner who believes he is in the low-trust one should extend trust unilaterally. What it does imply is that Davis's prescribed remedy, negotiation that separates real disagreements from conflicts of interest, needs either some trust or some means of verifying commitments, and the second is what his later essay on costly signals of trust supplies (see [Chapter 5]). Where the trust objection has most force is testimony: much of what a forum transmits is report rather than argument, and reports, like the observations of the astronomers in Vaniver's example below, cannot be checked by inspection. There the institutional view is right that a community which verifies nothing on trust cannot learn much.

(The claim that "So You Don't Trust Me?" is about costly signals is from the Ch. 5 summary; check before use.)

### F3. New paragraph for §6, after the stated-values paragraph, on legitimacy

> Davis's final heading, "LessWrong 2.0 Has Forfeited Its Intellectual Legitimacy," makes a claim of a different kind from Habryka's statement of authority. Habryka settles who may ban: he does, on his own responsibility, reviewable by Yudkowsky or the board of the organization he runs. Davis's claim is epistemic. By the argument of "Hazards of Selection Effects," a forum that removes its sharpest critics can no longer tell from the inside whether its ideas are good, and so cannot certify them to outsiders. Stated that generally, the argument proves too much, since every forum filters, and "Hazards" asks only that the filter be acknowledged and its costs tracked. Whether one ban, imposed after a rate limit and leaving author-level bans and other critics in place, amounts to a filter of the kind the essay describes is a question of degree: how many critics are removed or deterred, and whether those who remain can still say what Achmiz said. Davis does not try to answer it, and neither does Habryka, whose account of the ban's benefits, the authors it would keep, rests on complaints made to him privately. Each side offered the other an estimate of the costs of its preferred filter without evidence that an outsider could check.

(The last sentence depends on whether Habryka's complaints were private; check.)
