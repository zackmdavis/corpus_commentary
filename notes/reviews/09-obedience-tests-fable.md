# Review: 09-obedience-tests (Fable)

## Overall

This is a careful chapter with a sound spine: one demand (name the force) traced across twelve years, separated from the polemical theory that grew around it, and tested against both the research and Davis's own later record. Its best move is to let the 2023 draft testify against the 2017 theory. Its weakest moves are three: an inference in §5 that is not licensed by the studies it rests on; a reconstruction in §4 that stops one step short of the point most favorable to Davis in his own record; and a §7 that holds Davis's 2021 comment to a standard (disproving any contribution by Vassar) that no record could meet. The chapter is also 1,300 words over ceiling, the plan's reception items are absent, and the summary in `chapter-summaries.md` is bolder than the chapter it summarizes.

## 1. Weak or overstated arguments

**§5, "The literature tells against the second claim."** The chapter's argument is: "if a hold perceived as punishment is more distressing, a hold announced as punishment would be more distressing still." This does not follow. The studies report a correlation between perceived punitiveness and distress; they do not compare a hold announced as punishment with one disguised as care, and the chapter admits as much two sentences later ("Whether punishment is the ward's purpose is a separate question, which none of these studies measures"). More important, the distress attached to perceived punitiveness may arise from the gap between what staff say and what they do, which is Davis's own complaint. On that reading the research is neutral between "disguised punishment is distressing" and "announced punishment would be distressing," and the chapter's lede ("The literature does not support the further claims … that the ward exists mainly for the convenience of families and that its staff lie") is stronger than §5 earns. What the research does cut against is Davis's proposed wording: "sick of your antics" withdraws goodwill along with the euphemism, and benevolence is among the elements the literature associates with lower perceived coercion. That is a narrower and defensible point, and the chapter should make it instead. A replacement paragraph is proposed below.

Also, the lede's "that its staff lie" is not tested by any literature in §5. The lying claim is assessed in §6 via the Roberta bet. The lede should not attribute to the literature a finding it does not contain.

**§5, reliance on abstracts.** The paragraph on Priebe, Katsakou and Galon–Wineman carries real weight (placing Davis in Katsakou's "ambivalent" group; the 39–71% figures), and all three were "read in abstract only." For a monograph that will be cited, that is thin. [memory] Priebe et al. 2010 in the *British Journal of Psychiatry* and Katsakou et al. 2012 are likely available in full through PubMed Central or the journals' open archives. Fetching the full texts before publication is worth the effort; the placement of Davis in a qualitative category should rest on the category's actual description, not an abstract's summary of it.

**§6, "the bet could not discriminate between them."** Too strong. Alexander, a psychiatrist, bet at 20:1 that friends' messages would reach a detained patient; that is evidence about professional expectations of what wards normally do. Non-delivery therefore discriminates between "messages normally get through" and "they do not," even though it cannot discriminate between "lost" and "lied about." Davis's inference to *lying kidnappers* overreaches, as the chapter says; but the chapter should concede that something was tested and that a psychiatrist's prior lost. Separately, the chapter should make explicit what it only implies: a ward that "refused to confirm or deny" that a patient was present was not lying, and if the draft reports no false statement by staff in the Roberta case, then the "lying" in "lying kidnappers" rests on the undelivered message alone. Say that in one sentence.

**§4, "the danger the institutions acted on was, on his own account, real."** The lede says "real grounds for alarm"; §4 says "real danger." The record shows the former (talk of suicide, two nights without sleep, a disappearance), not the latter, and the first evaluation that morning found he did not meet criteria. Use "grounds for alarm" throughout.

**§8, "footnoted each use."** If the language-model footnotes were on work submitted to the university, then some disclosure was contemporaneous and to the institution, and the sentence "much of the rest he disclosed afterwards, to readers and not to the institution" is right but the lede's "a practice of telling the instructions that bind from the instructions that do not" should note that the disclosure practice was mixed. More substantively, see §3 below on who the right audience for the disclosure is.

## 2. Fairness to Davis

**Too hard, §4: the step the chapter does not take.** The chapter's own reconstruction supplies the strongest version of Davis's complaint and then does not state it. Between the morning finding ("does not meet criteria for … 5150") and the afternoon hold, nothing in the record as the chapter gives it shows the clinical picture changed; what changed is that Davis left a house whose terms had been set as a condition of his staying. The morning's arrangement was voluntary in form; leaving it was within the rights the evaluation had just affirmed; and the leaving was what brought the police. If the afternoon hold rested on the same facts the morning had found insufficient, plus the disappearance, then the condition of staying with his mother was a term of compulsion presented as a term of a voluntary plan, which is exactly the gap between "the rules that are written down" and "the rules that are actually enforced" that the 2017 bagel-shop note names. This does not show the hold was wrong, and the chapter's point about grounds for alarm stands. It shows why "care" that morning was experienced as the first move of a detention, and it is the place in his own case where the demand to name the force has its clearest application. The chapter should say so; a proposed insertion is below. The main agent should check whether the draft transcribes the stated grounds of the afternoon hold.

**Too hard, §7: "without supporting Davis's flat denial."** Davis wrote that Vassar "had nothing to do with causing" the February episode. The chapter faults the record for not supporting this, but no record could support a flat denial of any contribution by any correspondent during a sleepless week. The fair finding is about confidence: Davis stated a negative with more certainty than the record allows, which fits the study's recurring observation about his confidence regarding causes (Ch. 2). Rephrase in those terms rather than treating the denial as a claim that failed a test it could not pass.

**Too hard, §7, last paragraph.** The early-2021 episode the draft attributes to "the everyone-yelling thing" is a stressor from interpersonal conflict in Vassar's circle. Alexander's 2021 charge was about an ideology (psychiatrists as evil; psychosis as breaking shackles) that discouraged treatment. Conceding the first does not support the second, and the chapter should distinguish them before saying the omission was "material." It remains material to Davis's *general* selection claim, which is the right, narrower conclusion; a tightened paragraph is proposed below. Note also that Alexander's July 2022 retraction, made after interviews, already treats people like Davis as having "some other risk factor," so the omitted material would not obviously have altered the accuser's own revised conclusion. Say whether it would have.

**Too hard, §2: a test Davis did not set.** "It does not say that release required him to agree that his detention had been good for him." Davis never claimed release was conditioned on affirmation; the sentence sets a strict operationalization and then observes the record does not meet it. What "affirm it as care" amounts to in a ward is, in clinical practice, the construct of *insight* [memory]: the notes record whether the patient accepts that he is ill, and lack of insight is routinely cited as grounds for continued compulsion and medication. The chapter should ask the main agent to check whether the clinical notes Davis transcribes in the 2023 draft mention insight or his denial of illness. If they do, that is the concrete form of the affirmation demand, and the chapter has the evidence it says is missing.

**Too soft, §6: the 2023 draft holds both positions.** The chapter credits the parenthesis "(Nice people doing their jobs!)" as the memoirist restoring the individuals the theory erased. But the same draft contains the chapter's own "strongest version" of the jail passage. The draft does not move from theory to correction; it holds the honest-jail proposal and the nice-people concession side by side. The chapter should say so rather than present the parenthesis as a resolution.

**Too soft, §8: who the disclosure serves.** The 2026 post addresses readers, and the chapter notes that this is not notice to the institution. Two further things should be said. First, on Davis's own 2024 signaling account, the audience whose map a credential affects is whoever reads the credential, not the university that issues it; "If anyone has any doubts about the meaning of my Bachelor of Arts … they can read this post" addresses exactly that audience. This is a point in Davis's favor the chapter misses: by his theory the disclosure reaches the party it needs to reach. Second, and cutting the other way, the disclosure also preempts the fraud charge he says he feared, so he collects the credential and the credit for candor from the same readers. The chapter need not resolve this, but it should name both.

**"Cranky."** §5 describes Davis's mother as "frightened and cranky." If "cranky" is Davis's word, quote it; if the chapter's, cut it.

## 3. Missing objections

- **The law already names the force, in writing.** [memory] A California 5150 hold is accompanied by a written notice stating the legal grounds and the patient's rights, and the rights handbook Davis quotes is part of that apparatus. So the institution does "name the force," on paper; Davis's objection is to the conversational register of staff and of the outpatient program. The chapter's note 1 half-sees this. Making it explicit strengthens Davis's "written rules versus enforced rules" point and sharpens the question: the demand is not that the ward say it is detaining him (it did, on a form) but that its staff not simultaneously ask him to call the detention care.
- **"No procedures."** The chapter's reply (the ward administered a treatment he now endorses) meets Davis's claim only if he endorses the antipsychotic as such. If his 2021 endorsement is of a sedating regimen for sleep, on the strength of an insomnia guide, then the treatment he endorses and the treatment the ward intended may differ, and his point that psychiatry lacks procedures in the bone-setting sense survives the concession. Check what the Wellness Strategy Notes draft and the Rocks draft say about *which* drug he endorses and why.
- **The paperwork error's direction.** The false "self presented" entry describes a police transport as a voluntary arrival. The chapter is right that one copied line does not show selection for misleading. By Davis's functional account, though, what one would look for is whether errors run in the institution's favor; a single instance cannot establish a pattern, and the chapter could say that this is the test, and that the record has one data point.
- **Soft paternalism.** [memory] Feinberg's *Harm to Self* distinguishes hard paternalism from intervention to establish whether a choice is voluntary. Davis's "correct to want to avoid the psych hospital, but … not … sane" concession places his case within soft paternalism's territory, where the question is whether the intervention was proportionate to the uncertainty, not whether it was paternalist. One sentence would locate the dispute. Related: [memory] psychiatric advance directives ("Ulysses contracts") exist precisely so that "had someone I trusted handed me the same pill … I would have taken it" can be made institutionally operative. Davis's position has an institutional form he does not mention.

## 4. Scholarship left out

All [memory]; the main agent should verify before use.

- **Goffman, *Asylums* (1961).** The packet's own prompt names the sociology of total institutions, and the chapter has none. Goffman classes the mental hospital with the prison, the barracks and the boarding school as "total institutions," which is Davis's 2013 one-liner in sociological dress; his essay on "the moral career of the mental patient" describes a ward regime in which progress depends on the patient's accepting the staff's account of why he is there. This is the closest scholarly analogue to "coerces its recipients into affirming it as 'care'," and its absence is the chapter's largest gap.
- **C. S. Lewis, "The Humanitarian Theory of Punishment" (1949).** Argues that treating the compelled as patients to be cured rather than offenders to be punished is crueler than honest punishment, because it removes the limits of desert and denies the person standing as someone whose account is owed an answer. This is the Rocks passage's argument in general form, from an author Davis would not be suspected of borrowing from.
- **Lidz et al. 1995, "Perceived coercion in mental hospital admission," *Archives of General Psychiatry*,** and **Hoge et al. 1997**, the MacArthur papers the chapter dates "from memory." Fetching one abstract would turn the memory label into a citation and would let the chapter say what the Perceived Coercion Scale measures (voice, validation, respect, fairness: the chapter's "informed, heard and dealt with fairly").
- **Insight.** [memory] David 1990, "Insight and psychosis," *British Journal of Psychiatry*, introduced the clinical construct; the critical literature on "insight" as acceptance of the clinician's frame (e.g., work in the philosophy of psychiatry on insight and disagreement) is the professional counterpart of Davis's refusal to "use its language and categories." One sentence and a citation.
- **Compulsory schooling.** The chapter credits Caplan from memory. The "obedience test" idea has an older literature: [memory] Illich, *Deschooling Society* (1971); Holt, *How Children Fail* (1964), whose subject is precisely children learning to produce the teacher's checkmark rather than the answer, which is the Charles Goodhart parable; Gatto's "The Seven-Lesson Schoolteacher." The parable's "The teacher always gave him a checkmark, therefore his way … was correct" is Holt's book in a sentence. Citing Holt would also give the §8 "second reading" some external support.
- **Foucault** on school, prison and hospital as one disciplinary family would be the obvious citation for the 2013 one-liner, but it would need more than a name-check to earn its place; Goffman does the work with less.

## Proposed prose

**Proposed replacement for §5, paragraph beginning "The literature tells against the second claim."** (Addresses the unlicensed inference; keeps the point about goodwill.)

> The literature bears on the second claim less directly, and what it shows tells against Davis's remedy rather than his diagnosis. The ward's purpose is not something these studies measure; they measure what patients later say. Patients who experienced a measure as punitive, arbitrary or incompetent reported more distress from it, and Galon and Wineman list benevolence on the part of authority among the elements of procedural justice that reduce perceived coercion. None of this shows that a hold announced as punishment would be more distressing than one disguised as care, since no study compares the two, and the distress that attaches to perceived punitiveness may come from the gap between what staff said and what they did, which is Davis's own complaint. What the studies do show is that patients fared best, by their own later account, when treated with evident goodwill, and the label Davis proposes, "Society or your loved ones are sick of your antics," withdraws goodwill along with the euphemism. The candor the research supports is candor about grounds and process. It is not an admission of hostility, and nothing in the research suggests the second would be kinder than the first.

**Proposed insertion for §4, after "the danger the institutions acted on was, on his own account, real."** (Addresses the step the chapter does not take; "real" should become "grounds for alarm" in the preceding sentence.)

> One feature of the sequence, which the draft records and does not press, supports Davis's complaint about description more than the reading so far allows. Between the morning's finding that he did not meet the criteria for a hold and the afternoon's detention, the record as he gives it shows no change in the clinical picture; what changed was that he left a house whose terms had been set as a condition of his staying. [QUOTE NEEDED: the draft's account of why the police were called, or the clinical note's stated grounds for the afternoon hold, if transcribed.] If the second hold rested on the facts the first evaluation had found insufficient, plus the disappearance, then the condition of staying with his mother functioned as a term of the compulsion while being presented as a term of a voluntary plan. That is the gap between the written rule and the enforced one that the 2017 note names. It does not show that the afternoon hold was wrong; a disappearance after talk of suicide is a reasonable ground for alarm. It shows why Davis experienced the morning's care as the first move of a detention, and it is the point in his own case where the demand that compulsion be named has its clearest application.

**Proposed replacement for §7, paragraph beginning "Davis's public reply of 2021 did not include this."** (Addresses clarity and the stressor/ideology distinction.)

> Davis's public reply of 2021 did not include this, and the draft says why: "A defense lawyer has an easier job than a rationalist—if the prosecution makes a terrible case, you can just destroy it, without it being your job to worry about whether your client is separately guilty of vaguely similar crimes that the incompetent prosecution can't prove." The draft then concedes that a later episode "actually *were* the result of being on the receiving end of (what I perceived as) 'the everyone-yelling thing'" from Vassar's circle. A rebuttal of a specific charge is not obliged to list every adjacent vulnerability, and the comment said it was partial ("I don't want to derail the thread by going into the details of my own situation"). But the comment also advanced a general claim, that the correlation between knowing Vassar and psychiatric trouble was mostly selection, and the conceded episode bears on it, since an episode brought on by the circle's conduct is not selection. What the draft attributes to the circle is a stressor, not the ideology Alexander described, so the concession does not support his original charge; it qualifies Davis's. By the standard of Chapter 5 the comment was a disclosed but partial account whose omission mattered to one of its claims, and the draft supplies the rest.

**Proposed insertion for §2 or §6, on the lineage of the demand.** (Addresses the scholarship gap. Every attribution here is from memory and must be verified; the paragraph should not be used until it is.)

> The demand has a lineage Davis does not cite. Erving Goffman's *Asylums* (1961) classed the mental hospital with the prison, the barracks and the boarding school as total institutions, and described a ward regime in which a patient's progress depends on his coming to accept the staff's account of why he is there; the patient who holds to his own account is recorded as lacking insight. C. S. Lewis's "The Humanitarian Theory of Punishment" (1949) made the argument of the Rocks draft in general form: to treat the person one compels as a patient to be cured rather than an offender to be punished removes the limits that desert sets and denies him the standing of someone whose account of himself is owed an answer, so that such cure is crueler than honest punishment. Neither settles anything about a ward in 2017. They show that the demand to name the force is not an idiosyncrasy of the corpus, and that its psychiatric counterpart, the demand to show insight, has a literature of its own.
