# Review: 10-politics-race (Fable)

## Summary judgment

The chapter is well organized, reads the corpus closely, and its best sections (§2 on the triskaidekaphobia exchange and the footnote disclosure; §3 on the Schelling-point theory) are the kind of thing the study exists to do. Its two main critical arguments are weaker than its conclusion claims. The "third equilibrium" objection in §4 has, after the draft-2 concessions, been reduced to something Davis already grants, but the lede and conclusion still charge him with a "dichotomy." The empirical verdict in §5 is built on one side's summaries of the other side's work and is stated with more confidence than the Note on method entitles it to. §6's "gerrymander" charge misdescribes what Davis's sentence does, and the chapter is softer on Alexander's statement than Ch. 7 was on comparable statements by Yudkowsky. The chapter also leaves out the two bodies of scholarship most directly on its subject: the economics and philosophy of statistical discrimination (which is what Davis's *Planecrash* argument is about, under its established name) and the theory of preference falsification and norm change (which is where "preference cascade" comes from). Details follow.

## 1. The "third equilibrium" argument (§4, lede, §7)

The draft-2 summary says the third equilibrium "is no longer an objection Davis misses" and that "the real disagreement is the scope and stability of a fence around treatment." The body of §4 agrees: it records that Davis grants "always test the individual's aptitude, never use sex as a proxy" as "a fine rule" for most jobs, that he is "strongly in favor" of equality before the law, and that he floated the law/policy distinction. But the lede still says "The published review frames the choice as one between two equilibria of belief," the end of §4 says "The published review assumed an answer by treating the belief as the only fence," and the conclusion says "Its overreaches follow from a dichotomy the theory does not need. The published review treats true belief and differential treatment as a package." These are three different charges, and the body supports only the weakest of them (the published review did not raise the question the unpublished draft raised).

Two specific problems.

(a) The chapter reads the Murray review's second Schelling point as "carrying its own norm of treatment." The blockquote the chapter gives is about what appears "on shared maps," and the Pinker passage is about "how epistemology works," that is, about whether *cognition* can avoid group priors. Whether the review anywhere says that the state should condition treatment on race or sex is not shown by anything quoted. If it does not, then "treats true belief and differential treatment as a package" is the chapter's inference from silence, and the honest statement is that the review is about belief and does not discuss treatment. Check: does the *Human Diversity* review make any claim about law or policy, or is "treatment" imported from the 2022 *Planecrash* comment?

(b) The paragraph beginning "Davis's own coordination argument bears on such a rule" hedges itself into near-vacuity. It says the Schelling logic supports "never" as a fence, then that state conditioning is "not one trade-off but many," then that "salience is only part of what keeps a rule in place," and ends by establishing that the fence is "available," which Davis has already granted two paragraphs earlier. The derivation does a page of work to arrive where Davis is. The paragraph that follows, on the *Facing Reality* draft, is where the real question lives (can a treatment fence hold without the belief?), and it gets one paragraph and the verdict "unknown."

Davis's strongest argument on the stability question is under-reconstructed. The draft's "unstated premise that it would lead to, you know, *actual* equality" supplies a mechanism, not just a worry: under a treatment fence plus an equality belief, persistent disparities in outcomes can only be explained by ongoing discrimination, which generates pressure to breach the fence from the other side (outcome-based remedies). That is the hereditarian political argument since at least Murray, and [memory] it is the standard way the disparate-impact doctrine is said to convert a treatment rule into an outcome rule. The chapter should state it, because it is what makes the *Facing Reality* question hard rather than merely open, and because it is an argument *inside* Davis's equilibrium framing.

On the other side, the chapter's "rule against conditioning on certain categories... adopted because the procedure's operator is powerful and not trusted" is one reason for a fence; the literature has a better one that does not require distrust. [memory] Coate and Loury (1993, *American Economic Review*, "Will Affirmative-Action Policies Eliminate Negative Stereotypes?") and the statistical-discrimination literature descending from Arrow and Phelps show that acting on an accurate group prior can produce a self-confirming equilibrium: if employers discount a group's signals, members of the group under-invest in the signal, and the prior becomes true. That is a Bayesian-consistent reason for the fence, and it meets Davis on his own terms (equilibrium selection), where "distrust of the decider" does not. [memory] Schauer's *Profiles, Probabilities, and Stereotypes* (2003) is the philosophical treatment of exactly the chapter's point that rules against using accurate generalizations are rules, not epistemic claims, and his examples (rules of evidence, age limits) are the chapter's "rules of evidence" analogy. Neither is cited. "Statistical discrimination" is also the term the chapter should use at least once: Davis's "what we call discrimination... is actually just Bayesian reasoning" is a restatement of the statistical/taste-based distinction, which has been in economics since the 1970s, and a reader who knows that literature will wonder why the chapter treats the point as new.

tailcalled's causal criterion (last paragraph of §4) is handled fairly, and the reply the chapter constructs for Davis is sound. One small addition: [memory] the "causally downstream" idea is Borsboom, Mellenbergh and van Heerden's (2004) causal theory of measurement validity, which tailcalled credits tentatively; the chapter could say so.

## 2. The race and IQ verdict (§5)

The chapter's verdict has two parts: "the direct evidence... and the selection test of 2021 tell against a large genetic contribution and cannot exclude a small one," and "'Obviously correct' is not defensible." The first is stated with more confidence than the reading supports; the second rests on a reading of a sentence whose context is not shown.

**The reading.** The Note on method says the hereditarian primary literature (Rushton and Jensen 2005; the admixture studies) was not read, that Bird was read in abstract, introduction and conclusion, that Winegard was read in abstract and opening, and that the Turkheimer piece is a *Vox* article. The plan called for Martin et al. on polygenic-score portability, Harden's book or its reviews, Dickens and Flynn 2001, Cofnas 2020, Warne, and a critical review of *Human Diversity*; none of these appears. So the chapter has read the environmentalist side's reviews and the environmentalist side's summary of the hereditarian side. That is the method the brief warns against, in mirror image. Either the reading should be done, or the verdict should be scaled to the reading: "the direct evidence the critics cite is old and weak; the one genomic test read does not support the selection argument; the hereditarian primary case was not examined." "Tell against a large genetic contribution" is not something the chapter's sources establish: Bird's result bears on the natural-selection argument for an EA-polygenic-score difference, which the chapter itself says "is not an estimate of every possible genetic contribution," and Nisbett 2012's "no new direct evidence" is a claim about 2012.

**One-sidedness in the rebuttals.** "The arguments Davis rehearses are answered in this literature" should be "contested." Each of the three environmentalist arguments the chapter gives has a hereditarian reply the chapter does not mention. The Flynn effect: [memory] Wicherts et al. (2004, *Intelligence*) found that Flynn gains are not measurement-invariant across cohorts, while [memory] the black–white gap has generally been found invariant, which is the hereditarian ground for saying the two are not the same kind of difference; Flynn himself argued at length against this inference, so the point is live, not settled. Adoption: [memory] the Minnesota Transracial Adoption Study (Scarr and Weinberg; Weinberg, Scarr and Waldman 1992 follow-up) is the hereditarians' adoption evidence and the environmentalists' explanandum; the chapter's "adoption into better-off homes raises IQ by 12 to 18 points" is about within-group adoption gains, which does not by itself answer the between-group question. The SES-control argument is answered well enough. If the chapter is going to say "answered," it must engage the replies; if it does not want to, "contested" is the word.

**"Minority opinion."** "Defensible as a minority opinion" asserts a distribution of expert opinion the chapter has not established. The only survey evidence it mentions is the one Turkheimer discounts (response rate "around five percent"), and that survey's authors claim the opposite distribution. [memory] The survey is probably Rindermann, Becker and Coyle (2020, *Intelligence*; earlier version 2016), and I do not recall its response rate being five percent; it should be named and the rate taken from the paper, not from a *Vox* article. Without a source, "minority" should go.

**"Obviously correct."** The chapter concedes that "the label is the newspaper's, used with irony, and the position is Murray's with the standard caveats, which the post lists." If "racist *like Charles Murray*" is ironic and the position is hedged, then "obviously correct position" may modify the *stance* (that sensible people will reach Murray's hedged view in the course of thinking about it), which is a claim about reasoning trajectories tied to the deception charge, not a first-order claim that the gap is obviously partly genetic. The chapter's bridge to the first-order claim is a footnote ("how much do you want to bet on that?"), which signals confidence but not obviousness, and whose context the chapter does not give. The hardening may be rhetorical (a more belligerent sentence) rather than epistemic (a higher credence). Both are worth saying, but they are different findings, and "not defensible" is a verdict on the second. Check: quote enough of the "Agreeing With Stalin" passage to show what "the obviously correct position" refers to, and what the footnote bets on and at what odds.

**Internal tension with §3.** The Schelling-point theory explains why people hold egalitarian beliefs "where, on Davis's view, the evidence tells against them." §5 then finds that on race the evidence does not settle the question. If so, the hedged egalitarian belief about race ("no good evidence of a genetic cause") may be held *on* the evidence, and the theory's explanandum partly disappears for that case. (It may still apply to psychological sex differences, where [memory] the evidence for some differences is much stronger.) The chapter does not notice that its own verdict in §5 weakens the premise of the theory it credits in §3, and it should say so; it is the kind of cross-section observation the Conclusion's "the thesis frame does not decide the empirical question" invites.

**Ch. 5's standard applied to §3.** The Schelling-point theory is a functional explanation of an orthodoxy: the belief is held because of what it does, not because of evidence. That is a motive attribution at the scale of a culture, and Ch. 5's attribution problem applies to it as much as to attributions to individuals. The chapter credits the theory for "not imputing stupidity or bad faith," which is true, but it does impute function, and nothing in the review (as quoted) tests the functional claim against the alternative that the belief is held on the evidence as its holders read it. The chapter should apply its own standard here, or say why it does not.

**Smaller points.** "Which is rarer in writing on this subject from Davis's side than from the other" is an unsourced generalization about a literature; cut it. The *Vox* piece is an op-ed; the chapter should say so rather than treating it as parallel to Nisbett et al. 2012. The works-cited entry says "Dickens & Flynn (2006)" while the plan had Dickens and Flynn 2001 (a different paper, the heritability–environment feedback model); check which one Nisbett et al. summarize for the 5.5 points. The nested quotation in the Nisbett sentence ("““There is not much...") is mangled. "The the next Schelling point" inside the Davis blockquote: if it is in the original it needs [sic] or silent correction by the study's convention; check.

## 3. The charge against Alexander (§6)

The chapter's category argument misdescribes what Davis's sentence does. Davis's "Gettier attack" claim is about *the newspaper's* assertion: the *Times* said Alexander is a racist like Murray; that assertion, read as its readers would read it, was true of Alexander; the evidence the paper gave was bogus. Davis is not drawing a category; he is reporting that someone else's category applies. The chapter's objection ("A category drawn to include both a person who hates a group and a person who accepts a psychometric estimate... predicts badly") is an objection to the *Times's* category, and if it is a noncentral fallacy it is the paper's. What the chapter can say against Davis is narrower and is already in the paragraph: by calling the charge "essentially correct" without stating the definition, Davis lends the word's condemnation to a content he thinks correct, and a newspaper reader will take the condemnation. That is a claim about communicative effect, in Ch. 5's terms (what the sentence is optimized to make a reader believe), not about category misuse "by the account of categories that Davis defended... for a decade." The two should not be run together, and the "gerrymander" word (in the summary) should go.

The chapter is softer on Alexander's statement than Ch. 7 was on Yudkowsky's. Alexander called the *Times's* claim "a weirdly brazen type of falsehood" and said it implied he agreed with Murray's "offensive views on race." If Alexander holds a hedged hereditarian view (which the chapter says the essays and the 2014 email support), then the statement is not merely "a newspaper's inference was invalid," which the chapter's charitable gloss makes it; it is a sentence whose effect on readers is the belief that Alexander does not share Murray's views on race. By Ch. 5's functional test, which Ch. 7 applied to Yudkowsky's 2018 clause, that is a case of not-technically-lying, and "offensive views on race" is the same equivocation on the word the chapter charges Davis with, run in the other direction. The chapter should apply the same test to both men. As it stands, it gives Alexander a reading ("a person may find a hypothesis plausible and also think it right to say that a newspaper's inference was invalid") that it would not have given Yudkowsky, and this is a cross-chapter inconsistency a hostile reader will find.

The conclusion's "a related slide, from a stated conditional to a definition given only by italics" implies chronology. The "crypto-sexists" conditional is the memoir's account of a Facebook comment written the week of the article; the Gettier sentence is in the same memoir installment about the same week. These are two retrospective statements about the same days, not a slide over time. "Inconsistency" is defensible; "slide" is not. Check: when was each sentence written, and are both in "Agreeing With Stalin"?

## 4. The speech arguments (§2)

This is the chapter's best section, and the reading of the footnote disclosure as "the norm of accounting applied to part (c) itself" is right and important for the book. Two points.

The Bostrom symmetry claim needs checking at both ends: that Davis's heckler's-veto post attributes the spoiling case to Bostrom, Douglas and Sandberg, and that [memory] "The Unilateralist's Curse and the Case for a Principle of Conformity" (2016) actually defines the curse to cover a single agent's power to *prevent* an initiative. My recollection is that the paper treats the undertaking case and mentions the symmetric case only briefly; if the symmetry is Davis's extension rather than their definition, the chapter should say so.

Free-speech scholarship is absent. The Bayes-structural argument (speech as information plus social control; rules against the second gerrymander the first) is [memory] a version of the "chilling effect" and "slippery slope" arguments in Schauer's *Free Speech: A Philosophical Enquiry* (1982) and his "Slippery Slopes" (1985, *Harvard Law Review*), and the "heckler's veto" is [memory] Kalven's term (*The Negro and the First Amendment*, 1965). Davis's "nonsense-math in large areas of psychology, sociology, political science, history" is a claim about ideological distortion of fields, on which there is an empirical literature ([memory] Duarte et al. 2015, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, "Political Diversity Will Improve Social Psychological Science," and its commentaries) that would let the chapter say whether steven0461's count or Davis's entanglement claim is the better-supported picture, instead of leaving "neither side settled that."

The chapter is mostly clean. ## Proposed prose

**Proposed replacement for §4, paragraph beginning "Davis's own coordination argument bears on such a rule".** Problem addressed: the paragraph argues at length for a conclusion Davis has already granted, and qualifies itself into vagueness; the replacement states the point once and moves the weight to the question that is actually open.

> Davis himself grants the rule for most jobs, so the question is not whether a fence around treatment can be drawn but how far it extends and whether it holds. The coordination argument of "Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point" (Chapters 4 and 5) bears on the second question. On a continuous trade-off with no salient intermediate point, only the extremes are stable, and "never use the category as a proxy" is a salient extreme in a way that "use it a little" is not. A legal rule of that form does not require anyone to believe that groups are identical; it requires the state not to act on the belief, at a stated cost in predictive accuracy, and it is the kind of disclosed, costed departure from optimal inference that the norm of accounting permits. Pinker's sentence, read as such a rule rather than as a claim about how epistemology works, describes this arrangement, and Davis's objection does not reach it. What the argument does not show is that the rule's salience will keep it in place once the belief that seemed to justify it is gone, which is the question Davis put to himself in the unpublished *Facing Reality* review and answered "I don't know."

**Proposed addition to §4, after the *Facing Reality* paragraph.** Problem addressed: the chapter gives one reason for a fence (distrust of the decider) and omits the established literature on statistical discrimination, which supplies a reason inside Davis's own framing. All citations are from memory and must be verified before use.

> Davis's claim that what Americans call discrimination "is actually just Bayesian reasoning" has a name in economics, statistical discrimination, and a literature that has considered since the 1970s why it might be prohibited even where the group prior is accurate [memory: Phelps 1972; Arrow 1973]. The strongest reason is not distrust of the decider. Where employers act on a group prior, members of the group have less reason to invest in the signals that would distinguish them, and the prior confirms itself: a self-fulfilling equilibrium in which the statistics are accurate because they are acted on [memory: Coate and Loury 1993]. That is an argument of exactly the kind Davis favors, about which equilibrium a population settles into, and it gives the fence a rationale that does not depend on anyone pretending not to have a prior. The philosophical defense of rules that forbid acting on accurate generalizations, with the rules of evidence as the standard example, is Schauer's [memory: *Profiles, Probabilities, and Stereotypes*, 2003]. [QUOTE NEEDED: Davis's "born of distrust in reasoning in an adversarial environment" sentence, already quoted above, for contrast.]

**Proposed replacement for §5, final verdict paragraph, first three sentences.** Problem addressed: the verdict is stronger than the reading disclosed in the Note on method.

> The study's verdict, on the sources read, is narrower than either side's. The direct evidence that the environmentalist reviews cite is old and limited, as the reviews themselves say; the one genomic test read for this study does not support the argument from natural selection on which the recent hereditarian case most depends; and the hereditarian primary literature, including the admixture and test-structure arguments assembled by Rushton and Jensen, was not examined here and is known through its critics. On that basis the cause of the gap is unknown, a genetic contribution of some size is neither established nor excluded, and the arguments Davis rehearsed in 2020 are contested rather than answered. "Probably partly genetic" is a probability judgment the evidence does not settle, defensible when held as loosely as Davis held it in 2020. Whether "obviously correct" in 2024 is a higher credence or a more belligerent sentence is discussed above; as a credence it is not defensible on this evidence.

**Proposed replacement for §6, the sentences from "It does not show that the public statement was false" to the end of that sentence.** Problem addressed: the chapter applies a charitable reading to Alexander that Ch. 7 did not extend to Yudkowsky.

> Whether the public statement deceived is a question for the functional test of Chapter 5, applied in Chapter 7 to Yudkowsky's statements of 2018 and 2021. Alexander's statement said that the article implied he agreed with Murray's "offensive views on race" and called the implication "a weirdly brazen type of falsehood." It did not say which of Murray's views he rejected. A reader of the statement would conclude that Alexander does not share Murray's views on race; if the essays and the 2014 email are read as Davis reads them, that conclusion is false, and the sentence that produced it was true only on a reading of "offensive views" that the reader was not given. By the standard the study applied to Yudkowsky, that is a disclosed-direction accounting at best and an optimized misimpression at worst, and the same equivocation on the word that this section charges Davis with, run in the other direction. What the leaked email cannot show is a motive: a person may believe a hypothesis plausible, think it right to say a newspaper's inference was invalid, and also prefer that readers not learn what he thinks.
