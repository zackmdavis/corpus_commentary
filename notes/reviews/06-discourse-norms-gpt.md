# Review of Chapter 6: Against Collaborative Truthseeking: Norms for Discourse, and the Achmiz Affair

The chapter reconstructs a substantial dispute and gives Davis’s critics unusually sustained attention. Its strongest passages distinguish functional accusations from evidence for those accusations, acknowledge Davis’s corrections, and turn his selection-effects argument back on the evidence for a harsh intellectual culture. The Achmiz discussion also usefully separates the justification for moderation from the accuracy of the administrator’s account.

The main revision needed concerns the chapter’s own thesis. It repeatedly treats disclosure as sufficient to answer Davis’s objections to accommodation and moderation. Disclosure can correct a misleading impression about a policy; it does not establish that the policy preserves inquiry, distributes costs fairly, or honors an institution’s stated purpose. Several verdicts depend on moving between those propositions without argument. This makes the chapter too charitable to the moderators in some places and insufficiently charitable to Davis in others.

All assessments below concern the packet’s presentation. Claims about the underlying posts require checking.

## 1. Disclosure does less work than the chapter assigns it

The central overstatement appears in §5:

> “It is the norm of accounting applied to a community: a filter is legitimate if it is stated and its costs are tracked.”

Neither the preceding reconstruction of “Hazards of Selection Effects on Approved Information” nor the chapter’s argument establishes this sufficient condition. An openly acknowledged filter could systematically exclude the best objections. Tracking that damage would establish its existence, not legitimate it.

The same problem governs §6:

> “By the argument of ‘Hazards’ that is a serious charge only if the filter is large and unacknowledged, and Habryka acknowledged his at length.”

Why must the filter be *both* large and unacknowledged? A disclosed ban could have large effects; a single ban could establish a precedent that changes many participants’ conduct. A community might accurately advertise a selection process that nevertheless makes its conclusions unreliable. The relevant counterfactual includes anticipatory silence and changed recruitment, not merely the contributions of the banned person.

The Salamon exchange needs corresponding revision:

> “But Salamon’s readers are not taking her reports at face value; she has told them her filter.”

This generalizes from the readers of one comment to her audience generally. More fundamentally, knowing that criticism is costly does not tell readers which accusations were withheld, how selection varied by target, or how to adjust their beliefs. Disclosure may make an audience appropriately uncertain without restoring the evidence needed for a judgment. The distinction from Chapter 5 between distortion and missing information remains useful, but missing information can defeat an institution’s purpose.

Davis’s strongest reply would therefore be: acknowledged suppression is preferable to concealed suppression, yet still incompatible with the epistemic service being promised. The chapter needs to establish whether he makes that argument before interpreting his position as an inability to recognize disclosed exceptions.

**Proposed replacement for §6, paragraph beginning “But Salamon’s readers…”**

*This addresses the inference from disclosure to adequate epistemic repair.*

> Salamon discloses that the cost of public accusation affects what she says. That disclosure gives readers a reason to treat her silence cautiously, but it does not reveal the accusations she withholds or their evidential strength. Davis’s coin-room example identifies a real loss: reports selected against unwelcome results cannot be interpreted as an unselected record. What the example does not establish is that a truth-telling norm requires reporting every result regardless of cost. The dispute concerns how much information the norm requires its adherents to supply, and under what conditions withholding it remains compatible with their stated commitments.

## 2. The anti-extortion argument is narrowed without justification

In §4:

> “Its extension needs the premise that tactful phrasing distorts the map.”

An anti-extortion argument need not require this premise. Davis could argue that concessions reward escalating demands even when each concession preserves factual content. He could also argue that tact consumes attention or makes criticism depend on satisfying the target’s preferences. Those arguments require evidence about incentives and costs, but they are not refuted by showing that tact conveys the same proposition.

The opening quotation from “Don’t Negotiate With Terrorist Memeplexes” itself concerns trying not to say hurtful things. That can involve truthful silence, not merely falsehood or pretending not to notice. The chapter should check whether it has retrospectively narrowed the 2018 policy to fit its proposed distinction between concealed distortion and disclosed accommodation.

Conversely, the chapter is too soft when it says:

> “The argument is sound where the concession demanded distorts the map.”

Even there, the anti-extortion inference requires additional premises: that accommodation reinforces future demands, that refusal improves the relevant equilibrium, and that the immediate costs do not outweigh that benefit. A reason against distortion does not by itself establish this particular strategic argument.

The astridain exchange supports a narrower criticism. If Davis calls improved reception “deception” of an offended subsystem, he needs to explain why that subsystem’s response deserves protection. Does it accurately register information, or mistake a correction for an attack? Why should triggering it count as epistemic fidelity? This directly challenges his extension of functional deception without assuming that all requests for tact are harmless.

Likewise, thanking Arnold demonstrates that Davis recognizes some benefits of managing reception. It contradicts a universal prohibition on accommodation only if he actually holds one. The chapter already quotes his explicit concession that goodwill belongs in many successful discourse procedures. Those concessions must constrain the final characterization of his theory.

## 3. The standard of “derivation” shifts across §2

The chapter moves among three requirements:

- A guideline must follow from principles of inference.
- A guideline must have an empirical explanation of how it improves inquiry.
- A guideline must say what purpose it serves.

These requirements differ substantially. “The demand for derivation survives these replies, since it asks only that a guideline say what it is for” quietly substitutes the weakest for the strongest.

Byrnes’s objection therefore needs a verdict. The motor analogy appears to conflate constraints that every physical process satisfies with standards that some reasoning processes violate. Davis’s underlying request for justification may survive, while the analogy fails. Say so if the full post supports that reading.

Nor is Bensinger’s inability to supply a mechanism evidence that goodwill serves something other than accuracy. A practice might have comparative empirical support before its mechanism is understood. The chapter should ask what evidence suffices to justify a guideline under uncertainty, rather than accept deduction as the default standard.

The conclusion reproduces the false division:

> “where it serves comfort, retention, or civility instead it should say so”

Retention and civility can serve accuracy by preserving access to testimony, objections, and sustained attention. The chapter explains this repeatedly, then reinstates the opposition in its verdict.

The treatment of “‘Justice, Cherryl’” also stops too early. Even if everyone wants to be seen as right, it does not follow that collaborative inquiry is nonexistent or that those claiming to practice it are lying. People can have mixed motives and undertake practices that constrain them. The problem is an invalid transition, not merely an unsupported premise.

**Proposed replacement for §2, paragraph beginning “These essays set a test…”**

*This distinguishes epistemic justification from derivation and avoids opposing retention to truth.*

> These essays ask proponents of discourse norms to explain how their rules improve inquiry. That demand is justified, but its satisfaction need not take the form of deduction from laws of inference. A rule may help because it preserves attention, makes correction easier to accept, or retains contributors whose evidence would otherwise be lost. Such claims require empirical support. Davis’s objection is strongest when a contingent practice is presented as an unquestionable requirement of rationality, or when its costs are concealed.

## 4. The disagreement argument needs more precise premises

Section 3 introduces Aumann as proving that:

> “honest Bayesian reasoners with common priors cannot agree to disagree.”

[memory] Aumann’s theorem concerns common knowledge of posterior probabilities under a common prior; “honesty” is not an independently specified psychological condition in the theorem. The chapter should state the formal conditions before discussing Cowen and Hanson’s interpretation. Otherwise it imports the conclusion about honesty into the theorem’s description.

The sentence “Persistence is evidence that something besides truth-seeking is going on” also needs qualification. Persistent disagreement can reflect failures of the idealization without revealing a competing goal. The chapter mentions permissive evidence and prior differences but gives too little attention to unequal background knowledge, misunderstood propositions, uncertain expertise, or the cost of making evidence mutually accessible.

The treatment of Aaronson does not settle this. An author’s conclusion that complexity is not a major barrier is relevant, but the chapter should explain the bound, assumptions, and relation to human conversational costs. A worst-case upper bound does not establish that actual conversations require that much computation; neither does a favorable complexity classification establish practical affordability. The packet renders Pace’s figure as “236,864,” while the summary gives \(2^{36,864}\). Check both the mathematics and the rendered typography.

The contrast between “vanity” and “interest” is also too sharp:

> “only interest makes a disagreement a disguised negotiation.”

Protecting intellectual standing can itself be an interest over which people bargain. The stronger criticism is that persistence does not identify which process produced it, nor establish that psychoanalytic negotiation is the appropriate remedy.

Finally, assuming good faith needs disaggregation. It can mean believing the other person well motivated, withholding accusations, applying a higher evidential threshold, or responding only to the argument. Ngo’s defense of restraint need not require inaccurate beliefs about motivation. This distinction would connect the chapter more precisely to Chapter 5’s false-positive/false-negative problem.

## 5. Trust is not exhausted by belief and goodwill

The chapter says Davis’s auditor definition:

> “meets the strongest form of the institutional objection”

It meets the need to define reliable testimony. It does not yet meet the need to produce and sustain it. An auditor counterfactual describes what trustworthy reporting would achieve; it does not explain how a community economizes on checking or maintains incentives to report failures.

Similarly, the question “whether trust should ever outrun the evidence” misdescribes some of Sabien’s position. A charitable interpretation of an ambiguous remark can be supported by the history of the relationship. Declining to demand repayment or immediate proof can be a practical commitment without changing one’s factual beliefs. The chapter should distinguish judgments about reliability, willingness to accept vulnerability, and obligations between collaborators.

Sabien’s sweeping personal verdict also deserves explicit criticism. “He has never seen them” and “beyond his comprehension” exceed the evidence supplied here. Davis’s adoption of that description might be sincere, ironic, or an argumentative concession; the reply must be checked before treating it as autobiographical confirmation. Even sincere agreement would not establish the claim’s breadth.

The chapter can retain Sabien’s substantive objection while rejecting his authority to diagnose the limits of Davis’s experience. That would apply the same standards to critics’ psychological attributions that it applies to Davis’s.

## 6. The moderation analysis needs standards beyond transparency

The chapter repeatedly concludes that neither side measured costs. That is a useful evidential limitation, but it risks flattening differences in responsibility and evidence. Administrators must sometimes decide under uncertainty; critics need not supply a complete alternative institution before identifying a false claim. Neither consideration gives either party immunity from scrutiny.

The following questions would make §6 more discriminating:

- What conduct was prohibited, and how clearly had Achmiz been warned?
- What evidence connects that conduct to the alleged losses?
- Why were author bans, rate limits, or narrower restrictions inadequate?
- Were comparable cases treated consistently?
- What opportunities existed for correction, appeal, or reconsideration?

These are questions for the supplied record, not assumptions that every forum must adopt a particular procedure.

“Habryka had published case law” does not establish that Achmiz had adequate prior notice. Nor does describing him as “a commenter working at the edge of its rules” neutrally summarize the dispute: it suggests strategic circumvention that needs evidence.

The purported tension between the reputational flag and moderation also needs argument. Owning one’s judgment of an author does not automatically confer a right to express it in every venue or require the author to answer. Davis’s stronger case would concern selective protection from scrutiny or a conflict with LessWrong’s advertised purpose.

The factual audit needs cleaner separation. A missing verb may explain one ambiguous sentence without vindicating the underlying list. Ngo’s assessment of the implication of “false claims” concerns an accusation’s pragmatics, not whether the factual claims were accurate. Present the original wording, the repair, and the remaining evidence separately.

Finally, “The market clears” deserves a stronger answer. The chapter has identified no mechanism ensuring that a critic bears the costs imposed on respondents, moderators, and readers. Conversely, administrators’ estimates of those costs can protect favored authors. That reciprocal problem is more informative than concluding that neither side counted departures.

## 7. Two passages treat Davis unfairly; another is too forgiving

The “cowards” paragraph calls Sabien “right in substance” because Davis criticizes an author who does not want others to read corrections. But that is a conditional description of purpose. It is not equivalent to saying that every author ban expresses that purpose. Davis can consistently support the feature while condemning some uses. Check the surrounding exchange before treating Sabien’s generalization as substantially vindicated.

The “Lady Justice” paragraph introduces a loaded claim about women and a simulated real person without showing what it establishes about the ban. Its placement suggests explanatory significance the chapter does not defend. Either explain its relevance to Davis’s theory of discourse and acknowledge the evidential limits of an unpublished role-play, or remove it. “He judged it unpublishable for that reason” also needs checking: the quoted concern about intent to injure does not necessarily explain the publication decision.

By contrast, “Listening Disorders” receives too much credit for acknowledging costs. Stating that contempt makes people’s days worse does not establish that the policy is justified or adequately costed. Its empirical premise remains unsupported by the chapter’s account. Disclosure deserves credit as candor, while the policy still requires assessment.

## 8. Scholarship that would sharpen the argument

The chapter should integrate a few targeted sources rather than expand its bibliography indiscriminately. All descriptions in this section are from memory and require verification.

- **[memory] Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, chapter 11:** the knowledge norm of assertion would help distinguish norms governing what a speaker may assert from procedures intended to improve collective inquiry. Davis’s broad appeal to inference laws currently moves between these levels.
- **[memory] Karen Jones, “Trust as an Affective Attitude” (1996), and Annette Baier, “Trust and Antitrust” (1986):** both offer resources for distinguishing trust from prediction of reliable behavior. They would directly test whether the barometer analogy answers Sabien.
- **[memory] Helen Longino, *Science as Social Knowledge* and *The Fate of Knowledge*:** the conditions involving venues for criticism, uptake, public standards, and tempered equality concern the organization of critical interaction. The chapter’s single concluding sentence neither presents nor tests them adequately.
- **[memory] C. Thi Nguyen, “Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles” (2020):** the distinction between missing voices and structures that discredit outside testimony could sharpen “Hazards,” especially its criticism of trusted summaries of hostile sources.
- **[memory] Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990):** monitoring, graduated sanctions, and accessible conflict resolution offer useful comparative questions about moderation. They should not be imported as a ready-made verdict on a forum.
- **[memory] Kate Klonick, “The New Governors” (2018):** its account of private platform governance would help separate institutional authority, procedural legitimacy, and substantive moderation policy.
- **[memory] Victoria McGeer, “Civilizing Blame” (2013):** this offers a way to examine criticism as an intervention in another person’s agency, beyond choosing between accurate description and emotional accommodation.

The existing sentence that Longino’s norms “require the pricing Vaniver describes” is unsupported. A concern with community composition does not entail that particular vocabulary or method of evaluation.

## 9. Structure, overlap, and prose

Sections 4 and 6 contain most of the chapter’s argument but too many distinct tasks. Give trust and the anti-extortion argument separate subsections; divide the Achmiz section into grounds for the ban, factual disputes, and institutional legitimacy. Trim the procession of companion essays in §2 to make room.

The chapter’s distinctive contribution should be how norms distribute the work and costs of criticism. Chapter 4 already argues that disclosed departures need not be defections; Chapter 5 develops functional attribution and selective reporting; Chapter 7 applies both to leadership. Repeating their conclusions here is less valuable than testing their limits in an institution.

The plan promises engagement with Longino’s four conditions and the strongest case for community governance. Those remain underdeveloped. Conversely, the unpublished role-play and the end-of-section inventory of concessions distract from the chapter’s governing question.

Several recurrent phrases substitute classification for analysis: “That is the norm of accounting,” “That is the coordination argument,” “the strongest essay,” “the right question,” and “the historical evidence cuts the same way.” Reduce these verdict sentences. Show which premise is supported and which inference fails.

“Priced,” “costed,” and “measured” also need consistent meanings. Sometimes “priced” means merely acknowledged; elsewhere it means quantitatively estimated or institutionally imposed. The conclusion cannot credit Davis with pricing his contempt while faulting everyone else for offering no measurements unless it distinguishes those standards.
