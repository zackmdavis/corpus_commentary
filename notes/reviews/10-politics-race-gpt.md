# Review: The Egalitarian Schelling Point

The chapter’s strongest material concerns Davis’s acknowledgment that egalitarian beliefs may serve legitimate political purposes, and his disclosure of the personal commitments behind his resistance to speech restrictions. Its principal weaknesses are in the arguments built around those concessions. The proposed “third equilibrium” is neither established as an equilibrium nor clearly excluded by Davis’s preferred arrangement. The empirical section sometimes answers a stronger hereditarian claim than the one it attributes to Davis. The Alexander section, meanwhile, makes unsupported assumptions about what *racist* means and draws an invalid inference from Davis’s admission of self-interest.

These problems affect the chapter’s opening and conclusion, and the conclusions already carried into the book’s Introduction and Conclusion. They warrant revision before polishing. All observations below concern the packet; proposed interpretations of the underlying posts require checking.

## 1. The “third equilibrium” needs reconstruction

The central objection begins: “The theory has a gap, and the gap is in the menu.” But the menu appears to mix different kinds of alternatives. A commitment to truthful public discussion is a norm governing inquiry and communication. Equal treatment is a norm governing action. They can coexist without constituting a third alternative to either empirical egalitarianism or truthful discussion.

The chapter needs to establish that Davis’s “truth” arrangement excludes enforceable constraints on treatment. Its own evidence complicates that attribution: he endorses individual aptitude tests for most jobs, considers a distinction between constitutional principles and policy, and is “strongly in favor” of equality before the law. Those concessions cannot merely be acknowledged after the objection has been declared successful. They may mean that Davis already recognizes the proposed arrangement and doubts its durability.

The *Facing Reality* draft is especially consequential. The chapter says it “states the difficulty for the third equilibrium more plainly than any critic.” That suggests the strongest disagreement concerns whether equal treatment can survive altered public beliefs, rather than whether Davis can distinguish belief from treatment. Publication status matters to what readers encountered, but an unpublished acknowledgment remains evidence about his position.

There is also an internal tension in the book’s thesis. The chapter credits Davis’s choice of truthful discussion, justified by its consequences, as part (b) displacing part (c). Yet an exceptionless speech rule can itself have consequentialist justification. Declaring one’s stake and acknowledging competing goods does not establish that exceptions are permitted. Specify what rule Davis relaxes here. Otherwise this is evidence that accounting accompanies his coordination argument, not that it supersedes it.

**Proposed replacement for §4, paragraph beginning “The theory has a gap…”**

*This addresses the conflation of inquiry norms with treatment norms and incorporates Davis’s concessions into the objection.*

> Davis’s argument leaves a question about the relation between public belief and political restraint. His preferred norm of truthful discussion could coexist with rules limiting the use of group membership in public decisions. His concessions concerning aptitude tests and equality before the law suggest that he accepts some such limits. The disagreement is therefore about their scope and stability: which decisions should those rules govern, what costs would they impose, and would people continue to uphold them after abandoning the empirical beliefs that Davis thinks helped sustain them? His unpublished review of *Facing Reality* recognizes this last question explicitly.

## 2. Salience does not establish stability or justice

“Davis’s own coordination argument is the strongest case for such a rule” overstates what the chapter demonstrates. Even if “never” is salient, salience alone does not show that a prohibition is stable, enforceable, beneficial, or superior to other salient rules. The chapter supplies no account of incentives, sanctions, monitoring, or deviations. Its opening promise that Davis’s argument supports the treatment rule “better than” his rejection therefore goes unfulfilled.

The claim that state conditioning on race or sex forms one continuous trade-off is also questionable. Conscription, employment selection, medical provision, and the collection of statistics present different decisions. A categorical prohibition across them is not self-evidently the relevant extreme. Nor are “equal treatment,” “equality before the law,” and excluding particular predictive inputs interchangeable principles.

[Memory] Schelling’s *The Strategy of Conflict*, Lewis’s *Convention*, and Bicchieri’s *The Grammar of Society* offer distinctions among focal solutions, conventions, and norms sustained by expectations. The chapter needs those distinctions more than additional uses of “Schelling point.” O’Connor’s models, as described here, explain how unequal conventions can arise; they do not by themselves establish that empirical egalitarianism prevents conflict, that it functions through false belief, or that truthful discussion would destabilize it.

The same caution applies to “the study’s thesis holds [that a disclosed departure] is not a defection.” That is a conclusion requiring argument here, not an available premise. Disclosure can clarify a departure while leaving others opposed to it. A declared discriminatory rule would not become acceptable merely by stating its costs; a declared nondiscrimination rule would not become stable merely by stating its informational losses.

The sentence “some decisions will be worse than a Bayesian would make” also grants too much. Worse by which objective? A decision may sacrifice predictive accuracy while improving the institution’s performance on its actual objectives, including fair treatment and resistance to abuse. Bayesian belief formation does not uniquely determine a decision without utilities and constraints.

The analogy to prior convictions should be qualified or replaced. [Memory] Their admissibility depends on jurisdiction, purpose, and procedural circumstances; they are not simply information that juries categorically cannot hear.

## 3. The causal objection from tailcalled is overstated

The chapter endorses this distinction:

> “if your measurement is causally downstream from your variable of interest, it’s treating people as individuals, while if it’s confounded with or causally upstream from your variable of interest, it’s stereotyping.”

This is an interesting proposal, but the chapter’s explanation—that “a downstream measurement captures the exceptions that a correlated category misses”—does not follow without further assumptions. A downstream measurement can be noisy, confounded, or manipulated. An upstream cause can be specific to the individual. Consider a person’s training history: its causal position does not make using it equivalent to stereotyping.

Davis’s best response would distinguish the information added by a measurement from its causal direction. Even an informative individual test may leave a group prior relevant to a posterior estimate. To defeat that response, the chapter needs either a sufficiently informative measurement or an independent normative reason to exclude the prior.

Present tailcalled’s proposal as a challenge about what counts as individual evidence, not as a settled causal refinement that “survives Bayesian criticism.” Check the full April 2021 *Human Diversity* thread before asserting that “the corpus does not answer it.”

## 4. The empirical verdict needs claims of matching strength

The chapter appropriately distinguishes individual heritability from the causes of group differences. It also acknowledges Davis’s disclaimer of expertise. But its final assessment moves among several different propositions:

- Some genetic contribution to the observed gap is probable.
- A substantial genetic contribution is probable.
- Genetic differences account for most of the gap.
- Hereditarianism is “obviously correct.”

These require different evidence. Findings against a large contribution do not by themselves defeat a small one. Conversely, a merely possible contribution does not establish a probable one. The chapter should identify which proposition each cited result bears on.

The sentence “Its best form” introduces Winegard, Winegard, and Anomaly’s evolutionary argument. That judgment is premature without examining the other arguments the plan required. [Memory] Rushton and Jensen’s 2005 review assembles claims involving adoption, admixture, psychometric structure, and biological correlates; Nisbett’s 2005 response contests that package. Fetching both would permit assessment of the strongest available case rather than selecting its broad evolutionary rationale as representative.

Even that rationale receives an insufficient answer: “The argument establishes that some difference in some direction is a priori possible.” Davis’s argument, as quoted, concerns relative plausibility, not logical possibility. The appropriate criticism is that unspecified selection pressures do not establish the direction or magnitude of differences relevant to the observed American gap, and do not distinguish competing causal accounts of that gap.

Davis’s own objections to Murray in the *Facing Reality* draft sharpen this criticism. But the draft should not be paraphrased as saying causal agnosticism itself is incoherent. One can remain agnostic about the causes of an ability distribution while making causal claims about its consequences. Identify the specific inference Davis challenges and whether Murray’s argument requires that inference.

### What the cited evidence can establish

“The new genomic methods … have so far not supported a genetic explanation” is broader than the evidence presented. Bird’s study requires a more exact description: its phenotype, samples, genetic scores, selection model, and assumptions connecting its result to the American IQ gap. Failure to detect divergent selection is not automatically an estimate of the contribution of all genetic differences to that gap.

The chapter simultaneously treats poor score portability as a reason comparisons are unreliable “in either direction” and uses Bird’s result against a substantial genetic contribution. Those positions may be reconcilable through different analyses, but the reconciliation must be shown.

[Memory] Martin and colleagues’ work on ancestry-dependent polygenic-score performance and Mostafavi and colleagues’ work on prediction differences within an ancestry group would help explain why score transport and mean comparisons require care. [Memory] Within-family estimates can reduce certain sources of confounding; they do not automatically validate every cross-population comparison.

The adoption, historical-change, and gap-narrowing evidence likewise needs calibrated conclusions. Large environmental effects demonstrate that heritability does not entail immutability. They do not identify the environmental share of this particular group difference without additional argument. “European ancestry brought no measurable advantage” compresses distinct studies into something resembling a direct ancestry test. Check what Eyferth and Moore actually measured, their samples, and what alternative explanations their designs address.

The 2012 review and 2017 commentary cannot alone support an unqualified verdict covering the chapter’s 2026 endpoint. The main agent should search for later evidence and responses, then date the assessment explicitly.

## 5. Has Davis’s confidence actually increased?

“In four years ‘probably part of the explanation’ … became ‘the obviously correct position’” is a major interpretive claim. Its two quotations do not yet have a demonstrated common referent.

In “Agreeing With Stalin,” “being a racist like Charles Murray” might refer to accepting biological variation, permitting inquiry, rejecting accusations against Murray, endorsing particular racial estimates, or some combination. The surrounding passage must establish that it refers specifically to the causal explanation of the American black–white IQ gap.

The *Guess I’ll Die* wording is relevant, but “technical reasons for believing” does not necessarily express greater confidence than “probably.” The chapter should distinguish a rise in rhetorical force from a rise in stated empirical confidence. “The corpus contains no new argument in between” also needs a defined scope: the relevant passages examined, rather than an unsupported negative about the entire corpus.

**Proposed replacement for §5, paragraph beginning “By 2023 and 2024…”**

*This preserves the concern about rhetoric while making the empirical interpretation conditional on verification.*

> The later passages use less qualified language. The unpublished *Guess I’ll Die* draft describes the earlier review as giving reasons to believe that the gap is partly genetic, while “Agreeing With Stalin” calls being “a racist like Charles Murray” the obviously correct position. The latter phrase requires interpretation: its force as evidence of increased empirical confidence depends on which of Murray’s claims it endorses. If it includes the genetic explanation of the American IQ gap, the passage supplies no corresponding argument for the greater certainty. If it refers more broadly to the legitimacy of studying group differences, it cannot establish that Davis’s confidence about this particular causal claim increased.

## 6. Description, race, and the Alexander dispute

The chapter is too accepting of Davis’s description of the opposing position. “A theory of why egalitarian beliefs persist despite evidence against them” assumes a unified doctrine already refuted by evidence. Which beliefs: equality of rights, identical distributions, wholly environmental explanations of particular differences, or resistance to particular racial classifications? The political theory requires identifiable claims and identifiable holders.

Likewise, “Davis’s grievance against the left is epistemic” is too exclusive. His stated concerns also involve autonomy, institutional authority, affiliation, and interests. The chapter can establish that he formulates political grievances through epistemic criticism without treating his own formulation as exhaustive.

The Fausto-Sterling passage offers an important missing objection. Different consequences can justify different thresholds for accepting or acting on a claim without changing the claim’s truth. That does not vindicate every asymmetric evidential practice. It does show why Goldberg’s distinction, as summarized, does not settle the dispute. [Memory] Heather Douglas’s *Science, Policy, and the Value-Free Ideal* would provide a precise account of inductive risk against which to test both positions.

The chapter also lacks an account of its group categories. [Memory] Appiah’s “The Uncompleted Argument: Du Bois and the Illusion of Race,” Haslanger’s “Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We Want Them To Be?,” and Glasgow’s *A Theory of Race* offer contrasting resources for distinguishing racial classification, ancestry, and social position. This matters directly to what causal contrast “the black–white gap” names.

The Alexander section’s strongest point is that support for some propositions associated with Murray does not vindicate the newspaper’s particular inference. Its weakest is the assertion that *racist* necessarily carries “animus or the endorsement of unjust treatment” in the newspaper and its readers’ understanding. Neither that usage nor that audience interpretation is established.

The charge of a categorical “gerrymander” is therefore premature. Davis may be accepting a hostile label conditionally or using it ironically, much as the chapter explicitly recognizes in his “crypto-sexists” passage. The difference between those passages needs demonstration from context. The study should examine the actual attributed proposition before deciding whether the disagreement is semantic or evidential.

There is a clearer logical error at the end:

> “If Davis would have joined the cover-up had his own interest not been at stake, then the others’ silence is explained by their interests and their judgment of risk, which is a weaker charge than deception.”

Self-interest and risk can explain deception. Davis’s admission does not weaken the accusation on that ground. It may challenge his moral distinction between himself and others, while leaving the descriptive claim untouched.

**Proposed replacement for §6’s final two sentences**

*This separates explanation, accusation, and self-application.*

> Davis’s admission places his own possible conduct under the same criticism. It does not establish whether the others deceived their readers: interests and judgments of risk could explain either misleading speech or a defensible refusal to discuss a subject. That question turns on what the public statements communicated and what evidence supports attributing a contrary belief to their authors.

The leaked email should also remain explicitly secondhand throughout. The chapter initially acknowledges that limitation, then says the email “shows” a private–public difference. Its 2014 date further prevents it from straightforwardly establishing Alexander’s beliefs when he responded in 2021.

## 7. Speech, structure, and prose

“The most widely accepted part of his political writing” needs reception evidence or deletion. The calculator discussion should separate suppressing an answer, suppressing intermediate reasoning, and declining to publish on a topic. Alexander’s proposal, as quoted, concerns the third; Davis’s response argues that it impairs the second. That causal connection is plausible but needs evidence.

The strongest objection to Davis is that public communication consumes attention and can communicate more than its literal content. His strongest reply is that localized suppression can become systemic error. Neither is answered by counting whether a forum produced a major new conclusion in one year. The heckler’s-veto symmetry likewise does not establish equal expected harm from publication and veto.

[Memory] Mill’s *On Liberty*, chapter II, already belongs to Chapter 6 and can be cross-referenced. Schauer’s *Free Speech: A Philosophical Enquiry* and Shiffrin’s *Speech Matters* would help separate defenses grounded in truth production from defenses grounded in the interests and agency of speakers and listeners.

The chapter broadly fits its planned remit. Its main overlap is the repeated validation of the “norm of accounting,” already developed in Chapters 4–7. Keep the triskaidekaphobia footnotes here, but shorten their repeated interpretation in §§2, 3, and 7. Use that space for the empirical distinctions and the political mechanism. The COVID draft adds a new causal controversy without establishing its relevance; cut it unless its factual and methodological characterization receives independent checking.

Several sentences have the brief’s discouraged verdict style: “The theory has two virtues,” “The theory has a gap,” “This is the right question,” and “The contribution is…” They repeatedly announce assessments before supplying their grounds. The opening also compresses every verdict into one paragraph, leaving little interpretive development for the reader. A shorter opening centered on belief and treatment would improve the chapter.

Finally, §7 says “Most of the political writing is held to that standard.” The selected cases do not establish that quantitative judgment. Conclude with the standard and the demonstrated failures, without estimating the proportion of the corpus that meets it.
