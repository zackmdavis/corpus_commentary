### Where to Draw the Boundaries?
- Path: `AAL_content/2019/where-to-draw-the-boundaries.md` · 2019-04-13 · philosophy (philosophy of language) · 3934 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Apr/where-to-draw-the-boundaries/
- Summary: Originally on LessWrong (2019-04-13; 111+ comments). "Followup to" Yudkowsky's "Where to Draw the Boundary?" (2008). Interlocutor "the one" argues a list can't be wrong; boundaries capture tradeoffs per values; "I can define a word any way I want—in accordance with my values!" Davis: definitions are "wrong" not because a unique boundary exists but when they fail to capture statistical structure; dolphins-as-fish captures convergent-evolution structure but mispredicts oviparity; Thingspace subspaces; Bayes-score criterion. Reply to "Everything we identify as a joint is only a joint because we care about it": "Everything we identify as a joint is a joint not 'because we care about it', but because it _helps us think about_ the things we care about." Distinctions: political map vs. omitting Everest because a sister died; "poison" for prediction vs. for fear of lawsuit; utility over model performance vs. over features of the model itself. Numerical example (Foos at [1,2,3], Quuxes at [2,4,6]): if you actually used the merged concept internally you'd predict 3 and 4.5, not 4 and 6; redefinition changes truth values of sentences. Complications: Sorites, unclear relevant dimensions, Schelling points. "the map is not the territory" — arbitrariness in map ≠ arbitrariness in territory. Appeal-to-arbitrariness as Fully General Counterargument; "If the choice were _really_ arbitrary, you would be satisfied with the choice being _made_ arbitrarily." Parenthetical: if you want to optimize others' models rather than the world, "you could just _lie_." Vice-President-of-Sorting parable; information theory: title inflation lowers mutual information I(pay; title) from ≈0.918 to ≈0.2516 bits — "_making language less useful_." Proposes "38. Your definition draws a boundary around a cluster in an inappropriately 'thin' subspace of Thingspace." Musashi adaptation: "Do not ask whether there's a rule of rationality saying that you shouldn't call dolphins fish. Ask whether dolphins are fish." Thanks Alicorn, Sarah Constantin, Ben Hoffman, Zvi Mowshowitz, Jessica Taylor, Michael Vassar.
- Themes: categorization, philosophy-of-language, thingspace, information-theory, mutual-information, gerrymandering, dolphins, yudkowsky-sequences, map-territory, values-vs-structure
- Xrefs: UUT 2018 "Categories…", "Blegg Mode"; AAL 2021 "Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception", "Blood Is Thicker Than Water 🐬", "Reply to Nate Soares on Dolphins"; UUT 2023 "A Hill of Validity…" (context: the "one"'s quoted wording may echo a specific public statement — verify there). LW comments (`reception_data/lw_comments/2019-04-13_where-to-draw-the-boundaries.json`).
- Quotes:
> When rationalists say that definitions can be wrong, we don't mean that there's a _unique_ category boundary that is the True floating essence of a word, and that all other possible boundaries are wrong. We mean that in order for a proposed category boundary to _not_ be wrong, it needs to capture some statistical structure in reality, even if [reality is surprisingly detailed](http://johnsalvatier.org/blog/2017/reality-has-a-surprising-amount-of-detail) and there can be _more than one_ such structure.
> No. Everything we identify as a joint is a joint not "because we care about it", but because it _helps us think about_ the things we care about.
> There is an _important difference_ between "not including mountains on a map because it's a political map that doesn't show any mountains" and "not including Mt. Everest on a geographic map, because my sister died trying to climb Everest and seeing it on the map would make me feel sad."
> There is an _important difference_ between having a utility function defined over a statistical model's _performance_ against specific real-world data (even if another mind with different values would be interested in different data), and having a utility function defined over features of _the model itself_.
> But if the one were _actually using_ the new concept of Foo _internally_ and not just _saying the words_ "categorize it as a Foo", they _wouldn't_ predict 4 and 6! They'd predict 3 and 4.5
> But if speakers of particular language were _already_ using "Foo" to specifically talk about the stuff near [1, 2, 3], then you can't swap in a new definition of "Foo" without _changing the truth values_ of sentences involving the word "Foo."
> But the fundamental thing is this: _the map is not the territory_. Arbitrariness in the map (what color should Texas be?) doesn't correspond to arbitrariness in the territory.
> You should _never_ say, "The choice of word is arbitrary; therefore I can say whatever I want"—which amounts to, "The choice of category is arbitrary, therefore I can believe whatever I want." If the choice were _really_ arbitrary, you would be satisfied with the choice being _made_ arbitrarily: by flipping a coin, or calling a random number generator.
> (Oh, and if you're actually trying to optimize other people's models of the world, rather than the world itself—you could just _lie_, rather than playing clever category-gerrymandering mind games. It would be a lot simpler!)
> But from the perspective of people who wanted to use titles as an easily-communicable correlate of the other features of a Job, all that's actually been accomplished is _making language less useful_.
> Do not ask whether there's a rule of rationality saying that you shouldn't call dolphins fish. Ask whether dolphins are fish.

### The Univariate Fallacy
- Path: `AAL_content/2019/the-univariate-fallacy.md` · 2019-06-15 · mathematics (statistics) · 778 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Jun/the-univariate-fallacy/
- Summary: LW-first. Multivariate distributions can overlap on every variable yet be separable jointly (3D figure); Yudkowsky suggests "covariance denial fallacy"/"cluster erasure fallacy." Worked example: 40 independent variables, each giving ≤0.807 bits; Python tallying likelihood ratios yields ~0.99999 confidence.

- Xrefs: UUT 2018 Metablogging plan; UUT draft `high-dimensional-social-science-and-the-conjunction-of-small-effect-sizes.md`. Context: Lewontin's fallacy (Edwards 2003) — outside literature for research phase.

> The denial of this possibility (in arguments of the form, "the distributions overlap along this variable, therefore you can't say that they're different") is sometimes called the "univariate fallacy."

### Schelling Categories, and Simple Membership Tests
- Path: `AAL_content/2019/schelling-categories-and-simple-membership-tests.md` · 2019-08-25 · philosophy (philosophy of language, game theory) · 2281 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Aug/schelling-categories-and-simple-membership-tests/
- Summary: LW-first; followup to "Where to Draw the Boundaries?". Under coordination and observation constraints, a single salient variable (x₄₁, ~1.585 bits vs ~1.18 for any other single variable) can be the best shared category basis — a Schelling point — even if it's not what you'd pick from a God's-eye view. "Applications": age of majority as simple membership test for illegible "maturity" (regressional Goodhart); delegation to authority (USDA Grade AA eggs); self-identification as delegation to the person ("Are you a Blue or a Green?") — subject to adversarial Goodhart; title inflation ("everyone in the finance industry is a 'vice president'"; Ben Hoffman's etymology). Mentions collaborators on "intersubjective epistemology" (Hoffman, Jessica Taylor). Footnote: his supermarket "booth" job. Takeaway: coordination is costly; aspire to Bayes-structure precision anyway.
- Themes: schelling-categories, coordination, goodhart, self-identification-as-delegation (implicit gender application), categorization, ben-hoffman, jessica-taylor
- Quotes:
> In conclusion, the right categories to use _given_ constraints on communication and observation, might be different from the category boundaries you would draw from a "God's eye view", in part because consideration of which categories are easy for different agents to _coordinate_ on is relevant, not just raw information-theoretic expressive power. Thus, "Schelling categories."
> It's a lot simpler to talk about whether someone has pledged allegiance to the rallying flag: just _ask_ someone, "What color do you believe the sky is?" (using sky-beliefs as as an "objective" simple membership test), or simply, "Are you a Blue or a Green?" (delegating the classification problem to _the person themselves_ as the authority whose discernment is to be trusted)—and whatever they say, that's what they are.
> We've seen that objective measurements like age are subject to regressional Goodhart, but the delegation-to-authority strategy is furthermore subject to [_adversarial_ Goodhart](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/EbFABnst8LsidYs5Y/goodhart-taxonomy): once a category-membership test has been established, some agents might have an incentive to create examples that _pass the test_, but don't have the complicated, illegible properties than made the test a useful proxy in the first place.

- Quotes:
> If the coin gets flipped a sufficiently large number of times, a reporter whom you _trust_ to be impartial (but isn't), can _make you believe anything she wants without ever telling a single lie_, just with [appropriate selective reporting](https://slatestarcodex.com/2015/09/16/cardiologists-and-chinese-robbers/).

### Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist
- Path: `AAL_content/2019/maybe-lying-doesnt-exist.md` · 2019-10-14 · philosophy (honesty, philosophy of language) · 2374 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Oct/maybe-lying-doesnt-exist/
- Summary: LW-first (59 comments). Reply to Scott Alexander's "Against Lie Inflation" (itself prompted by Jessica Taylor's "The AI Timelines Scam"). Davis also frowns on the expanded sense of "lying," but for different reasons. (1) Appeals to consequences are invalid for optimal categorization just as for facts (Hume epigraph; Litany of Tarski); "If Elijah's behavior is, _in fact_, deceptive ... I can't make his behavior not-deceptive by _changing the meanings of words_"; equivocation risk is symmetric ("lie deflation" defends the guilty; Ruby Bloom's workplace example). (2) The folk concept may be like Roderick Long's "zaxlebax": the intensional definition (conscious intent) may not match the common extensional cases if self-deception is ubiquitous (Hanson); notes earlier Alexander ("Niceness, Community, and Civilization") called a politician's misleading statistic "a lie." Personally prefers "motivated," "misleading," "distorted," but won't tone-police; doesn't excuse motivated reasoning, which responds to incentives.
- Themes: lying-and-deception, appeals-to-consequences, scott-alexander, jessica-taylor, self-deception, motte-and-bailey, philosophy-of-language
- Xrefs: AAL 2020 "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!"; AAL 2012 "Lies and Delusion"; LW-only 2019 "Status 451 on Diagnosis: Russell Aphasia"; LW comments.
- Quotes:
> But this is an [appeal to consequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_consequences). [Appeals to consequences](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/P3FQNvnW8Cz42QBuA/dialogue-on-appeals-to-consequences) are invalid because they represent a map–territory confusion, an attempt to optimize our _description_ of reality at the expense of our ability to describe reality _accurately_ (which we need in order to _actually_ optimize reality).
> If the one comes to me and says, "You should choose to define _lying_ such that Elijah is not lying, because if you said that he was lying, then he might feel angry and defensive," this is _insane_. The map is not the territory!
> But that's not an argument for changing the definition of lying; that's an argument that I have an incentive to lie about whether I think Elijah is lying!
> If your interest in the philosophy of language is primarily to _avoid being blamed for things_—perhaps because you perceive that you live [in a Hobbesian dystopia](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/YRgMCXMbkKBZgMz4M/asymmetric-justice#puGDkhWCcaNJEMkdz) where the primary function of words is to elicit actions
> The first description certainly _sounds nicer_, but as aspiring epistemic rationalists, we're _not allowed to care_ about which descriptions sound nice; we're _only_ allowed to care about which descriptions match reality.
> And I _definitely_ don't want to excuse motivated reasoning as a mere ordinary human failing for which someone can't be blamed! One of the key features that distinguishes motivated reasoning from simple mistakes is the way that the former _responds to incentives_ (such as being blamed).

### Algorithms of Deception!
- Path: `AAL_content/2019/algorithms-of-deception.md` · 2019-10-19 · philosophy (honesty, Python) · 1506 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Oct/algorithms-of-deception/
- Summary: LW-first. Python Reporter/Audience model: an honest reporter yields the true distribution; `reporter_2` (selective reporting) and `reporter_3` (merging outcomes into a "oneorfour" category) both skew the audience toward "4" without any false report. Dramatized: betrayed Audience says "You _lied_"; each Reporter sincerely denies — "using language in a way _you_ dislike, is not lying. I can define a word any way I want!" Deception defined non-mentally: communication that systematically results in less accurate predictions for a listener using normally-correct inference.
- Themes: deception-as-algorithm, selective-reporting, category-merging-as-deception, naturalized-epistemology, conflict-vs-mistake
- Xrefs: AAL 2021 "Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception" (expansion of `reporter_3`); AAL 2020 "Algorithmic Intent".
- Quotes:
> He is perfectly sincere. Nothing in his _consciousness_ reflects _intent_ to deceive Audience, any more than an eight-line Python program could be said to have such "intent."
> If true "beliefs" are models that [make accurate predictions](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/a7n8GdKiAZRX86T5A/making-beliefs-pay-rent-in-anticipated-experiences), then _deception_ would presumably be communication that systematically results in _less_ accurate predictions (by a listener applying the same inference algorithms that would result in more accurate predictions when applied to direct observations or "honest" reports).

### Firming Up Not-Lying Around Its Edge-Cases Is Less Broadly Useful Than One Might Initially Think
- Path: `AAL_content/2019/firming-up-not-lying-around-its-edge-cases-is-less-broadly-useful-than-one-might-initially-think.md` · 2019-12-26 · philosophy (honesty) · 2558 w · https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Dec/firming-up-not-lying-around-its-edge-cases-is-less-broadly-useful-than-one-might-initially-think/
- Summary: LW-first; reply to Yudkowsky's "Meta-Honesty." "Is this literally true?" is ambiguous (as audience will interpret vs. permits a true interpretation); the latter is weak. All speech is optimized for functions besides informing. By analogy to AI alignment patches ("nearest unblocked strategy"), a not-lying rule without intent to inform trains skill at not-technically-lying (motte-and-bailey, false implicature, stonewalling, selective reporting, "gerrymandered category boundaries"). *Atlas Shrugged*'s State Science Institute statement on Rearden Metal as a model; imagined scientist checking each sentence is "literally true." Wizard's oath most useful against adversaries; "clever logic puzzles about implausibly sophisticated Nazis" won't help with intellectual dishonesty, which "plus sufficient intelligence, is enough to destroy the world." Footnote: confidentiality promises and narrow construal (Open Opal/Paranoid Paris).
- Themes: honesty, meta-honesty, yudkowsky, not-technically-lying, intent-to-inform, nearest-unblocked-strategy, ayn-rand, intellectual-dishonesty
- Xrefs: UUT 2023–24 memoir (confidentiality disputes; Yudkowsky); AAL 2023 "Contra Yudkowsky on Epistemic Conduct…"(LW-only).
- Quotes:
> Now ask yourself how often your speech has been partially optimized for any function _other_ than providing listeners with information that will help them [better anticipate their experiences](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/a7n8GdKiAZRX86T5A/making-beliefs-pay-rent-in-anticipated-experiences). The answer is, "Every time you open your mouth"[^mouth]—and if you disagree, then you're lying.
> So _another_ hypothesis about the psychological effects of adopting the wizard's code is that—however noble your initial conscious _intent_ was—in the face of sufficiently strong incentives to deceive, you just end up accidentally training yourself to get _really good_ at misleading people with a variety of [not-technically-lying](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PrXR66hQcaJXsgWsa/not-technically-lying) rhetorical tactics
> A theory of under what conditions conscious explicit unambiguous outright lies are acceptable doesn't help very much with combating _intellectual_ dishonesty—and I fear that intellectual dishonesty, plus sufficient intelligence, is enough to destroy the world all on its own, without the help of conscious explicit unambiguous outright lies.
> But one thing I'm pretty sure _won't_ help much is _clever logic puzzles about implausibly sophisticated Nazis_.

- Summary: LW-first. Explaining-away/collider bias (Koller & Friedman flu/mono; Pearl burglar/earthquake): if someone is wrong, stupidity and dishonesty are anticorrelated explanations; holds whether motivated reasoning counts as either; symmetric across persons.
- Themes: explaining-away, bayes-nets, stupidity-vs-dishonesty, conflict-vs-mistake
- Xrefs: AAL 2012 "Idiot or Alien?"
- Quotes:
> Here's another example: if someone you're arguing with is wrong, it could be either because they're just too stupid to get the right answer, or it could be because they're being dishonest—or some combintation of the two, but more of one means that less of the other is required to explain the observation of the person being wrong.
