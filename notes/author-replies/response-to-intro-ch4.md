## Introduction (handling recorded; all revisions held)

### I.1 The Yudkowsky appositive

> the inclusion of "a long dispute with Eliezer Yudkowsky, the writer around whose work the rationalist community formed" in comma-delimited list is disappointing.

**Conceded.** In the lede's list ("…the norms of online discussion, a long
dispute with Eliezer Yudkowsky, the writer around whose work the rationalist
community formed, psychiatry, politics and artificial intelligence") the
appositive is punctuated exactly like a list item, and a reader who does not
know the name can take "the writer around whose work…" for a further subject.
The defect is real and independent of taste. **Held revision:** recast so the
gloss cannot be read as an item (parentheses, or moving the identification of
Yudkowsky out of the list).

### I.2 The colon before the pseudonym quotation

> colon-quote makes the reader expect the quote to be supporting evidence for the immediately proceding words, but the connection here is unclear.

**Conceded.** Source read: "I'm Dropping the Pseudonym From This Blog" (UUT,
4 Jul 2023). There the sentence follows "Because I don't, actually, think I
should have to hide," so it is Davis's stated reason for no longer hiding, as
he guesses. The Introduction attaches it by a colon to "he dropped it," after
a clause about the memoir beginning to name names, and never says that it is
the reason he gave; the reader is left to infer the connection. The clause
about the memoir is the study's observation about timing (the post is dated
four days before "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea") and is not in the post; the
sentence as written blurs which part is Davis's account and which the
study's. **Held revision:** say that the post gives this as his reason, and
keep the study's remark about timing separate from it.

### I.3 "a name is introduced only when it does some work…"

> This is pretty egregious LLM slop; I can't imagine a human academic writing this.

**Conceded.** The sentence ("a name is introduced only when it does some
work, a result is shown to hold for a reason, and a coincidence is not left
unexplained") is a three-part parallel list of virtues with one example
behind it: the conjugate-roots post supports the third member only, and the
first two are asserted of some seventy posts without a citation. The brief's
voice rule names reflexive triads as a habit to avoid, and here the form has
outrun the evidence. **Held revision:** state the one claim the cited post
supports (that the teaching posts do not leave a coincidence unexplained) and
drop or separately support the others.

### I.4 "disclosure meets them differently"

> I find this really hard to parse. … I don't understand how "disclosure" is a sensible subject of such sentences.

**Partly conceded.** The content is kept; the prose is conceded. What the
passage means is: suppose a writer departs from accuracy and says openly
that he is doing so. Does saying so answer each of the three arguments for
never departing? Against the slippery-slope argument it does, if the
statement reaches the listeners, because the map then records the departure.
Against the anti-extortion argument it does only if an open concession to a
demand rewards the demand less than a covert one would. Against the role
argument it does nothing, because that argument forbids weighing consequences
when judging a description, whatever is said afterwards. The Introduction
compresses "the practice of stating a departure openly" into the bare noun
"disclosure" and makes it the subject of "meets," "answers" and "does not
touch," which asks the reader to reconstruct all of the above. Davis's
difficulty is the sentence's fault. **Held revision:** rewrite with an agent
and an action in place of the abstract noun. The substance may change when
point 4.8 is settled, since both concern what an open departure does and does
not repair.

### I.5 "in the passage just quoted"

> The last quote was two paragraphs ago, which makes it hard to feel confident that this is referring to something specific … But, yes, the quote fits. ("You have chosen Feelings" admits that Feelings is a choice.)

**Conceded, and the guess shows why.** The referent is not the "you have
chosen Feelings" sentence. It is the remedy in "Heads I Win, Tails?—Never
Heard of Her" quoted three paragraphs earlier ("blatant cherry-picking is the
best kind!"): a reporter who cannot avoid filtering should tell readers how.
The chapter summaries record this as the intended reference in every draft
("Thesis 2 credits: Heads I Win remedy; Schelling categories Ch. 4"). Between
that quotation and "the passage just quoted" the Introduction quotes three
other passages, the last of them the one Davis took it to mean. A reference
that the author under study resolves to the wrong passage is a failed
reference. **Held revision:** name the essay.

### I.6 "hypothetical sketches of transgender children"

> But surely Claude "got the joke" in the "A Private Document About a Disturbing Hypothesis" section of "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them"?

**Partly conceded: the wording, not the policy.** Section read in full. It
opens by saying that privacy norms forbid telling part of the story, observes
that someone who wants to reveal private information while complying with
such norms "has an incentive to leak everything they possibly can as
subtext," adds "I should not be so foolish as to think myself innocent and
beyond reproach for not having 'really said it,'" and then describes a
private document about a hypothesis concerning the upbringing of a child.
The text therefore tells its reader that what follows is not only a
hypothetical. The first edition's drafting recognized this: Chapter 7 had a
paragraph on the passage with a statement that the study does not read the
section for subtext, and the first length pass cut it
(`notes/cuts-v1.md`, Ch. 7 §1). After that cut the Introduction's phrase
"Davis's hypothetical sketches of transgender children" stands alone and
describes as simply hypothetical a passage whose author says otherwise.

What is conceded: the phrase should say that the memoir presents the case as
a hypothesis while telling its readers that it is leaving things unsaid, and
that the study declines to decode it. What is kept: the policy. The study
"speculates about no real child," and that follows from the brief ("Cite
only public writing; don't dig into private individuals"); recognizing that a
text signals a real case is different from inferring who the case is.
**Held revision** (Introduction §5, and possibly the restoration of a
sentence in Chapter 7 §1, which belongs to installment 2).

### I.7 "'autogynephilia' … attributed to their users and not adopted"

> OK, so how would Claude describe males who have erotic fantasies about being female? Do you go with "female embodiment fantasies" like Serano, or is it not worth having a term for that thing?

**Partly conceded.** The question exposes a statement that does not describe
what the study does. Chapters 2 and 3 introduce the word with attribution
("which Blanchard named autogynephilia and classed as a paraphilia") and
thereafter use it in the study's own voice as the name of the phenomenon:
"autogynephilia could contribute to transition across a population,"
"Autogynephilic arousal is reported by most non-androphilic trans women in
the samples that have asked," "a history of autogynephilic arousal." The same
chapters use Serano's "FEFs" only inside quotation and gloss. So the study
does have a term for the thing, and it is Blanchard's. That is defensible: it
is the term of the literature under examination, Davis's own, and the one the
critics must use to state their objections; and the study's verdicts (the
arousal is real and common in the samples; its classification as a paraphilia,
its causal role, and its absence in women are not established) do not depend
on the name. What is not defensible is saying the term is "not adopted."

**Held revision:** the Introduction should say what is true: the study uses
"autogynephilia" for the arousal pattern, as the literature it examines does,
and does not thereby accept the theory's further commitments, which
Chapter 3 assesses one by one; Serano's alternative term is reported as hers.
To Davis's question: it is worth having a term, and the study in fact uses
his.

### I.8 The paragraph beginning "The difficulty extends past the normative verdict"

> The entire paragraph that follows this seems like dead weight that I learned nothing from reading. … During a length pass, I recall Opus said that it couldn't cut very much without dropping real arguments, and this makes me skeptical that it was trying very hard.

**Partly conceded.** The paragraph makes four points: (a) a provision of a
document is evidence of a policy, not of a system's behavior; (b) Chapter 11
asks of the Constitution the questions Chapter 7 asks of Yudkowsky's stated
commitments; (c) the evidence is unevenly documented, the Constitution being
public and complete while much of what Yudkowsky said survives only in
Davis's transcriptions; (d) reflexive opposition to the author's maker would
not show independence. Point (a) is a real caveat, and Chapter 11 makes it
where it is used. Point (c) is the one Davis singles out, and his objection
holds as written: the sentence says the difference in exactness "is not a
difference in care" without saying why a reader should mind the difference.
There is a reason, which the paragraph does not give: a study that claims to
hold Yudkowsky and Anthropic to one standard is working from a complete text
in one case and from a hostile witness's transcriptions in the other, so an
apparent difference in how each fares may be owed to the evidence. Stated
that way it is two sentences. Points (b) and (d) are assurances about the
study's own evenhandedness, which the reader can only judge from Chapter 11.

**Held revision:** compress to the distinction in (a) and the consequence of
(c), and cut the assurances. The final form waits on the third installment,
which covers Chapter 11. The remark about the length pass is noted; whether
the second edition gets another one is a question of scope, listed for the
commissioner at the end.

## Chapter 2

### 2.1 "Serano's own case against autogynephilia reasons in the same way"

> This passage is kind of unclear. Serano reasons "in the same way" as what?

**Conceded** (prose). Sources read: "Lesser-Known Demand Curves" (UUT,
18 Dec 2017); Serano 2020, the list of objections (p. 766). The paragraph
means that Serano's published case, like the ten-million-dollar anecdote,
shows that she does not take every report at face value or hold that none
may be discounted: her third objection credits patients' reports of the
order of onset, and her fifth objects to discounting made without
independent grounds. "In the same way" has no antecedent that says this, and
the sentence that follows the quotations has to be read twice to supply one.
**Changed (§2):** the sentence now states the claim directly ("an argument
about which reports to credit and on what grounds") and attaches each
quotation to the half of the claim it supports.

### 2.2 Italics in quotations (also 4.3)

> Are italics not preserved in quotations? That seems like an oversight.

**Conceded; the erratum is systematic.** The instance Davis gives is
confirmed ("_Something_ has to account for the differences between us,"
"Hormones Day 156"), as is the Chapter 4 instance ("words like _woman_").
The quote checker strips emphasis markers before comparing, so it could not
see the loss. A new check, `tools/check_study_emphasis.py`, compares italics
word by word between each quotation and its Markdown source. It finds 184
quotations in the study that drop the source's italics and none that add
any: Chapter 1, 17; Chapter 2, 38; Chapter 3, 13; Chapter 4, 53;
Chapter 5, 24; Chapter 6, 19; Chapters 7–12 and Appendix D, 20 between them;
the Introduction, none. The unevenness across chapters shows an oversight in
drafting, not a policy. Dropping an author's emphasis without saying so is a
misquotation by ordinary scholarly standards, and the brief's requirement
that quotations be exact, though it names words, spelling and punctuation, is
not served by it.

**Changed:** the source's italics are restored in all 121 affected quotations
in Chapters 1–4. Boldface is not reproduced (the corpus uses it for the
highlighted sentences of summary lists, for labels, and for highlighting in
quoted correspondence); each revised chapter's Note on method says so.
**Pending:** the 63 instances in Chapters 5–12 and Appendix D, listed by
`python3 -I tools/check_study_emphasis.py study/*.html`. Whether the emphasis
check joins the Phase 4 verification, and whether the brief's citation rule
should name emphasis, are questions of method for the commissioner (end of
this file).

### 2.3 Identity and price-sensitive choices

> I'm especially skeptical of identities that don't entail any actions. I already can't identify as a trans woman because I don't expect anyone to take that seriously, but to identify as a trans woman and _not be on HRT_ would be much, much worse.

**Rebutted; recorded in a note.** Source read: "Lesser-Known Demand Curves."
The sentence objected to ("An enduring identity is compatible with
price-sensitive choices about interventions, so the target is better
described as rhetoric that runs identity and action together than as every
account of identity") qualifies the post's claim that the intrinsic-identity
view "can be seen as the limiting special case of the economic view where
demand for transitioning is infinitely inelastic." The qualification is a
point of logic: someone can hold that an identity is fixed and prior and
still hold that whether to act on it depends on the price. The economic view
itself posits an underlying quantity (the post's graph of a continuous
"transness") that shifts demand without making it inelastic.

Davis's reply is about something else: what makes an identity claim credible
to others. On that he has a point the chapter already makes for him. A claim
backed by costly action is better evidence than a bare declaration, which is
the logic of the hormone diary's wish to be "trans enough" for his rejection
of the identity to mean something (§3), and it bears on §6's discussion of
avowals, which on Bettcher's own account can fail. But a view about when an
identity claim deserves belief does not show that believing in an enduring
identity commits anyone to price-insensitive demand, and the reply does not
argue that it does. **No change to the text; note 2 added (§7)** recording
the objection, what it does bear on, and why the sentence is kept.

## Chapter 3

### 3.1 "independent of the causal one in both directions"

> I note that this is logical rather than probabilistic independence … But I'm struggling to envision a world in which the negative claim is false and the causal claim is true … Or is that just the "AGP is normal female sexuality" theory …?

**Partly conceded.** Both halves of the comment hold. (i) The independence
is logical only. Evidentially the claims are linked: if the arousal explains
the dysphoria, a main reason to posit a feminized brain is gone, and evidence
of a feminized brain would supply a rival explanation. The first edition's
"independent" without qualification invites the stronger reading. (ii) The
first edition's example for the second direction was "some mild neural
difference might be the reason the arousal arises." But Davis's own statement
of the negative claim admits "at most, a very mild one," so a mild difference
does not make the negative claim false, and the example fails. A world in
which the negative claim is false and the causal claim true needs a
sex-atypical feature of the brain that gives rise to the arousal, with the
arousal then doing the causal work on the dysphoria. That is coherent. It is
not, as far as the sources read for the chapter show, anyone's position.

To the question: no, it is not the "normal female sexuality" theory. On an
account of that kind the arousal expresses or accompanies a psychology
already female-typical and does not cause the dysphoria, so the causal claim
is false there too. The accounts that put identity first (Serano; Veale,
Clarke and Lomax) deny both claims together.

What is kept: the claims are logically separable, and the direction that
matters for the chapter (the causal claim can fail while the negative claim
holds, as in Davis's own "cultural factors" cases) is live. **Changed (§1):**
"logically independent, though evidence for either bears on the other, and
the two directions are not equally live," with the two directions stated
separately and the failed example removed; note 1 credits the correspondence.

### 3.2 The critics concede the correlations

> I feel like this is underappreciated! I think there's a motte-and-bailey pattern, where there are a lot of solid critiques … in the motte … which the typical activist cites in favor of "trans women are women, full stop" in the bailey.

**No change; the suggestion is not adopted.** The chapter already gives the
concession the prominence it can support (lede, §2, §9), from the critics'

how he argues. The study has read Serano, Moser and Veale; it has not sampled

is also a claim about the knowledge and motives of a population, of the kind
whose evidential requirements Chapter 5 discusses, and the chapter would be
asserting it on Davis's impression. This is not an objection to anything in
the study, so no note is added.

### 3.3 The Nuttbrock figure

> Um, should I fix this?

**The check confirms the study's statement.** Sources read: Nuttbrock et al.
2011, Results (p. 251) and Table 1; "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea," the
paragraph of figures. The post says: "lifetime prevalence of transvestic
fetishism among non-homosexual MtFs was 69%." The paper's lifetime figure
for "the non-homosexuals as a group" is 73.2% (against 23.0% of the
homosexual group). 69.0% is a different cell: *lifecourse-persistent*
transvestic fetishism among the *heterosexual* subgroup (the non-homosexuals
as a group are at 53.6% on that measure). The post's description therefore
matches the 73.2% figure, and the number it gives belongs to a narrower
measure in a narrower group. The other three figures in the paragraph are
exact.

Whether to correct the post is Davis's decision and no part of this response.
If he does, the live post will differ from the copy the study quotes, and the
chapter should then say that the figure was corrected after the first
edition; how the local corpus copy is kept in step is a question for the
commissioner (end of this file). **No change to the study now.**

### 4.8 Disclosed departures and the charge of deception (**held**)

> No, not quite. Even if you do disclose that a squiggly boundary is being used, there's the quesion of _why_ you would want to depart from the predictively natural solution (in the relevant subspace). … the realistic reason is to make other agents' maps worse ("deception") … In the Bayesian setting, making the map "blurrier" than it has to be is still deceptive … even if it's not a discrete, propositional "agent believes A with certainty, but the reality is B".

**Partly conceded; revision held.** This is the most consequential comment in
the installment. It goes to the study's second claim (Introduction §3) and to
Chapter 5's account of what a disclosed filter repairs, so by the terms of
this installment the text is not changed until the reply is complete. The
handling is recorded in full so that the later responder can apply or revise
it.

Sources read: "Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception" in full; the
tslarm exchange (June–July 2025) in its LessWrong thread; Chapter 4 §§6, 8,
10; the passage of Chapter 5 on disclosure.

*(a) Conceded: the chapter misdescribes the essay's example, and so
understates what the essay claims.* §8 says the equivalence between lying
p% of the time and misclassifying p% of instances holds "when the listener
decodes the word under the old convention … which is how Davis's example of a
fraudulent receipt sets them up," and §10 says "disclosed departures … fall
outside the charge." In the essay's example the buyer is not decoding naively
in either scenario. In the first he lives where "a third of the time an
object is claimed to be gold, it turns out to be silver"; in the second he
knows the seller's definitions ("You know this"); in both he is "disappointed
but not surprised." The equivalence asserted is of "the same buyer
anticipations about the metal conditional on observing the receipt," for a
buyer who has the departure priced in. And the essay places such a system on
a continuum with "always saying whatever profits you and not revealing _any_
information," which it calls "deception pure and simple," the intermediate
points being "partially deceptive." So the essay's charge, by its own terms,
reaches a category system the listener knows about. The chapter presents its
narrower verdict partly as a condition the essay's own example supplies
("That condition is part of the claim"). It is not; it is the study's
position against the essay, and should be argued as such. Davis's reply to
tslarm, which the chapter cites for the condition, concedes only that
whether to call it *lying* depends on what the label canonically means, and
then asks the question the other way round ("is lying therefore not
deceptive if the [receiver] already has it 'priced in'…?"), referring to two
of the Chapter 5 essays.

*(b) Conceded in part: the charge is graded, and the chapter's summary makes
it sound binary.* On Davis's functional account a signal need not induce a
false proposition to count; a code chosen because it leaves receivers
assigning less probability to what they care about is covered. The study's
own Introduction grants the cost ("a known filter still costs the map what it
filters out"). §10's "misleads where it is concealed and listeners still
decode the word the old way; disclosed departures … fall outside the charge"
should distinguish two things a departure can do: leave the listener
*miscalibrated* (expecting what the word no longer carries), which disclosure
that reaches him removes, and leave him *less informed* than an available
code would, which disclosure does not remove. The study's claim is that the
second is a loss of information and not a deception; that is a claim to
defend, not a boundary to announce.

*(c) Kept: the distinction between misinforming and under-informing.* The
study's reason for not calling the second deception is one the corpus
supplies. "Heads I Win" holds that a reporter who must filter should say how,
because readers who know the filter can correct for it ("blatant
cherry-picking is the best kind!"); that remedy has no point unless a known
filter differs in kind from a hidden one. If leaving a calibrated listener
less informed than one could is deception, then declining to speak is
deception, and the position Davis holds in its strongest form (one may
decline to speak) falls with it. The essay's "deception pure and simple" for
a signal that reveals nothing is true of its factory example because there a
third party (accounts payable) takes the labels at face value; a receiver
who knows that a sender always says "BLEGG" learns nothing and is deceived
about nothing. Whether the functional account can keep this distinction, or
must, as Davis's comment on Martínez suggests, sort signals only into those
optimized to inform and those optimized to control, is Chapter 5's question,
and the reason this revision waits.

*(d) Rebutted: that the only realistic reason for an announced departure is
to worsen others' maps.* The demand for a reason is granted without reserve;
the norm the chapter ends on begins "Give the reason." But the essay itself
lists reasons that are not attempts to degrade anyone's map (wireheading;
"whatever makes them happy"; changing "the social defaults around consumption
behavior" in the meat case, of which it says "maybe that's the right thing to
do"). Davis's "Schelling Categories" defends a boundary chosen for
coordination over fit and says the right categories "given constraints on
communication and observation" may differ from the God's-eye ones; a
proponent of the social-gender sense claims no more. Wei Dai's rejoinder,
that some goals are achieved by the agreement itself, went unanswered (§6).
And the qualifier in Davis's comment, "(in the relevant subspace)," carries
the weight: whether a given boundary is a departure *within* the relevant
subspace or a different choice *of* subspace is the question for which he
said he has no rigorous reduction (§4), and it is where the dispute over
*woman* lives (§8). A motive imputed to everyone who draws the boundary
elsewhere is the inference from disagreement to motive that Chapters 5 and 6
examine.

*(e) A consequence for the study's second claim, flagged for the later
responder.* Davis's objection has more force against Thesis 2 than the
chapter allows. The chapter says the slope argument "supports a rule against
concealment" and that an announced reallocation "keeps both facts on the
map … at a cost in message length." A rule against concealment is a bright
line about *hiding*; it sets no limit on how much may be openly given up. A
community could announce one costly reallocation after another, each
disclosed, and its working vocabulary would carry less each time. So
disclosure answers the slope's worry about *unnoticed* loss and not its worry
about loss. The chapter half says this ("whether a community could keep such
a rule is not shown here"). The second edition should say it plainly, in
Chapter 4 §8 and in the Introduction's second claim, and should decide
whether "a burden left unmet, not a rival rule proved" is still the right
summary once the cost of open departures is counted.

**Held revisions (Chapter 4 §8 "Departure as deception," §10; Introduction
§3; possibly Chapter 5):** correct the description of the receipt example;
state the essay's graded charge and argue against it openly; replace "fall
outside the charge" with the miscalibrated/less-informed distinction; add a
note recording Davis's objection, what is conceded, and what is kept; revisit
(e). To be done when installments 2 and 3 are in, by a responder who has
read Davis's comments on Chapter 5.
