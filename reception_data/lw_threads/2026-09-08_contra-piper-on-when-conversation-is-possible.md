#### [>] 307th · 15 karma · 2026-09-08 · https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/79YHdub9LRRSjBQK9/contra-piper-on-when-conversation-is-possible?commentId=JG4ZdqMdvokciHKsa
As someone who falls into the latter camp, I would say the reason I'm not a fan of arguing about someone's true reason for believing X is more pragmatic, which is that when it's done it's mostly done to bulverize rather than for any truth seeking reason. Talking about the object level issue is hard and fiddly, but making up a picture of why your opponent is wrong is fun and can be persuasive to bystanders.

As for why I think it's bad:

*   Accusations of bias are easy to dress up rhetorically and often can't be disproven
*   Even in the case where the speculation is accurate, it doesn't have much bearing on the actual issue; you can successfully identify someone is biased towards believing X for silly emotional reason Y but they probably also have concrete reasons A, B, and C, and X may in fact be true despite the silliness of Y.
*   Conversely, even in the case where X is false, convincing them of not-X seems more likely if you address their concrete reasons A, B, and C rather than spending time trying to figure out which bias is leading them to buy A, B, and C and trying to convince them that they are suffering from that bias.

As a result of all this, I just find it uninteresting to argue about people's motives. It seems highly unproductive and is usually done by people playing games rather than truth seeking. If we're talking about X, and you say I only believe that because Y despite me saying I believe it because of A, B, and C, I become less interested in the conversation, because now the convo is shifting to be about Y but regardless of whether Y is true or false it doesn't have much to do with X.

Obviously people can in fact be wrong about their own motives and so there is a case where it can actually be productive, but even among rationalists I feel like I've much more often seen the unproductive failure mode described above.

#### [-] Jai · 11 karma · 2026-09-08 · https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/79YHdub9LRRSjBQK9/contra-piper-on-when-conversation-is-possible?commentId=mkE4KX6ayxaGSokcX
Moving debates to be about the motives of one side or another (in an adversarial context) is an implicit attack on credibility. Rather than making arguments about observable state in the world, the argument is now about whether one party should be paid any heed at all. It's also typically going to be fruitless for the accused party: either they're reflective enough to be fairly confident in what their beliefs are such that they immediately know that the accusation is false and are now faced with the chore of providing empirical evidence for something they're very confident in, or they're not and their epistemics on the matter are unlikely to be sharpened under adversarial pressure.

In either case, you're not arguing about the thing you were arguing about anymore, you're arguing about whether one of you should be arguing at all.

Motive arguments are arguments of last resort, when you're confident that not only is your opponent wrong, but that they are so wrong for such fundamental reasons that there is no reason to pay their arguments any attention at all. And that can happen - sometimes a party is motivated in such a way that they'll reliably generate bad arguments for a position. But that's not a target you aim for if you want to have an ongoing dialogue.

(This assumes an adversarial context; in non-adversarial contexts motives can be questioned as a means of understanding. Politics is always adversarial.)

#### [-] sarahconstantin · 51 karma · 2026-09-08 · https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/79YHdub9LRRSjBQK9/contra-piper-on-when-conversation-is-possible?commentId=awpw8iHdsGCWpK4nj
I think most people have *some*, but not *infinite*, ability to "not take things personally", to engage in conversations that border on personal attacks without emotions interfering with their ability to track logic.

Speaking only for myself, when something gets too triggering, too close to an attack on my ego or something I hold dear, then the conversation will predictably get unproductive. I will *either* defend myself when I'm in the wrong, *or* submit socially when I'm in the right, and *I can't tell which it will be*, but nobody is going to learn the truth and nobody is going to have a good time.

It is, maybe, an okay social convention to suppose "when somebody takes things personally, we *presume* they are guilty of something they don't want to admit." But it's not literally *true*. People can also get defensive or emotional when they're falsely accused. If you assume "everybody who's uncomfortable talking to me about this touchy subject is Up To No Good", you will be wrong a lot of the time. It is not at *all* uncommon for people to be insecure about things that are in fact fine. Particularly if they default to social submission rather than social dominance.

If you want to engage in honest intellectual reasoning about touchy subjects, you can put in effort to make them less touchy, by establishing social safety.

If you choose to assume that everybody who has a limit, beyond which they get uncomfortable, take things personally, etc, is not worth conversing with, then you are going to have a very biased view of the world. I have seen this happen to people. They are functionally limited to a tiny circle of interlocutors and their views become, from my perspective, very skewed and ungrounded. People who literally can't be triggered are psychologically unusual and they are not the only people worth learning from. I would *strongly* recommend continuing to intellectually engage with people who are limited to a "comfort zone", within that comfort zone, and optionally putting in the work yourself to *make* their comfort zone larger.

If you *have* a comfort zone, beyond which you can't engage productively, I would recommend being prompt and explicit about ending conversations when they approach the edge of what you can handle. Take responsibility for politely bowing out. Do not concede that you are in the wrong, because *you don't know that*. Let them believe what they want, but end the conversation before you say a bunch of things you regret. Push to the edge of your own comfort zone cautiously and at your own pace.
