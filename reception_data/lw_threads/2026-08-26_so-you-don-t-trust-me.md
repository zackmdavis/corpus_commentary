Yes but even worse, this is mostly not even a problem of "understanding".

A newly gained intellectual understanding of why X is good actually is insufficient to get someone with years of touchiness about X to not be touchy. There are lots of "unprocessed" heuristics (~maladaptive behavior/instincts), and it takes time/effort to change them quickly, or sometimes at all (for trapped priors).

It might not be the best someone to work on that particular heuristic at that time. A human following optimal human protocol will probably be carrying lots of unprocessed heuristics, that are mostly worth quick verbal acknowledgement and acceptance "I'm touchy about X" than deep change (tho someone at that level will probably have change happen over time)

Interestingly, most times one wants to oppose the wisdom of their emotions, there's a wrinkle to look out for. If someone new to human relations read this post and decided they'd distrust people who are touchy about honesty, they'd probably correctly be filtered out by touchy honest people. Honest people do have benefit of having and presenting raw edges to learn others' skill levels at judgement and fluidity. Someone with rigid beliefs about "how other people should be" is probably not great to work with!

In practice, I find posts like the above a good contribution to someone learning human socializing, it's good to know about implicit signals and equilibriums, but it's easy to go overboard and forget the dozen other factors that complicate what actual best behavior is for real situations. I do recommend learning theory (for people like me). If one learns theory both for why X and why not X (eg. why gender norms are good, and why gender norms are bad), one can recognize the specifics faster and more fluidly navigate actual situations.

> In my experience many honest people are VERY touchy about being trusted. This has more to do with their formative experiences and worldview about status, honor, and respect than about a directed intent.

The entire idea of honor/prestige is that you spend decades providing evidence that you are trustworthy. It is a costly signal, that can be ruined by violating the rules *once* \-\- that's exactly what makes it so reliable: if you are interacting with a person who haven't violated a rule even once during many years, it seems unlikely that the person would violate the rule now (that would be a too long con, and you are probably not worth it).

However, this costly signal can also be ruined by someone spreading an unsubstantiated rumor. Which is why people who value their honor react to such situations with offense.

There are cultural differences about the importance of honor. A lot of that depends on whether you have decades of repeated experience with the same people, or you mostly interact with strangers... compared to the traditional cultures we are more in the latter extreme, especially those of us who live in big cities. Caring too much about your honor may seem silly in our environment, like "you boast of something that none of us can verify", but the idea is that in other environments those claims would be verifiable.

Actually, even LessWrong has reinvented some aspects of honor in its karma system. The rule that only people with sufficient karma can vote on certain things, and that votes of people with much karma matter more... yeah, that is "karma" playing the role of (some aspects of) honor. These people are trusted to be rationalists in good standing - as judged by other rationalists in good standing - and therefore they get some privileges, over a rando who create an account yesterday. (From that perspective, attacking someone's trustworthiness is analogical do mass downvoting their comments to reduce their karma. That is a serious breach of rules, which has historically brought LessWrong down on its knees.)

Other situations where people reinvent honor is asking employees for references from their former jobs or their criminal record, or people who date someone investigating their potential partner's social background.

In other words, honor is more important than it is fashionable to admit in our culture. Smart people keep discovering or reinventing it, naive people ignore it and then probabilistically pay the price.

This can be true from a very low resolution view, but this low resolution obscures the dynamics that matter here.

The behavior itself, on the face of it, is 1) an attempt to get people to view them as honest and 2) an attempt to do so *without* engaging with the possibility that they might not be. It comes up when the other person has reason to view them as not honest. "No, we shouldn't think that. It doesn't matter the evidence, I don't want to see it. You should just use my preferred conclusion". It's not exactly an act of honesty.

Perfect honesty is hard. In order to be honest all the time, you have to see through the lies you tell *yourself*, which you expect to be able to get others to buy into. This same drive to "be honest" becomes a drive to be *dis*honest the moment perfect honesty becomes harder to achieve than "people \[myself included\] accept the claim that I'm honest" -- because it can only ever be implemented as the control of perception, and if your ability to see through the deceptions lags your ability to create them then Goodhart happens and honesty fails.

So this defensive behavior *might* correlate with honesty in other cases, if there's more variance in drive to be seen as honest than there is in ability to see through self deceptions. But it also might not. It's an empirical question.

What looking closely gets you either way, is a recognition that regardless whether "I'm honest in all these other cases!" is true, the "therefore you *shouldn't* look at the evidence on this case!" is itself an example of dishonesty.

So are honest people ever touchy about their honesty? Depends on how strict you're being about giving them the badge of "honest". You might want the bar to be low enough to give them a pass, and many might not clear the bar if you don't. But people that *are being honest* don't. And honesty at the point where honesty is questioned is necessary for limiting the Goodhart leakage.

People usually seem to hate it when negotiating counterparties try to create a pretense of a high-trust, non-adversarial setting. In fact, Wyatt's behavior in the story could be interpreted not simply as a frustrated outburst, but as calculated. He is announcing a "grim trigger" policy: defect on me, and I commit to defecting on you indefinitely. If credible, that would enable Dagny, even if she'd prefer to defect on a cooperating, to cooperate, and it thereby enables Wyatt to cooperate as well.  
  
By contrast, if one negotiating partner acts as if they're being "naively high trust," as Dagny wishes she could do, it in turn sets up their opponent to defect, no matter what. If the commitment is credible, then they are telling their counterparty it's safe to defect. If not, then the counterparty needs to defect anyway.

In an adversarial, potentially exploitative setting, where agents can read each other's policies, it's necessary for self-defense to be able to defect. Since human counterparties can't read each other's minds, displays of aggressively threatening behavior is a small costly signal that they're capable of reciprocating in bigger ways should their counterparty defect. So precisely the opposite behavior of what is necessary to sustain a high-trust environment.

One read on Altman might be that he is privately consistently adversarial, and he's reading his counterparties (Ilya in this case) and trying to figure out if they think he's high-trust or adversarial. If they read him as high-trust, then Altman will give high-trust signals, like being touchy about being distrusted, because that buys him the cooperation he needs to set up his defections. If they don't read him as high-trust, then he gives them the adversarial signals that show he's capable of defecting back.

Allowing the Potemkin safety inspection may be seen as perfectly ambiguous: see him as high-trust, and you can offer the evidence of the inspection. See him as a capable adversary, and you can read the calculated meaninglessness of it as a sign of strength.

There are two fundamentally different things someone might tell you:

*   "I don't trust you, because I don't do this 'trust' thing *in general*; if you want to convince me, show me the evidence"
*   "people should trust each other and I trust most people... but I don't trust *you*, Zack, *specifically*"

You seem to treat everything the former way, which sounds like an error someone on the autistic spectrum would make. (That means, LessWrong population is vulnerable to taking this kind of wrong advice.)

If it indeed is the former situation, even the Bible agrees with you: "Let your *yes* mean yes, and your *no* mean no. Anything more than this comes from the evil one." (Matthew 5:37)

In that case, you should respond with: Okay, let's discuss evidence. Are there certificates and references I could show you? Contracts I should sign? Generate some new legible evidence for the future?

(Depends on the specific thing you don't trust me about. If it is a business deal, let's sign a contract with specific penalties. If you are afraid that I will murder you if you meet me, tell your friends where you are going, and make a photo of us two and upload it to the cloud when we meet.)

If you are too paranoid and *nothing* can convince you... well, sounds like your problem not mine, you are locking yourself out of many potentially beneficial human interactions. And you are free to do so!

.

This all is different from when you live in a society where generally almost everyone trusts everyone, and suddenly someone comes with "Zack, I don't trust *you*". At least, you should be curious why, because the consequences are potentially dangerous, if more people start doing this. You might get locked out of the mutually beneficial network of trust, and lose all the associated advantages. Depending on circumstances, this might even be worse than living in a society where no one trusts no one, because it will be hard to compete with your neighbors if they are trusted but you are not.

If you did something wrong that has reduced the person's trust in you, you should know what specifically. Maybe it is that person's quirk (they simply don't like your face because it reminds them of someone they met a decade ago), then you can probably do nothing about it, you lost this one person. It still might make sense to threaten consequences lest they spread this attitude to others.

But if there is some character assassination going on, some campaign to reduce everyone's trust in *you specifically*, then you definitely want to know about that, the sooner the better. Feeling offended may be the nature's way to say "give me all the fucking evidence against my trustworthiness you have, so that I can challenge it in open". It also signals that you disagree, which is not strong evidence, but I'd say it is a kind of weak evidence -- at least it signals that it is *not* the kind of situation where everyone distrusts you for "everyone knows" reasons that even you are not trying to challenge (so if someone happens to claim that this *is* such situation, you have put doubt to that statement).

A [Vulcan rationalist](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/hollywood-rationality) might argue that even these statements and inquiries could be made with a calm face, but on planet Earth, emotions *are* a signal (even if not 100% reliable) of sincerity.
