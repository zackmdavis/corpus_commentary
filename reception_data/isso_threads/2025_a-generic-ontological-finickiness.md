There is also an aspect where saying that you have concerns about the boundaries of categories risks sounding insane to normal people. My own experience as someone who believes

> “Male and female are a result of anisogamy leading to predictable downstream traits that cluster together. Adding people with cross-gender identities to them on the basis of their cross-gender identity or hormonal treatments decreases the predictive power of these categories. This is concerning and we ought not make this change in language use.”

...is that it requires very prolonged arguments about sex and definitions that go nowhere. When I attempt to make the argument, people seem to view it as rigidity rather than a philosophical stance. And that's when they bother to engage at all. When I attempt to write it out my concerns, it is so long that nobody wants to read it.

Internally, many people do in fact have theory #0 - that a word is being used in a manner that does not make sense and that failure to make sense is a sort of affront to truth. But this is not an argument that seems to resonate easily. Since this is an issue of policy as much as philosophy, it is more legible to make arguments on the basis of fairness, women’s rights, the efficacy of transition treatments, etc. than it is to argue that we are weakening the predictive power of words and committing ontological crimes.

If you do experience 'generic ontological fickleness', you know that most people do not seem to consider it a valid reason to be concerned about something, and that you risk coming off as a pedant. Perhaps this may change one day, but seeing how quickly pro-rationality cultures like skeptics and Rationalists decided to stop caring about the utility of categories when it came to sex-related issues does not give me much hope for everyone else. (Arguably there are more holdouts among skeptics and Rationalists than among the broader public, but that is hardly encouraging.)
