> Naïvely, of course, you'd think it would be ideologically validating: L. and his manner and his handwriting were living proof that not all boys are masculine! But everyone knew that—even the smart sexists. No, the disturbing part was that if "feminine" handwriting—potentially—indicated "feminine" behavior more generally, that implied that "femininity" was a valid concept, which was itself not a notion I was inclined to grant. (Because why should a person's reproductive anatomy imply anything else about their mind, even if the occasional exception is admitted to? The whole idea is sexist.)

Masculinity/femininity is actually one of the psychometrically most difficult concepts I've encountered, and as I've studied it I've shifted my opinion on its nature in a constructivist direction. It's also probably the thing that most made me realize I needed to step up my game in terms of psychometric theory, causal inference methods, concept-building and nuance.

The most naive method people seem to use for thinking about masculinity/femininity is to pick some variable with a sex difference and phrase everything in terms of that. For instance, they might pick agreeableness, or digit ratio, or testosterone, or people-things interests, and then assume that 1) this can be used as the core for theories explaining sex differences, gender (non)conformity and gender issues, and 2) this matches your intuitive concept of masculinity-femininity. Further, while some people who follow this strategy are social constructivist and others are sex essentialist in their attitudes towards how masculinity/femininity works, both of those attitude tend to involve assuming that this variable acts in accordance with their philosophy. (I.e. social constructivists assume that sex differences in agreeableness are socialized, sex essentialists assume that they are innate.)

There are many problems with this approach, but perhaps the one readers of this blog are most familiar with is the univariate fallacy: Masculinity/femininity isn't just one thing, it's a whole constellation of variables. So readers of this blog might assume that the goal should be to find a constellation of variables that separate the sexes as much as possible. Maybe if you combine agreeableness and neuroticism and sex drive and libertarian vs communitarian political ideology and ..., you'll get a composite variable that separates the sexes very well, and this composite variable will be a good notion of masculinity/femininity.

Alas, this doesn't work at all. Sure, you get a variable with huge sex differences, but it doesn't really correlate much with anything of interest within the sexes. I've literally tried this, e.g. using a dataset with some items that seem like they'd intuitively be related to masculinity/femininity, I've tried to statistically predict sex with those items, and compared it to just adding the items up without any sex-based weighting. As I optimize it to better and better correlate with sex, it ends up correlating less and less with variables of interest within the sexes, like sexual orientation, self-perceived masculinity/femininity, or gender issues. The issue is basically that you can only really get what you ask for; if you take the multivariate sex-separating axis, you get the multivariate sex-separating axis, but this axis basically just tries to eliminate all within-sex variation and leave you with just the between-sex variation.

I think it may become more intuitive if you reflect a bit on what sorts of variables there exist sex differences on. Like yes, men are expected to be stoic, and there's a sex difference on neuroticism, which well matches how neurotic men seem more feminine. But that's not the only way sex differences work. There's a huge sex difference in feminism, with women being more feminist than men, yet you wouldn't particularly think of feminist women as being more feminine than other women; if anything, the opposite. As another example, there's this trait called "caregiver disgust sensitivity", which is stuff like how disgusting you'd find it to change a baby's diapers or give an elderly person a bath or similar; [men have marginally higher caregiver disgust sensitivity than women, yet masculine men have less rather than more caregiver disgust sensitivity than feminine men](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33231035/). I can definitely picture how a feminine gay man might go "eww" at certain caregiver tasks, whereas a masculine dad might just get them done. And remember sociosexuality! Let's not forget that HSTSs, despite their stereotypical femininity, are plenty sociosexually unrestricted.

The list goes on and on. I think there are complex patterns of reasons for why the different things are as they are. E.g. feminists are not feminine partly because feminism fights for the status of masculine women. But much of the time, such as with sociosexuality or caregiver disgust sensitivity, it seems best explained in terms of gender norms; people are labelled masculine/feminine based on how they relate to what is expected or required of people of their sex.

This actually raises a philosophical point. What is masculinity/femininity? How can I possibly claim that masculinity/femininity might not align with, or might even contradict, sex differences?! Isn't the entire point of masculinity/femininity to be a measure of male-typicality vs female-typicality?

And, well, my answer is that you can define the words how you want, but within the sexes, people have some agreement on the rank-ordering of an abstract notion of "masculinity/femininity", and this induces an axis of individual differences that does not align with the sex-separating axes. (Actually it induces two axes, one for masculinity-femininity among males, and another for masculinity-femininity among females; these axes are not fully correlated. And actually there might be four; I think masculinity and femininity are not fully negatively correlated with each other, though more research is needed.) And if you use a different verbal definition for masculinity/femininity than your subconscious ranking, you're going to end up in trouble. So I think the most central issue in understanding masculinity/femininity is to come up with an account of how and why we classify people as masculine/feminine in the way we do. And so far, I think it's looking good for gender norm models.

Anyway. Implicitly, to some extent you seem to be modelling masculinity/femininity as a "general factor"; with the whole point about e.g. feminine handwriting indicating feminine behaviors in other ways. And to some extent it's true, traits that would be considered masculine or feminine tend to have a pattern of correlations with each other (though often you end up with two uncorrelated factors, a Masculinity factor and a Femininity factor; I think much of that is just due to there being a "general activity factor", similar to how e.g. Dominance and Nurturance are uncorrelated, even though Dominance is disagreeable and Nurturance is agreeable, because both Dominance and Nurturance are extraverted). But this "general factor of masculinity/femininity" isn't the whole story; if it was, it would be easy to measure masculinity/femininity, due to the principle of Indifference of the Indicator. Instead, it seems like there's a set of subfactors or outside factors that are particularly critical for forming masculinity/femininity, and it is necessary to include their variance in the model if one wants a complete measure of masculinity/femininity. The best solution I can think of for this so far is to just measure a shitton of traits and apply statistics to them to extract the dimensions relevant to masculinity/femininity; but getting the data is difficult and takes time.

I think Lippa's approaches give several illustrations of how and why the sex-differentiating axis is not the same as masculinity/femininity.

So first of all, Richard Lippa's gender diagnosticity/sex diagnosticity scales are not actually a sex diagnosticity scale. He wanted to compute what psychologists call "internal reliability": roughly speaking, in a multi-item test, "internal reliability" refers to how much the different items agree with each other. But computing this requires multiple different items, which you would not get if you just find the axis that separates the sexes.

So Richard Lippa breaks his vocational interests test up into a bunch of small sets of items, and then computes sex diagnosticity within each set. This gives him multiple sex diagnosticity microtests, which he can then treat as "items" that he averages together for an overall sex diagnosticity score, and which he can compute internal reliability for.

But this approach does not actually yield the sex-separating axis. Because if there e.g. is an item that does very well at separating the sexes in its own unique way, then this item will only be present in one microtest, and its contribution will be divided away once one takes the average over the microtests. And similarly if there is a group of items that together can well predict sex using some subtle covariances, but which individually do not correlate much with sex, then they are unlikely to land in the same microtest, and therefore unlikely to get counted.

So what's left? Well if there's a sex difference in a higher-order factor that affects many items, then Lippa's approach will definitely be able to catch that. In general, Lippa's approach will also yield pretty similar results to just classifying jobs as male-skewed, female-skewed, or neutral, and looking at the proportion of male-skewed vs female-skewed jobs one is interested in.

(One intuitive way to understand this, perhaps: If you looked for the sex-separating axis for human body shape, then you would put basically all the weight on genitalia. But genitalia is only present in a single part of the body, so Lippa's scoring approach would not count genitalia much. Instead his approach would emphasize something like body hair or allometry, since it shows up in lots of parts of the body. There's an obvious "natural abstraction" perspective on this.)

So I see his approach as a sort of importance-weighted interests sex difference measure. And importance-weighted measures are one of the examples I mentioned as likely being more useful than the true sex-separating axis.

(And indeed if I use Lippa-style scoring, I get more correlation with homosexuality and gender issues and self-identified masculinity-femininity, at the expense of less correlation with sex.)
