Gender-neutral pronouns would be an important social technology, if we had them.  As it is, there is no way to use them without playing the game.  You just wind up calling everything an ox or an elk, in an attempt to strip the loyalty context from your reference to the deer.  But it doesn't work, because there's loyalty context embedded in your choice of gender-neutral pronoun!

Singular "They" is the pro-normalizing-transgender position. Unlike "ze" and other neologisms, singular "they" is allegedly a normal, natural-sounding usage, which supposedly does not call attention to the person being referenced.  But that's wrong!  Using "they" for a specific, known, or named individual is not a normal or historically-typical usage.  It sounds awkward, attracts attention, and genuinely confuses people who aren't in the know on this particular signalling game.  By contrast, a neologism tells people "I am using a term which you might not be familiar with, for an idea you might not be familiar with".  This is right and proper, I think:  Gender-neutral singular pronouns are a new addition to the language, and the main reason for having them is to refer to social categories of people that are newly legitimate (trans people) or outright new (non-binary) in the culture of speakers.  And yet, adopting "ze" on these sensible grounds has become staking a position as someone who cares insufficiently about trans people.

A norm around "not outing people" has fuzzy boundaries: someone definitely passes but you happen to know they are trans some other way, you think someone looks trans but you think someone who knows few trans people wouldn't know, or you are pretty sure everyone knows but still think it would still draw less attention to that person if everyone just coordinates around what that person seem to be going for. This certainly has an element of loyalty to it even if that not its main purpose, in terms of how unlikely it has to be you are really outing someone (or how unlikely that outing someone would have bad consequences) before you decide you don't care, and that even the ability to reliably do this implies a certain amount of in-group familiarity.

I have an unvalidated impression that straight (homosexual) trans people are more likely to pass but also more likely to be genuinely androgynous, in a shift away from the "does not pass at all" part of the distribution. (There's also I think an incentive--in the presence of compelling practical/social reasons to obscure someone's sex--to resolve any hesitation by being like, well we are not lying we are just talking about something else.) That might be a natural way that norms broadens even without talking about groups who might uh... be getting different things out of it. I saw pronoun circles a decade ago (relative to mandatory-on-every-zoom-call pronouns now) almost exclusively in contexts where many people might genuinely be perceived differently by different people.  It's clear that it does mostly function as a "pure" loyalty test now, but it's less clear that the recent-predecessor norms are exactly that. I think this makes it harder to walk back entirely because it leaves people open to the accusation that they won't tolerate even a (supposedly) small amount of inconvenience to avoid Dramatic Bad Consequences.

There's imo a loose equivalent to a norm where many people would repeat the known-to-the-speaker-to-be-wrong pronouns someone uses to refer to their same-sex partner even if they think that person is probably obviously(-to-everyone-else) gay. There are still important asymmetries though in that in the relationship context everyone recognizes that using the wrong set of pronouns is cumbersome/costly and that it's a response to stigma rather than an expression of inviolable identity.
