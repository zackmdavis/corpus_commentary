The first half of your post is a strong illustration of a feature of Blanchardianism that is very annoying to me as a psychometrician, and I would also argue "very wrong", though that is much more sensitive to the validity of psychometrics and such.

> Sometimes I want to categorize people by gynephilic/androphilic sexual orientation: this helps me make sense of how lesbians are masculine compared to other females, and gay men are feminine compared to other males. (That is, it looks like homosexuality—not the kind of trans people Scott and I know—is probably a brain intersex condition, and the extreme right tail of homosexuality accounts for the kind of trans people we mostly don't know.)

To me, "this helps me make sense of how lesbians are masculine compared to other females, and gay men are feminine compared to other males" is an absolutely bizarre example of where the value in classifying people by sexual orientation comes from. Instead I'd say the value comes from making sense of how "people's attraction to others isn't just uncorrelated across sexual targets and time, but instead someone who is attracted to one man at one time is also likely attracted to another man at a different time". Psychometrically, I'd be using a latent factor or latent class model.

This is illustrative of a feature of psychometrics that Blanchardians tend to oppose, namely in psychometrics it is very easy to justify the creation of a new category (just notice how you've got a bunch of correlations between stuff), and meanwhile Blanchardians tend to be extremely sparse (I think they prefer "parsimonious"?) about the categories available. When I reason about psychology, I frequently think "hm, I wonder what categories would be natural to think about here, I will run a study to find out", meanwhile when debating with Blanchardians they typically seem ideologically opposed to even discussing their categories or how to test a study to investigate the subject.

> And so, given that I already don't have much use for "if you are a sex, and you're attracted to that sex" as a category of analytical interest, because I think gay men and lesbians are different things that need to be studied separately, "if you identify as a gender, and you're attracted to that gender" (with respect to "gender", not sex) comes off even worse. What causal mechanism could that correspond to?> Imagine a Bayesian network with real-valued variables with a cause C at the root, whose influence propagates to many effects (E₂ ← E₁ ← C → E₃ → E₄ ...). If someone proposes a theory about what happens to the Ei when C is between 2 and 3 or between 5 and 6 or above 12, that's very unparsimonious: why would such a discontinuous hodge-pause of values for the cause, have consistent effects?

I don't think this is the correct way of translating it to a Bayesian network. Instead I'd more translate it to something like:

Cₓ₁ →E₁ ← Cᵧ₁

Cₓ₂ →E₂ ← Cᵧ₂

where 1 means women, 2 means men, Cᵧ means attracted to, and Cₓ means identifies as, and E₂ means attracted to being. So it's not really like lesbians and gay men get lumped together, at least not in the same way that lesbians and gynephilic trans women are lumped together. Rather the model is asserting that there is a symmetry between lesbians and gay men.

The model does lump lesbians and trans women together. I think it is correct to lump cis lesbians and transbians together along the axis of gynephilia. Sometimes Blanchardians object, but those objections aren't really compatible with "it looks like homosexuality is probably a brain intersex condition", though paradoxically AFAICT there is overlap in the Blanchardians who object and the Blanchardians who believe homosexuality is a brain intersex condition, and I think the resolution to this paradox is just that Blanchardians pretend to have some deep theory when really they are just blindly opposed to comparing trans women and cis women? Idk.

Anyway, what's probably more controversial is that it lumps trans women and cis lesbians together as both having a female gender identity. Here I'd say that at least psychometrically there sure does seem to be such a thing as a gender identity, or perhaps rather there are multiple correlated and interrelated things. E.g. you can ask people about their preferred sexed appearance to have, and it will be quite correlated in various standard ways.

Now a symmetry is a quite strong claim to make, but they do occur in lots of places in the world. Admittedly if they were just three unrelated variables, the probability of there being a symmetry would be infinitesimal, but here the variables are all related with just the target swapped out. In fact it seems to me that Blanchardians would presumably also be inclined to admit a symmetry, it'd just look somewhat different, namely:

Cₓ₁ ←E₁ ← Cᵧ₁

Cₓ₂ ←E₂ ← Cᵧ₂

That is, rather than identity contributing to autophilia, Blanchardians would propose that autophilia contributes to identity. (And it's not even like Blanchardians would disagree with there being a collider structure! They just insist that the additional cause of autophilia is some mysterious unobservable variable called "erotic target location error".)

> The surprising thing is that if you look at what trans women say to each other when the general public isn't looking, you see the same stories over and over again (examples from /r/MtF: "I get horny when I do 'girl things'. I think these lines of evidences are far weaker than what you do, because the only thing it shows is that there is a large number of trans women who have some sort of autogynephilia-like expression going on. But autogynephilia can and does exist to various degrees, and meanwhile a lot of Blanchardians are insistent that the autogynephilia they are talking about is extremely intense. Is this what most trans women claim to experience? Also I feel like if cis women were commonly autogynephilic, it would still show up differently in discussion for cultural reasons.

> While the promise of psychological research is that it might teach us things about ourselves that we don't already know, I still mostly expect it to all add up to normality—to retrodict the things we've already observed without the research.

I don't actually think this is true?

Factor analysis benefits from a lot of data. Comprehensive data on each individual, across many different individuals. I've sometimes tried to make up algorithms that stitch together a factor model out of tons of tiny fragments of information, but in my experience it doesn't work very well. Maybe there are some ways to make it work better, but I sort of doubt it.

I think what happens instead is that the things people learn without psychological research are different than the things people learn with psychological research? For instance, while stitching together tiny fragments of information to a factor model is hard, stitching together tiny fragments of information to a stereotype model is easy. That is, in each situation you observe, various variables e.g. demographic factors like sex or age or race are usually visible, so you can use these in a brain-level "regression model" to predict the situations you end up with, and therefore end up with a rich set of stereotypes/archetypes to reason with.

This model, that without psychological research one learns an impoverished perspective, also feels like it explains some oddities when talking with you and other Blanchardians/skeptics of psychological research. To me, differential psychology is mostly made out of traits. A lot of the interesting questions are about what variables load on what traits in which ways and so on. Meanwhile, you sort of bareeeeely acknowledge the existence of traits when discussing the statistics of overlapping variables, but most of your thoughts seem to focus on demographics, and pretty much the first and only thing you feel like doing with traits is compute the demographic-separating axis.

Or in other words, most of my focus is on how people differ within demographics, and I think people can't notice within-demographic differences from "what we've already observed without the research", so instead most people focus on how demographics differ from each other and collapse all the within-demographic variance into noise.

With all that said, I agree with being skeptical about Scott Alexander's and Aella's survey results. Mostly I think the whole issue about autogynephilia in cis women is stupid, because mostly people seem to be interested in it in order to use sex as an instrumental variable to test the direction of causality between autogynephilia and gender identity, and this seems stupid because exclusion restriction is absurdly implausible wrt. sex (somewhat overlaps with what you've said).

I also agree that probably gender identity is downstream of autophilias, though my reasoning is different. I'd argue that we generally understand sexuality to influence preferences rather than vice versa, and so we should expect that to generalize to autophilias too.

Here, I don't mean that any category you want is valid. Rather, there's a standard procedure for making categorize:

Collect a bunch of features you care about
Perform dimensionality reduction to see what the structure of the features are
If many of the features are strongly related to each other, that's a valid thing to classify by

For example, to create a category for biological sex, one might grab a bunch of sex-related features (genitals, gonads, hormones, chromosomes, ...), notice that they are strongly correlated, and create a category based on that. Similarly, for biological race, one might look at how all the different positions in the genome are correlated with each other and create a category based on that.

This sort of procedure might sound like something Blanchardians are interested in, since they care about sex and race, but empirically Blanchardians seem to be opposed to this methodology. My best guess is that the issue is that Blanchardianism is about invalidating people's identities, whereas in psychosocial cases the richest source of information about a person comes from asking that person about themselves, so permitting this method in psychosocial contexts is not acceptable to Blanchardians.

Or in DAGs, if X is "Do I want to be her or am I attracted to her?", Y is gynephilia, Z is feminine identity, and W is autogynephilia, Zack's model for the causality seems to be Y → W → X in the case of males and Y → X ← Z in the case of females, but we could just label it as Y → X ← Z(←W)  in both cases, and it would perhaps work out.

I believe this model makes different empirical predictions.
