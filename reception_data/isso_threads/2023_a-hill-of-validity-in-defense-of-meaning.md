I think the strongest argument against (well, not really "against", at an angle...) I can think of would be this: you're treating rationality as a safety blanket, not a tool. The goal of rationality is to think optimally, in a certain sense; it is not to ensure that others behave in a certain way. Rationality is not a compact, it's not a creed, it's not an ethos. If you demand, in the name of rationality, that somebody act a certain way, particularly in public, you are using it for something else: a way to force your social environment to be legible and consistent. This is extremely common for ("autists", he says, then backspaces) people who have problems parsing social reality: rationality seems to offer them a way to bound the danger that their ingroup can pose; even being unable to understand them fully, it would seem to limit the cognitive demands they can place on you. But the truth is, the Way is and has always been for yourself only. Others may walk on it, they may depart; but the laws of probability don't change thereby. The concern of rationality is only the landscape itself; it does not even demand that you follow the way, merely predicts what will happen when you fail to do so.

(Has Eliezer acted inconsistently with this view? Yes. Has the community? Yes.)
