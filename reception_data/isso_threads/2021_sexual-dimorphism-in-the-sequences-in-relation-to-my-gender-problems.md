There might be something more I should discuss.

There's the whole category/philosophy discussion, where people ask whether e.g. "depression" is a real category. But I find that there tends to be at least three different questions that have somewhat different answers:

What are the core defining properties of the variable that your categories exist within?
What correlates and especially what reliable indicators does it have?
What are easy ways to talk about and compress it?

It seems to me that most of your philosophy of sex has focused on the third question. For that question, you say that biological sex categories should be based on efficient and useful compressions of sex characteristics. This is a valid answer and in accordance with much prior rationalist philosophy, but I think there are other perspectives that are worthwhile too.

And one such perspective is revealed by:

> because they're male, right!?

"Because" is an answer to a "why" question, i.e. it's a question of causality, not just correlation. E.g. one wouldn't say that the reason someone has a penis because they marked "male" on a questionnaire asking about their sex, even though that is a very strong correlate. Rather one would say that he has a penis because of prenatal masculinization, which in turn is triggered by male gonads, which develop due to XY chromosomes.

So in order for "because they're male" to work as an answer, being male has to be a cause of acting in male-typical ways. This is problematic if "male" is just defined as some variable that highly correlates with (i.e. compresses) male-typical characteristics.

So it's about the first question, "What are the core defining properties of the variable that your categories exist within?". You can take the core defining property of maleness to be whatever is the common cause of male characteristics. (Empirically this appears to be genetics at conception, basically XY vs XX though with some rare complications.) If one defines a variable by common causes in this way, then the variable should generally exist independently of its outputs; e.g. it's possible to be male but then coincidentally have a bunch of pther factors that shift each of the sex characteristics away from the male mean.

Usually, one makes an approximation that the common cause is equal to an aggregation of the outputs. This is a good approximation for many reasons (the signal adds up and the noise cancels out, plus in cases where the noise just happens to align, at least the error is going to have less practical consequences), but it is not always accurate.

So anyway, a lot of the categorization philosophy has assumed a different answer to "What are the core defining properties of the variable that your categories exist within?" than "It is the common cause of a set of variables", namely "It exists to compress a set of variables". And the latter is a valid endeavor too, but it is worth keeping in mind that only the former answers "Why?" questions, not the latter.

The immediate thing that stands out from this correlation matrix is that the measure is kind of weird. For instance, those who were more OK with "coarse male anatomy" like body odor and body hair and belly fat were also less likely to find periods problematic. So these did not align into a strict male is better vs female is better spectrum. I suspect partly it's because my measure was weird. I asked people to say aversively or positively each thing would impact their life, which people tended to find hard to answer. In order for it to make sense for people to answer it for the opposite sex, I instructed them to imagine they had been magically and permanently transformed into the opposite sex. This is something I did for my convenience in analysis, and to try to keep it more relevant for what I wanted to correlate it to, namely net preferences to be the opposite sex.

And I suspect partly it just makes sense. Like there's a sort of Essential Similarity between smelly male bodies and female periods; they're both Gross Sexed Stuff. There's a way in which it's interesting in theory, because such factors should have limited opportunity to cause gender issues, since they apply to both male and female bodies.

Anyway, the other thing that stands out is a near-total failure of any of the 16.5 factors to correlate much with gender dysphoria/cross-gender ideation. While autogynephilia has a correlation of 0.55, the strongest of the factors has a correlation of -0.32, and it seems near tautological. It's the coarse male anatomy factor, which is about whether it is OK to have a hairy, smelly body with an impractically bulgy penis and an uneven concentration of fat on the belly. (I thought scoring low in this factor was basically the same as male gender dysphoria!? Yet it is mostly independent. 😲 😂)

Now I just want to give a reminder not to take the correlation matrix too seriously because the sample size is dangerously low (are masochism and masculinity truly the strongest correlates of liking to be expected to present feminine if one was a woman? I doubt).

But anyway, the lack of correlation raises a question: It's all well and good that people say these are things that are good/bad about being male/female. But if they don't correlate with gender issues, are they actually relevant to this conversation in any way?

My defenses of the "yes" answer to the above question:

I did not bring these factors up as a thing that makes trans women different from cis men in causing their transition. Rather, I brought them up as human-universal factors (well, a subset of them) which would show up in one's feelings about being male/female. Now definitionally something that is shared evenly across both cis men, cis women, trans men and trans women cannot be the distinguishing factor between trans women and cis men which causes the trans women to transition.

The survey is kind of messy in various ways (low sample size, confusing measures).


I also have a defense of "no" to the question, though:

While at first I only brought up the factors as being human-universal factors, after I had thought about it for a while, I thought they should also be able to explain a good chunk of Gaussian gender issues (I predicted a correlation with gender issues of "0.4 to 0.8", though with unclear causality, in the new sexology discord channel). Indeed, that is why I decided to do the survey, in the hope that they could be a good marker of Gaussian gender issues.

So, what's the rebuttal to the "human universals" argument I gave in the "yes" defense? Well, presumably these factors vary from person to person. Otherwise I wouldn't have been able to do the factor analysis, and also often it is just straightforwardly sensible that they would vary. In fact I had a theory: the variance of a variable tends to be roughly proportional to its size. For instance humans are 169 cm tall, with a within-sex 95th percentile range of like 24 cm (can't be bothered to compute the pooled SD). Meanwhile, carpenter ants vary from 1 cm to 1.3 cm. Here humans vary much more in size, partly because due to being bigger we have much more space in the range of sizes to vary in.

My logic for these gender factors was basically the same. The factors of gender satisfaction that should vary as much as possible from person to person would probably be the ones that people feel the most strongly about. (We can sometimes see straightforward mechanistic reasons to expect that scaling, e.g. if some people want children and other people don't want children, then the impact of factors relating to childhaving pressure on those who don't want children would scale by the amount of pressure, while it wouldn't scale at all on those who do want children.) And a priori one of the main ways we would expect people to form a feeling that they would rather be the opposite sex would be that there's some concrete factor in their lives that would be improved as the opposite sex, because that is consequentialist reasoning and humans are approximately consequentialist for instrumental convergence reasons.

But despite all of this logic, it basically failed, and scored Yet Another Win for autogynephilia as the almighty correlate of male gender issues. If we take this failure at face value, I don't think it necessarily absolutely destroys the case for Gaussian gender issues, but it does raise some questions about what the causes of those would even be, if not these sorts of factors. However, I am also unsure whether we should take it at face value, given the low sample size and improvable measurement.

A final note: The items tended to correlate with "gender oppression" (e.g. "I feel that I am not taken seriously because of my sex", "I feel that society is biased against my sex") and "gender norm misfit" (e.g. "I feel ashamed of my gender nonconforming traits", "I wish I had an easier time fitting in among people of my sex") more than they correlated with gender dysphoria/cross-gender ideation. A priori, it seems like this makes sense; a lot of the factors have straightforward relations to societal gender norms. Now, gender oppression and gender norm misfit are both correlated with but mostly independent from gender dysphoria/cross-gender ideation. So it is possible that this is a [good instance of the nontransitivity of correlation](https://twitter.com/tailcalled/status/1545326284942761988). If so, it's not that these factors are literally independent of gender dysphoria/cross-gender ideation, but instead "merely" that they have very limited impact.

In theory, I think it would be worthwhile for anyone who thinks the origin of gender issues should be untangled to study this in further detail. If nothing else, increasing the sample size so we can get the definitive words in how strong the correlations are. In practice, this road of untangling is probably going to boil down to a ton of microcauses, which are not going to be interesting to people, so this line of research is unlikely to ever get developed to the point where the questions get answered.
