He has this [fixation about not "lying"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xdwbX9pFEr7Pomaxv/meta-honesty-firming-up-honesty-around-its-edge-cases), and I absolutely believe him about that—it's just that his notion of not-lying (which he calls "honesty") [is almost completely useless](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/MN4NRkMw7ggt9587K/firming-up-not-lying-around-its-edge-cases-is-less-broadly), because smart people are good at deceiving-without-technically-lying by finding the ambiguities and edge-cases in natural language and driving a truck through them, in a way that less-smart people are not impressed by.

Among my select group of language-lawyer nerd friends, I can expect to sometimes get away with [apophasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apophasis): to say "I won't ask for this, because that would be rude, but I wish [...]" and have that be understood as different from just asking for the thing, because of the extra layer of indirection. Normal people ... do not bother tracking that distinction. Why would they? The statement was optimized for communicating the request while abusing the use–mention distinction in order to give me the out of not having "really said it."

Similarly, it's possible to craft public declarations that are carefully optimized to favor one side of a political dispute for instrumental reasons, while retaining a surface appearance of being neutral (that is, not the product of instrumental political scheming) on the grounds of not having "really said it", and without any individual sentence being unambiguously "false" as the speaker meant it.
