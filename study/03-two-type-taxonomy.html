Chapter 3Blanchard's Dangerous Idea: The Two-Type Taxonomy and Its Literature

Davis's gender writing draws on a body of sexology associated with Ray Blanchard, J. Michael Bailey and Anne Lawrence, which he defended for a decade and which its best-known critics, Julia Serano foremost, reject. The position bundles five claims of different strength: that male-to-female transsexuals fall into two clusters; that erotic arousal at the thought of being female is common in one of them; that this arousal causes the wish to transition; that the late-onset cluster has no feminized brain; and that a substantial majority of trans women today belong to it. The correlations on which the typology rests are well established, and the critics concede them. The frequency of the arousal among non-androphilic trans women is high in every sample that has measured it. The other three are less settled than the polemics on either side assume; the claim about brains, which Davis made his stated thesis, rests on very little evidence either way. Over the decade his considered statements came to match this assessment more closely than his polemics did, and the chapter ends by asking what that gap amounts to.

1. Five claims

One of the blog's first two posts was a joke at the expense of Serano's account of the two media stereotypes of trans women, which Davis suggested might be related to some underlying statistical reality ("Apophenia?," UUT, 5 Sep 2016). A month later a vignette had its narrator wearing a T-shirt reading LATE-ONSET GENDER DYSPHORIA IN MALES IS NOT AN INTERSEX CONDITION, YOU LYING BASTARDS ("Introductions," UUT, 19 Oct 2016). More than seven years later, when LessWrong commenters said they could not find his object-level claim, Davis stated the same proposition without the insult and labeled it his Empirical Claim: late-onset gender dysphoria in males is not an intersex condition. (Davis, comment on "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them," LW, 2 Jan 2024).

Between the two lies a decade of argument for a position larger than that sentence. It can be separated into five claims. (a) Descriptive typology: natal males who seek to transition fall into two groups, one attracted to men, feminine from early childhood and transitioning young, the other attracted to women, to both sexes or to neither, not conspicuously feminine as children, and transitioning later. (b) Prevalence of the arousal pattern: in the second group, erotic arousal at the thought or image of oneself as a woman, which Blanchard named autogynephilia and classed as a paraphilia, is common, and it is not common in women. (c) Cause: that arousal, or the sexual orientation it expresses, is the principal cause of the second group's gender dysphoria. (d) The negative claim: the second group's condition is not a brain-intersex condition; their brains and psychology are not feminized in the way the first group's may be. (e) Population: under present conditions in Western countries, most trans women belong to the second group and are, in the 2023 formulation that Chapter 2 examined as testimony, essentially, guys like me ("Blanchard's Dangerous Idea and the Plight of the Lucid Crossdreamer," UUT and LW, 8 Jul 2023; hereafter "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea").

The claims depend on one another less than the polemics suggest. The causal claim needs (b), since the arousal cannot explain dysphoria in people who lack it, but it does not need two discrete groups: autogynephilia could contribute to transition across a population that is continuous on every variable, and Davis's 2022 model, discussed in §2, says that it does. Nor does the causal claim need the arousal to be absent in women, which is a separate part of (b) with its own evidence. The negative claim is independent of the causal one in both directions. The late-onset group's dysphoria might have a cause that is neither the arousal nor a feminized brain, or some mild neural difference might be the reason the arousal arises, in which case a cause of the kind Davis believes in and an "intersex" feature of the kind he denies would both be present. The population claim, on the weaker of the two readings distinguished in §6, needs only (a) and (b). What the negative claim carries is the argument about the category woman that Chapter 4 examines, whose psychological strand requires that the late-onset group's traits be male-typical. Davis saw part of this early. His 2018 reply to Scott Alexander, after declining to review the evidence (A review of the empirical evidence for the two-type taxonomy is beyond the scope of this post.), wrote that it's possible to reject the hypothesis that autogynephilia is the cause of the second type, while still agreeing that there observationally seem to be at least two types of trans women, and specified what his argument there assumed: I am, however, supposing that the late-onset type or types is either not an intersex condition, or at most, a very mild one ("The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions," UUT, 23 Feb 2018). By 2018, then, the causal claim was optional in his own statement of the position, and the negative claim carried the argument about categories. Whether he wrote as if this were so is the question of §8.

Most of the papers Davis cites are in a collection he maintains on his blog. The collection is weighted toward the theory's proponents, though it includes Serano, Charles Moser and Jaimie Veale. This chapter starts from that collection and adds sources outside it: the 2015 United States Transgender Survey, two reviews of brain-imaging research on trans people, the largest pooled imaging analysis to date, a Swedish population survey, and the history of the controversy over Bailey's book. Where the figures Davis cites differ from the papers, the differences are noted.

2. The clusters

The descriptive typology is the oldest and least disputed part of the position. In the paper that set the terms of the later debate, Blanchard divided 163 gender-dysphoric male patients in Toronto into four groups by their scores on scales of erotic attraction to men and to women, then asked each patient a single question: Did you ever feel sexually aroused when putting on females' underwear or clothing? Fifteen of the hundred patients attracted to men said yes; forty-six of the sixty-three in the other three groups did, and the three non-homosexual groups did not differ significantly from each other (Blanchard 1985, Table II). Later work found the same pattern on other variables. In a Dutch clinic sample, the patients attracted to men applied for sex reassignment younger, reported more cross-gender behavior in childhood, were judged more convincing in appearance, and were less often married (Smith et al. 2005). The American Psychiatric Association's manual describes two broad trajectories for development of gender dysphoria: early onset and late onset in natal males, and states that adolescents and adults in the late-onset group frequently engage in transvestic behavior with sexual excitement (DSM-5, as Davis quotes it in "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea"). A review of adult gender dysphoria in the Annual Review of Clinical Psychology reports considerable evidence that sexual orientation in natal males with gender dysphoria is related to their developmental history and much else, while noting that the manual dropped it as a formal specifier (Zucker, Lawrence and Kreukels 2016).

The critics concede the correlations. Serano, whose objections to the theory are the most developed, writes that every single follow-up study has shown that, while the correlations that Blanchard and other researchers prior to him described generally hold true, there are always substantial numbers of exceptions in both directions (Serano 2020, 766–767). Davis quoted the concession in 2022 ("The Two-Type Taxonomy Is a Useful Approximation for a More Detailed Causal Model," UUT, 8 Jul 2022; hereafter "Useful Approximation"). Moser, in the most general academic critique of the theory, says the same: I am not suggesting that acknowledging a history of some autogynephilia is not correlated with a non-homosexual sexual orientation among MTFs, but correlation does not imply causation. (Moser 2010, 806). The concession is to correlations. It does not extend to clusters in the stronger sense, dense regions of the population separated by sparse ones, and still less to two discrete kinds, and the exceptions are not trivial. In a non-clinical sample of 234 trans women, Veale and colleagues found the autogynephilia measures correlated with attraction to women, as the theory predicts, but also that In contrast to Blanchard’s theory, however, those transsexuals classified as autogynephilic scored higher on average on Sexual Attraction to Males than those classified as non-autogynephilic (Veale, Clarke and Lomax 2008, abstract).

On the question of discreteness the evidence is weaker than Davis's early writing assumed. In 2016 he answered Ozy Brennan's remark that the factors may or may not be correlated, but there are many exceptions with indignation: Summarizing correlations is the entire point of making a taxonomy. ("Reply to Ozymandias on Autogynephilia," UUT, 1 Oct 2016). Correlated traits do not by themselves imply discrete groups. Veale applied taxometric procedures, which test whether a latent structure is categorical or continuous, to an online sample of 308 trans women, and reported that Results of the two other taxometric procedures, MAMBAC and MAXCOV, showed greater support for a dimensional latent structure. (Veale 2014). Lawrence replied that the analysis was invalid: the sample contained too few androphilic participants for a two-type structure to be detectable, probably 18 (5.8 %) at most, and three of the four indicators did not measure the variables that define Blanchard's types (Lawrence 2014, 1679–1680). If Lawrence's count is right, the dimensional finding is evidence about structure within the non-androphilic group, where autogynephilia then looks continuous, and it does not test the division between the groups. Nothing in Davis's collection supplies a stronger test. The correlations are established; whether they mark two kinds is open, with one contested taxometric study bearing on it.

Davis came to this view himself, prompted by a reader. In January 2021 tailcalled, an independent researcher who had run many surveys on autogynephilia and who started out sympathetic to the theory, accused the 2016 reply of a motte-and-bailey move, from correlations in its first sentence to two and only two different psychological conditions with different etiologies in its last (tailcalled, "Causality Is Essential: Reply to MTSW on Autogynephilia," Survey Anon's Gender Blog, 1 Jan 2021). Davis's reply conceded in its title and its first paragraph: But as Tailcalled's response points out, this is just wrong! What he withdrew was the argument, and he kept the conclusion on stated grounds: I'm making that scientific judgment between theories based on my own parsimony intuitions applied to everything I've read and seen, and Trying to get away with "You agree to the bimodality, therefore you must agree to the taxonomy" would be both lazy and wrong ("You Are Right and I Was Wrong: Reply to Tailcalled on Causality," UUT, 1 Feb 2021). The same post dismissed the main rival account of the bimodality, Veale, Clarke and Lomax's identity-defense model, as a stretch; §4 returns to it.

"Useful Approximation" (2022) is the considered result. It replaces the two types with a causal graph in which three things can make transition attractive to a male: femininity, autogynephilia, and cultural factors. A threshold model joins them: males transition if the sum of their levels of femininity, autogynephilia, and culture-related-trans-disposition exceed some threshold. The two types appear because sexual orientation pushes the first two factors in opposite directions, gay men being more feminine and straight men more liable to autogynephilia, so that among those who cross the threshold the factors are negatively correlated. Davis noted that a threshold also produces negative correlation by itself, through selection on a collider, and made an empirical claim about the size of the two effects: I'm claiming that the degree of bimodality induced by the effects of sexual orientation is substantially greater than that accounted for by the conditioning-on-a-collider effect. He also allowed that the second type might be a residual category, an androphilic vs. not-otherwise-specified taxonomy, since it is hard to tell autogynephilia apart from other reasons a not overtly feminine male might come to a gender clinic.

The model makes the typology an approximation whose accuracy is an empirical matter, which is an advance on the 2016 reply. It can accommodate the cases a strict two-type theory cannot, such as the gynephilic trans woman without autogynephilia, attributed to unusually strong cultural factors, or the autogynephilic trans woman who is more feminine than autogynephilic men who do not transition. Accommodating them after the fact is a weaker achievement than predicting how often they occur, and the model, as tailcalled pointed out in the comments, has no numbers with which to do so: A model doesn't state much unless one puts some numbers on it (tailcalled, 9 Jul 2022). The bimodality claim is the kind of statement a fitted model could test and an unfitted one cannot. Davis conceded as much at the close of the post (it would be a lot more work to pin down all these claims more rigorously). On (a), his position moved from an overstatement to an approximation whose untested parts he marked as untested.

3. How common, and in whom

The empirical core of "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea" is a paragraph of figures. After a Reddit poll, Davis turned to the journals (Reddit isn't scientific enough for you? Fine. The scientific literature says the same thing.) and cited four studies: Blanchard 1985, 73 percent of non-androphilic patients acknowledging erotic cross-dressing; Lawrence 2005, 90 percent of those with female partners before surgery reporting autogynephilic arousal; Smith et al. 2005, 64 percent of non-homosexual patients reporting arousal while cross-dressing in adolescence; and Nuttbrock et al. 2011, lifetime prevalence of transvestic fetishism among non-homosexual MtFs was 69%.

Three of the four figures are exact. Blanchard's 46 of 63 is 73 percent; Lawrence's heterosexual and bisexual groups together report some history of autogynephilic arousal in 170 of 189 cases, 90 percent; in Smith's sample, 28 of the 44 non-homosexual natal males who answered the question reported arousal, 64 percent. The Nuttbrock figure is a slip. The paper gives the lifetime rate among non-homosexuals as a group in so many words, the non-homosexuals as a group (73.2%); 69.0 percent is its rate of life-course-persistent transvestic fetishism among the heterosexual subgroup (Nuttbrock et al. 2011, 251 and Table 1). Since the correct figure is higher, the error understates Davis's own case.

The selection deserves more notice. The same four studies report the rate in the androphilic group: 15 of 100 in Blanchard's sample, 6 of 15 in Lawrence's, 23 percent in Nuttbrock's, and about 31 percent in Smith's (18 of 58 who answered). The contrast between the groups is large in every study, which is what the typology predicts. The androphilic rates are not near zero, which the strict version of the theory does not predict and which the paragraph leaves out. The measures also differ: a single yes-or-no item about clothing in Toronto, a broader question about arousal to the thought of being female in Lawrence's questionnaire, adolescent arousal while cross-dressing in Smith's. The samples differ in ways that matter for generalizing: a Toronto clinic in the early 1980s; the patients of one Portland surgeon, of whom 32 percent of those eligible returned the questionnaire; a Dutch clinic; and a New York community sample recruited partly from streets and clubs, two-thirds of it androphilic. None is a random sample of trans women. Their agreement on the direction and rough size of the difference, across different decades, countries and recruitment methods, is the strongest evidence for (b) in the four samples Davis chose.

The comparison with women is where the instruments are disputed. Moser gave an autogynephilia scale adapted for women to 29 hospital employees and reported that 93 percent had experienced some erotic arousal to the thought or image of themselves as women and 28 percent frequently (Moser 2009). Lawrence replied that Moser's items did not ask about the defining element, arousal at the thought of being female, and concluded that although Moser may have found something superficially resembling autogynephilia in women, there is little reason to think that he documented genuine autogynephilic arousal in women (Lawrence 2010). Lawrence's objection is partly conceptual. Arousal at the thought of being a woman has no obvious analogue for someone who is one, so the comparison cannot be settled by better sampling until the construct is defined for women, and any definition takes a side. Bailey and Kevin Hsu then gave Blanchard's original scale to four samples of autogynephilic men, four of non-autogynephilic men and two of women, and found the autogynephilic samples far higher and the women similar to the other men (Bailey and Hsu 2022). Serano and Veale objected that the autogynephilic samples had been recruited from communities organized around the fantasy and contained few trans women, so that the study answered a question nobody had asked: Bailey and Hsu’s study strikes us as an exercise in “begging the question.” (Serano and Veale 2022). tailcalled, who is no ally of Serano's, made the same objection independently (Substituting a sample of highly active members of online erotic AGP communities for trans women is an insanely biased methodology!; tailcalled, "Autogynephilia Discourse Is So Absurdly Bad on All Sides," LW, 23 Jul 2023).

Davis's own contribution to this exchange is an argument about measurement. Scott Alexander had found, in his blog's 2020 reader survey, that gay men's arousal at imagining themselves as men was as common as lesbian trans women's arousal at imagining themselves as women, and proposed what he called a boring hypothesis: if you identify as a gender, and you’re attracted to that gender, it’s a natural leap to be attracted to yourself being that gender. (Alexander, "Autogenderphilia Is Common and Not Especially Related to Transgender," Slate Star Codex, 10 Feb 2020). Alexander listed the weaknesses himself, beginning with First, weird Internet samples plausibly have more of every paraphilia. Davis's reply argued that a survey item can mean different things to different populations, as a screening question for bipolar disorder can catch a football fan who was happy and then sad, and that sexuality is the one domain where I think we have strong prior reasons to expect cross-sex empathic inference to fail ("Reply to Scott Alexander on Autogenderphilia," UUT, 18 Dec 2023). Psychometrics calls the requirement measurement invariance, the condition that an instrument measure the same construct in the groups being compared. The point is sound, and it applies to every comparison between groups in this section, on both sides.

Davis did not apply it evenly. The same reply cites a Reddit poll in which 82 percent of respondents on a trans women's forum answered yes to a forced-choice question about a gender/body swap/transformation "fetish" (or similar), and concludes, that makes me think it's a majority. Strictly, the poll raises a different problem from the one Davis pressed against Alexander. It estimates a rate within one population, and invariance concerns comparisons between populations. Its problems are validity and sampling, a loosely worded item answered by the self-selected members of one forum, and those are problems Davis would raise against a survey whose result he doubted. The exemption he gives himself is worded so as not to reach them: my skepticism is specifically about these mass survey questions where we haven't done the extra work to try to figure out whether the question means the same thing to everyone. Once the poll is used to support the further claim that these women are, in his phrase, guys like him, it does become a comparison between populations, between his experience and theirs, and his own objection applies to it in full. Davis states the basis of the asymmetry: a strong prior, from his own experience and from the theory of erotic target location errors (§4), that the male phenomenon and the female one are different things. He called it, in a comment, my No Way No Chance prior re AGP in women (Davis, 15 Jul 2022), and he opened the Alexander reply by admitting he was self-conscious about my answer sounding weirdly overconfident on non-empirical grounds to someone who doesn't already share my parsimony intuitions. That disclosure is the norm of accounting applied to evidence. It lets a reader see what the conclusion rests on, though it does not show that the prior is as strong as Davis holds it. A prior legitimately affects how its holder weighs ambiguous evidence; it cannot make that evidence persuasive to a reader who does not share it, and an essay addressed to such readers owes them the evidence without the prior.

On one point the prior met a test. The reply argued that the trans lesbian's quip about whether she wants to be a woman or to be with her expresses something cis lesbians rarely feel (I'm inclined to doubt it). A commenter, KartoffelElf, pointed out that a web search finds the phrase mostly in cis women's writing. Davis agreed at once: I agree that it was pretty dumb of me to write "I'm inclined to doubt it" based on my anecdotal personal experience without doing a very cheap web search, then argued from the contexts that the women meant something different by it (Davis, 24 Dec 2023). What failed was a checkable claim about who uses the phrase. Whether the uses describe the same experience remained in dispute, and Davis's reinterpretation of them was asserted from his reading of the contexts with no more testing than the original doubt. The stronger claim, made in "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea" to explain why the fantasy is self-defeating (At a minimum, because women aren't autogynephilic!), was answered in the LessWrong thread by Ann, who described herself as a bisexual cis woman and wrote that she found her own body interesting in a way that indeed appears to map to that described by this "autogynephilia" you speak of, and only wouldn't because the very definition tries to include being male (Ann, 9 Jul 2023). Her second clause states the construct problem from the other side. tailcalled added that his surveys found that autogynephilia in cis women and autoandrophilia in cis men does sometimes exist, and held that this refutes the categorical claim while changing little else (tailcalled, 9 Jul 2023).

One document bearing on the instruments is unpublished. In February 2025 Davis drafted a message to SEXNET, the sexology mailing list Bailey runs, about a study by Hsu, James Morandini and S. Rudd that compared male cross-dressers with transfeminine people, found that both groups differed more from control men and women than from each other, and concluded that autogynephilia figured importantly in both (Hsu, Morandini and Rudd 2025, abstract). Davis objected that the paper did not report where it had advertised, and that its lead author's Reddit history showed recruitment concentrated in forums for sharing autogynephilic erotica, while the mainstream trans forums were absent. He applied the same objection to Bailey and Hsu 2022 and said what that study could and could not show: it's important to notice that Bailey and Hsu 2022 itself only addresses (1)., that is, that women are not autogynephilic, not that trans women are. He named the intuition he was resisting, his own: After years of seeing case after case after case of MtFs denying and denouncing "autogynephilia" as a theory, and then turning out to blatantly exhibit the exact symptoms the theory is meant to describe, a certain parsimony intuition starts to kick in. And he stated the standard: We want to design studies that speak for themselves and tell us things about reality that we couldn't have known without the study, not studies that are rigged to convince other people what we already believed for other reasons. (unpublished draft, "sampling methodology concerns about Hsu, Morandini, and Rudd 2025," header date 22 Feb 2025). The objection is the one Serano, Veale and tailcalled had already made in public; what is Davis's is turning it on the researchers he most admires. The recruitment details come from his own reconstruction and were not checked for this study, the paper was read only in abstract, and whether the message was sent is not recorded in the corpus.

In the clinical, surgical and community samples examined here, a large majority of non-androphilic natal males seeking or living after transition report some history of erotic arousal to cross-dressing or to the thought of being female, and a much smaller but real minority of androphilic ones do. How intense and how central the arousal is varies, and the single-item measures of the older studies cannot say. Whether women experience the same thing is unresolved, partly because the studies on each side use instruments or samples the other side has given good reasons to distrust, and partly because the construct has not been defined in a way that applies to both. The categorical claim that women are not autogynephilic is unsupported, and the reports of women like Ann count against it; what they cannot yet show is that the experience they describe is the one the theory names.

4. Cause

The causal claim is that the arousal, or the orientation it expresses, produces the identity and the dysphoria, rather than the reverse or both coming from something else. Davis's case for it in "Useful Approximation" is that the graph's six arrows are each supported, and on this arrow he was direct: I claim the direction of causality is obvious. He gave two arguments.

The first is the pattern Blanchard and Kurt Freund called erotic target location error and Lawrence developed: men attracted to some kind of person or thing sometimes become aroused by the idea of being one, and some go on to want to become one (Freund and Blanchard 1993; Lawrence 2009). If men attracted to amputees sometimes wish to be amputees, the argument runs, then men attracted to women who wish to be women need no separate explanation. The pattern is real. Bailey, Hsu and Henry Jang surveyed men attracted to amputees, to animals and to very obese people, and found that All samples included substantial minorities of men reporting internalized sexual attractions and erotic target identity inversions specific to their external sexual attractions (Bailey, Hsu and Jang 2023, abstract). The samples were recruited from internet communities for those interests, and the same abstract reports that each internalized attraction correlated with autogynephilia, which is what tailcalled's alternative, a general factor of unusual sexual interests, would also predict. The evidence shows that the inversion exists across targets. It does not show which way the arrow runs in trans women.

The second argument is in the parenthesis that follows the claim of obviousness: If you want to argue that it goes the other way—that some underlying "gender identity" causes both autogynephilia and, separately, the desire to transition, then why does it usually not work that way for androphiles? tailcalled answered it in the comments. If autogynephilia requires attraction to women, as the erotic-target theory itself says, then a gender identity that produced autogynephilia would produce it only in men attracted to women; its absence in androphiles is what both hypotheses predict. He added that the theory's proponents, when pressed on androphilic trans women with autogynephilic fantasies, usually end up arguing that it sometimes works this way for androphiles and females, but that it just doesn't count for autogynephilic males (tailcalled, 9 Jul 2022). Davis did not defend the argument. He granted that the proponents had not thought the question through, and his remark about them, I'm happy to concede that "Blanchardians" are Dumb and Bad if it helps us focus on what arguments are true (Davis, 20 Jul 2022), concedes the faction while keeping the conclusion. He did not withdraw the argument from androphiles either; he left the sentence in the post.

tailcalled's own case for the causal direction rests on what sexuality is: a motive that is relatively fixed and early, so that a sexual interest in being a woman would be expected to produce a wish to be one. He found that argument better than any the proponents offered, and he guessed, from his surveys and some rough simulation, that a properly identified model would find the causation running both ways, with most of the variance in both variables coming from elsewhere (tailcalled, 16 Jul 2022). He offered it as a guess, and it leaves the direction an open empirical question.

The objections from the other side come with rival models, which Davis's writing names but does not examine at length. Serano argues from the order of onset: most ‘nonclassical’ trans women either never experience FEFs, or experience FEFs only after they have experienced gender dysphoria, so the fantasies (her "female embodiment fantasies") cannot be the cause (Serano 2020, 768). The claim is a disjunction, and the frequencies in §3 do not contradict it. They show that most non-androphilic trans women in these samples report the fantasies at some point, so for "most" to hold, the second disjunct must cover most of them: the claim stands or falls on the order of onset. Serano's alternative is The most reasonable conclusion is that gender dysphoria develops independently in a small percentage of AMAB people, and a subset of those individuals (along with a subset of cisgender women and men) will subsequently develop FEFs for other reasons (Serano 2020, 768). Veale, Clarke and Lomax proposed a second: biological and early-childhood factors determine how strongly a gender-variant identity develops, and personality and environment determine whether it is repressed, with the outcome that If defence mechanisms are used, then the resultant outcome is either a non-classical transsexual or cross-dresser, depending on the degree of gender-variance. (Veale, Clarke and Lomax 2010, abstract). Davis called that model a stretch without saying why. Both rivals place the identity first and treat the eroticism as downstream, and both explain the correlations of §2 at least as easily as the theory does.

The order of onset is therefore the evidence that matters, and the evidence is poor on both sides. Lawrence's Table VI, which Davis singled out (If I only get one table to showcase the correlational data, I'll pick Table VI), gives the mean age at which participants first wished to change sex as 9.8 and 8.0 years in two groups attracted to women before surgery, against 6.3 in an androphilic group (Lawrence 2005, Table VI). The same paper does not report when those participants first experienced autogynephilic arousal, so the table cannot show which came first in anyone. Its standard deviations are large (9.1 years in the first group), and the paper's second grouping, by sexual partners rather than attraction, reverses the order of the groups' means (Table VII). The reports are also retrospective, and the theory's founders documented the bias they are subject to. Blanchard and colleagues found in 1985 that heterosexual gender patients who presented themselves in a socially desirable light scored further in the "classic transsexual" direction on every history measure, and quoted a clinician's observation that such patients distort their past histories to fit in to their transsexual identity and are therefore more likely to report early transsexual urges to support the idea that they are basically female (Blanchard, Clemmensen and Steiner 1985). That is the proponents' best reply to the order-of-onset argument. It cuts against using retrospective reports of an early wish as evidence for either side, and it applies equally to retrospective reports of the fantasy, including Davis's own (Chapter 2, §4).

Serano's second objection is that cross-sex fantasies are common in the general population while transition is rare, so the fantasy cannot be sufficient. tailcalled pointed out that this has the same form as one of Davis's arguments and the same flaw: in a threshold model, many people can have a contributing factor without crossing the threshold (tailcalled, 16 Jul 2022). Moser's version is harder to answer that way: sexual motives weaken with age, yet Yet, older, often autogynephilic, MTFs continue to pursue SRS. (Moser 2010, 805). The commonness of the fantasy is uncertain in any case. The figure Davis gave in 2019, ~5% (?) of men with intense AGP, links to a Swedish population study that found Almost three percent (2.8%) of men and 0.4% of women reported at least one episode of transvestic fetishism. (Långström and Zucker 2005, abstract). The two measures differ in opposite directions. Arousal from cross-dressing is narrower than autogynephilia, which would make the true rate higher than 2.8 percent; a single episode is a far lower threshold than intensity, which would make the intense rate lower. The source neither supports nor refutes Davis's figure, which its link does not support. An unpublished draft gives about 3% of men are estimated to have experienced autogynephilia to some extent, closer to the source. In 2021 Davis cited a college survey in which 5.6% of males and 13.2% of females had fantasized about being the opposite sex ("Point Man," UUT, 8 Mar 2021); the 5.6 percent was three of 54 men (Hsu et al. 1994).

The causal claim has not been established, and neither have its rivals. The evidence is correlational and retrospective, the timing data cannot decide between them, and the one argument Davis gave for the direction of the arrow does not discriminate between the hypotheses. His own most careful statement concedes much of this. In the 2024 podcast with Phil Illy he called it a pretty good guess that like my AGP was a necessary cause of what I've been calling the beautiful, pure, sacred self-identity thing in his own case, allowed that there could be multiple causes, and described his position as having changed: because I'm less dogmatic about the two-type typology these days, I don't even necessarily care that much about, we need to popularize the two-type taxonomy specifically. ("Transcript: 'Autogynephilia and Epistemic Despair,'" UUT, 15 Jul 2024). What the evidence supports is narrower than the claim. Autogynephilic arousal is reported by most non-androphilic trans women in the samples that have asked, far more often than in the one population survey of men, and it is plausibly among the causes of transition for many of them.

5. The negative claim

The claim Davis chose as his empirical thesis in January 2024 is a negative one, and it is ambiguous. In the medical sense, an intersex condition is a difference of sex development, and no one in this debate holds that trans women of either group have one; on that reading the claim is true and uninformative. The sense the T-shirt means is the account of transsexuality as a female brain in a male body, and the evidence on that account comes mostly from samples not separated by orientation. The postmortem studies of the hypothalamus on which the brain-sex account first rested were all done in one Dutch laboratory, on trans women who had been treated with hormones, of mixed and partly unknown sexual orientation (Guillamon, Junque and Gómez-Gil 2016). The usable form of Davis's claim is the one in the 2018 essay: the late-onset group's traits are drawn, along most dimensions, from the male distribution rather than the female one. Chapter 4 (§8) finds that the psychological strand of Davis's argument about the category woman needs exactly this premise.

The imaging evidence that separates the groups is very small. The 2016 review of adult gender dysphoria summarized the studies of untreated trans people this way: Overall, evidence supports the sexual differentiation hypothesis in homosexual MtFs, but not in nonhomosexual MtFs. For the non-homosexual group it rested on two structural studies that found gray-matter volumes in line with natal sex, one of them a study that a second review classes as mixed by orientation. The same review reports findings that point the other way: In nonhomosexual MtFs, a femalelike response in hypothalamic activation while smelling odorous steroids (Berglund et al. 2008) and brain activity while viewing erotic videos (Gizewski et al. 2009) were detected, arguing for sex-atypical reactions in these groups. It also reports greater cortical thickness than in control men, and white-matter differences from control men in a sample mixed by orientation (Zucker, Lawrence and Kreukels 2016, 232–233).1 Lawrence was a coauthor of the review; tailcalled asked in the LessWrong thread whether the manual's section on the subject had been written under a friend of Blanchard and Bailey (tailcalled, 9 Jul 2023).2 Antonio Guillamon and colleagues, reviewing the structural studies, put the state of knowledge plainly: All we know about the morphology of the brain of nonhomosexual MtFs comes from a single VBM study (Savic & Arver, 2011). That study found the brains not feminized but unusual in regions where men and women do not differ, regions associated with the perception of one's own body: In summary, the cortex of nonhomosexual MtFs presents morphological peculiarities in regions in which male and female controls do not differ. Guillamon's group judged that a prediction by Blanchard and James Cantor along these lines had held up so far (Cantor seems to be right.), and added that to fully confirm the hypothesis, more independent studies on nonhomosexual MtFs are needed (Guillamon, Junque and Gómez-Gil 2016). The structural evidence for (d), in both reviews, is substantially one study.

Later work does not separate the groups. A systematic review of 39 imaging studies of gender identity found that some features in transgender people resemble those of their experienced gender despite the majority resembling those from their natal sex (Frigerio, Ballerini and Valdés Hernández 2021). The largest pooled analysis, of brain scans from 803 people at several sites, among them 172 trans women not treated with hormones, concluded that Rather than being merely shifted towards either end of the male-female spectrum, transgender persons seem to present with their own unique brain phenotype. (Mueller et al. 2021, abstract). Neither separated trans women by sexual orientation, so neither bears on the distinction Davis cares about. Both are inconsistent with the simple version of the view he opposes, that trans women's brains are female brains, and the second is equally inconsistent with the view that there is nothing distinctive to find. tailcalled, summarizing the multivariate studies he knew, reported that before hormones trans women's overall brain features match those of males better than those of females (tailcalled, 9 Jul 2023), while warning that the studies were small and many comparisons were made. Overall classification closer to males is compatible with a localized difference that matters for a particular experience, and these studies cannot exclude one.

Brains are not psychology, and the premise Chapter 4 needs is psychological. The sources assembled here measure it only indirectly. Smith's Dutch study found large differences between the orientation groups in childhood cross-gender behavior and judged appearance and none in height. Davis's own contribution is a tally of the State of Haskell surveys, in which about 3 percent of respondents were women, cis and trans in roughly equal numbers ("Survey Data on Cis and Trans Women Among Haskell Programmers," UUT, 24 Nov 2020). A commenter, Spencer, corrected his handling of non-responses, which Davis fixed and credited in the post, and objected that observing that 51% of women are trans is not meaningful since trans people are 91% male (Spencer, 26 Nov 2020). On the post's own figures, trans women were between 1.4 and 1.8 percent of respondents each year, while the estimate of the transgender share of the American population that Davis cites, 0.58 percent, covers trans people of every kind. Trans women are therefore over-represented among these programmers relative to the male population they came from, not merely present at its rate.3 That trans women are not under-represented in a male-dominated field, as cis women are, is weak evidence against female-typical occupational interests, and it is what male-typical socialization and opportunity before transition would produce in any case. The over-representation calls for an explanation of its own, whether selection into an online survey or something about the population, and none of the theories at issue was built to supply one. The question whether non-androphilic trans women are male-typical in the conjunction of psychological traits has not been answered by anything in these sources.

In the form Davis gave it in 2018, the negative claim is not contradicted by the evidence that separates the groups, and that evidence is one structural study and two functional studies whose findings point in different directions. The qualification his 2018 sentence contained, or at most, a very mild one, has turned out to be needed, and it makes the claim hard to test, since Davis has not said what finding would count as more than mild. When adrusi, a late-onset trans woman without autogynephilic fantasies whose challenge Chapter 2 discusses, pressed him in 2023, he drew the line at a bone fide intersex condition that has no particular symptoms other than a mysterious desire to be female (Davis, 9 Aug 2023). No study has found such a condition, and none has been designed to look for it. The claim is better described as unrefuted than as established. It asks for less than the causal claim and is correspondingly more secure, and it is less secure than the correlations, which several samples across several decades confirm.

6. The population

The population claim combines three quantities: the share of trans women who are not exclusively attracted to men, the share of those with a history of autogynephilic arousal, and the share of those for whom it is the main reason for transition. Davis's 2018 statement of the first was that the majority of male-to-female trans people in Western countries do not fit this profile, the androphilic early-onset one. The clinical literature he cites does not establish it. Blanchard's Toronto sample was 61 percent homosexual, Nuttbrock's New York sample 68.5 percent, Smith's Dutch sample of natal males about 54 percent; in Lawrence's surgical sample 9 percent had been predominantly attracted to men. The proportion runs from under a tenth to over two-thirds, depending on place, period and recruitment.

A source outside Davis's collection is more informative about the present. The 2015 United States Transgender Survey, an online convenience sample of nearly 28,000 people, asked respondents which terms described their sexual orientation. Among trans women, 19 percent chose straight or heterosexual, which for a trans woman means attracted to men; 27 percent gay, lesbian or same-gender-loving; 20 percent bisexual; 16 percent pansexual; 6 percent queer; 6 percent asexual; and 6 percent another term (James et al. 2016, 59, Figure 4.28). The labels do not map cleanly onto Blanchard's categories, some who chose "queer" or "pansexual" may be mainly androphilic, and a convenience sample of this size is still a convenience sample. On these figures a mainly androphilic share much above a quarter would require many of the bisexual, pansexual and queer respondents to be mainly attracted to men. Online samples elsewhere look similar: in Veale's, by Lawrence's count, at most 18 of 308 were androphilic. For the online populations that most of the corpus's debates concern, Davis's first quantity is probably right.

The second quantity, from §3, is perhaps two-thirds to nine-tenths in older clinical samples. Multiplying ranges drawn from different populations with different measures gives only an illustration, and on that illustration something between a half and three-quarters of trans women would be non-androphilic with some history of the arousal, if the older samples' rates hold for the people in a survey of 2015. That conditional is what the weak reading depends on. Nuttbrock found that Older participants were roughly twice as likely to report all measurements of transvestic fetishism., and that younger participants were more often androphilic (Nuttbrock et al. 2011, 252), which points to a falling share. Their sample was a New York community sample with many non-White participants recruited partly from streets and clubs, while the national online survey, whose respondents were mostly young, shows a non-androphilic majority, so the two do not describe the same population. Davis's own models do not settle the direction.4 "Lesser-Known Demand Curves" argued that as transition becomes less costly, more of the mildly dysphoric will transition on the margin ("Lesser-Known Demand Curves," UUT, 18 Dec 2017), without saying who those marginal transitioners would be. If the costs of transition have fallen hardest on the late-onset group, which on the theory passes less easily and is more often married, cheaper transition would bring in proportionally more of them; the 2022 graph's arrow for cultural factors points the other way, toward transitions for which autogynephilia is not the main reason. Which effect dominates is an empirical question that nothing measured since the older samples answers. The qualifier under modern conditions marks the period those samples describe worst.

The third quantity is the causal claim, and §4 found it unestablished. The phrase guys like me adds more than cause. As Chapter 2 argued, it asserts a resemblance in experience that one detailed case cannot carry to a population. Davis's own essay contains the qualifications. It allows that the second type may be a residual category, androphilic/"not otherwise specified", and it gives internalized misandry in boys taught that male sexuality is predatory as a possible source of dysphoria in males who are not autogynephilic at all. It grants the individual exception without suspicion: If an individual trans woman swears that she doesn't match the feminine/early-onset type, but also doesn't empathize with the experiences I've grouped under "autogynephilia", I don't have any proof with which to accuse her of lying, and of a particular acquaintance, that she denied experiencing autogynephilia at all, and I believe her.

On (e), a weak reading has to be separated from a strong one. On the weak reading, that a majority of trans women in the contemporary United States are not exclusively attracted to men and that most of these have some history of autogynephilic arousal, the claim is plausible, with large uncertainty about whether older rates hold for younger cohorts. On the strong reading, that a substantial majority transition mainly for the reason Davis believes he would have, it goes beyond the evidence, because it rests on the causal claim and on a resemblance of inner life that surveys have not measured. The essay's headline states the strong reading, and its evidence and qualifications support the weak one.

7. Method and the record of correction

Davis's methodological contributions to this literature are small and mostly sound. "The Univariate Fallacy" (LW and AAL, 15 Jun 2019) shows with forty simulated variables that two distributions can overlap heavily on every variable separately and yet be almost perfectly separable taken together: And yet if we look at many variables, we can achieve supreme, godlike confidence about which distribution a point was sampled from. The mathematics is correct, and it answers a real argument, Daphna Joel and colleagues' conclusion from overlap and inconsistency across brain features that human brains do not belong to one of two distinct categories: male brain/female brain (Joel et al. 2015, abstract). Replies by Marco Del Giudice and colleagues and by another group made the same point in the same journal in 2016, three years before Davis's post, which does not mention them; his 2021 post "Sexual Dimorphism in Yudkowsky's Sequences, in Relation to My Gender Problems" credits Del Giudice's critique when it takes up Joel (Del Giudice et al. 2016; Chekroud et al. 2016; known here by title and citation). The demonstration assumes, as Davis stated, that the variables are statistically independent; real traits are correlated, which reduces how much each adds, and separability in the aggregate is a fact about classification that leaves open whether the groups are discrete kinds. Chapter 4 takes up the use Davis made of the result in his argument about categories.

The 2019 post on effect sizes argues that standardized measures will underestimate the "true" effect size when measurement error or an unrelated common factor inflates the variance ("Does General Intelligence Deflate Standardized Effect Sizes of Cognitive Sex Differences?," UUT, 1 Sep 2019). The point is elementary and correct, and it explains why, when "The Social Construction of Reality" cites Del Giudice's multivariate sex difference in personality, it gives the latent estimate of about 2.7, the figure the paper headlines, and not the one the same paper reports for observed scores: The uncorrected multivariate effect size for observed scores was D = 1.49 (Del Giudice, Booth and Irwing 2012). The choice follows from Davis's argument, though the parenthesis that makes it does not tell the reader a second figure exists.5 The Haskell post was corrected on a commenter's objection and says so. The measurement argument against Alexander is the most consequential of these contributions, and its fault is the uneven application described in §3.

Across the decade, the record of correction is consistent in what it concedes and what it keeps. Davis conceded the inference from correlation to taxon (2021), the claim about lesbians' phrasing (2023), the dogmatism of his early defense (2024: The reason I was so insistent that they were right about everything was because no one else had any credible alternative.), and, in a draft he may not have sent, the methodological case against his own side's recent studies (2025). Of the argument from androphiles he conceded the faction, not the argument. In the Illy conversation he was asked about tailcalled's disillusionment with the proponents' rigor and answered, I almost want to plead guilty to that in some sense, because his interest had never been the sexological details. What he kept is the negative claim, the frequency claim, and the conviction that the causal story is right. He stated a minimum ask: that autogynephilia be acknowledged as a real thing that is clearly a causal factor in many gender problems, and that Biological Sex Actually Exists and Is Sometimes Relevant, Even When It Makes People Sad. The first half is somewhat stronger than the literature warrants, and the second is not in serious dispute.

8. Did the polemics follow?

The register of the gender blog is often polemical, and the question is whether the polemics tracked the concessions. The early polemics cannot have, since they predate them, but they set the baseline. A post of January 2017, a single line under the tag "cathartic," announced that there are actually three types of male-to-female transsexualism: effeminate homosexuality, autogynephilia, and—by far the most common—the third type that we made up in order to keep our jobs ("Revised Taxonomy," UUT, 28 Jan 2017). "The Social Construction of Reality," written in 2019 in a register the corpus labels as epistemic horror, explains the politics of the transgender movement by sex differences in coalitional aggression: Autogynephilic males are better at large-scale coalitional politics than actual lesbians for the reasons men in general are ("The Social Construction of Reality and the Sheer Goddamned Pointlessness of Reason," UUT, 11 Aug 2019). The claim depends on (e) in its strong form and on a theory of political outcomes nowhere argued. The same habit of confident reading of opponents shows in a test Davis set himself and published. Predicting which of eighteen entries in Ozy Brennan's ideological Turing test were written by the theory's adherents and which by its opponents, he got 11 right and a logarithmic score of −18.012 (just worse than chance), because several of his most confident calls were wrong ("Thing of Things Transgender Intellectual Turing Test Predictions and Commentary," UUT, 29 Mar 2017).

After the concessions the picture is mixed. "Point Man," published five weeks after the reply to tailcalled, introduces its account as Here's my theory. It tells the history of transsexualism as two groups, the extreme tail of feminine homosexuals for whom medicine worked well and, Separately, there are, and perhaps always have been, paraphilic men who wished they were women—autogynephiles, for whom it worked less well, a claim about outcomes supported by a single link to a blog post about passing, and it explains the growth of the trans population as a coalition recruiting a lot of young men with autogynephilia or internalized misandry. The post labels the account as Davis's own, which is consistent with a conclusion held on parsimony intuitions after its argument was withdrawn. It does not tell the reader that this is the status of the conclusion, and in the terms of this study's thesis that is a failure of the norm of accounting in emphasis. The short posts of 2023 carry the 2022 qualification on their face: one describes the theory as the story that makes so much more sense than "gender identity" as a first approximation, even if the underlying reality is going to be more complicated than that ("Janet Mock on Late Transitioners," UUT, 11 Jun 2023), and the endorsement of Illy's book addresses readers approximately sold on the big picture ("Book Endorsement: Phil Illy's Autoheterosexual," UUT, 21 Jun 2023).

"Blanchard's Dangerous Idea," a year after "Useful Approximation," asks whether the two-type taxonomy is approximately true and answers (answer: yes, obviously), then, after a series of what it calls laughable denials of autogynephilia, asks How dumb do you (proponents of gender-identity theories) think we (the general public) are? and answers that one too. The first answer is defensible as worded, since "approximately" carries the 2022 qualifications. The second is a taunt about implausible public messaging, and it stops short of a charge of knowing pretense: of the clinicians who treated him, the same essay says that they're not—lying, and calls their framework transparently garbage-tier epistemology instead. The charge is of distortion without conscious deceit, the kind Davis's functional account of deception was built to describe (Chapter 5). Whether the denials are as laughable as he says depends on the questions about testimony that Chapter 2 examined.

A critic could describe the result less kindly, and the description has to be faced.6 The narrow statement Davis gave when asked for his object-level claim, that late-onset gender dysphoria in males is not an intersex condition, or at most, a very mild one, is the easiest of the five to defend because it says the least. The polemical claims, that the taxonomy is approximately true and that most trans women are guys like him, say much more. A position defended at its narrowest when challenged and advanced at its broadest when not is the pattern tailcalled named in 2021, and Davis conceded that charge then. The record does not fit the pattern exactly. The main polemical essay carries its own qualifications (the residual category, the honest exception, "I believe her"), the concessions were made in public, the 2022 model is a revision with content, and the narrow statement of 2024 was given on request in a thread about something else, without the strong claims being withdrawn. The effect on a reader is nonetheless much the same. Someone who meets the headline sentence and the taunt is not told that the author's considered position is narrower, and when the considered position narrowed, Davis did not go back and narrow the essays. The norm of accounting asks a writer to say what a conclusion rests on. The gender polemics met it in their qualifications and failed it in their emphasis.

Davis's attitude to the controversy over Bailey's The Man Who Would Be Queen (2003) was partisan from the start: a 2016 email to Alexander, as he reports it, carried the subject line J. Michael Bailey did nothing wrong. Alice Dreger's history of the controversy describes how a small number of transwomen (particularly Lynn Conway, Andrea James, and Deirdre McCloskey) worked to try to ruin Bailey, and concludes that the charges of research misconduct were not substantiated (Dreger 2008); her account was contested in commentaries published with it and in the public accounts of the people she names, none of which was read for this study, and her conclusions are reported here as hers. His 2017 commentary on the Turing test held that the critics who disparage Queen as "unscientific" are missing the point because a popular book need not marshal all the evidence. The Murray review of 2020 judged Murray's presentation of the theory as fact, which I think is basically correct, while faulting him for not mentioning the critics ("Book Review: Charles Murray's Human Diversity," UUT, 28 Apr 2020). There Davis held a popularizer to the standard of saying why the alternatives fail, which his own polemics met least consistently.

This study's thesis, that Davis's position consists of a criterion of honesty, a norm of accounting and an argument that the norm admits no exceptions, applies to this chapter only at its edges. The verdicts on (a) through (e) are empirical and owe nothing to it. It applies to how Davis handles evidence. The concession to tailcalled, the disclosed prior in the Alexander reply, and the 2025 draft's demand for studies that speak for themselves are the norm of accounting turned on his own side's arguments, and they are the best of the sexological writing. Disclosure makes reasoning assessable without making it right, and the prior Davis disclosed is still a prior. tailcalled's complaint that the proponents' theories are theories of the etiology of transsexuality. But they also basically universally focus on the latter kinds of questions, the social questions about passing and women's spaces, fits the corpus (tailcalled, 9 Jul 2022), and Davis came close to granting it when he told Illy that he had written so much out of a personal need, not curiosity about the sexological details.

9. Conclusion

Blanchard's idea, as Davis defended it, is partly right. The correlations among orientation, childhood femininity, age at transition and erotic cross-dressing hold in every sample, and the critics concede them; whether they mark two kinds is open. The arousal is reported by most non-androphilic trans women in every sample that has asked. The causal claim and its rivals are all unestablished, and the order of onset that would decide between them rests on retrospective reports that both sides have reason to distrust. The claim Davis put forward in 2024 as his empirical thesis is the narrowest of the five and is unrefuted, mainly because so little evidence bears on it. The claim he pressed hardest in polemic, that most trans women are men like him, is the least supported, because it rests on the causal story and on a resemblance of inner life that no survey has measured.

Davis's considered position by 2024, less dogmatic about the typology and insistent on a few propositions about sex and about autogynephilia as a cause, is close to what a reader of the literature, its critics included, could defend, and the concessions that brought it there were made in public, several of them in reply to tailcalled. His polemics carried the qualifications in their text and the earlier confidence in their emphasis, and that gap explains some of the hostility the theory met in his hands, though the reception gives other reasons too (Chapter 2). Chapter 4 asks which parts of his philosophy of categories need these claims at all. It finds that only the psychological strand of the argument about woman does, and that strand needs the premise about male-typical traits that this chapter could not settle.
