Chapter 5Algorithms of Deception: Honesty Without Intent, and the Attribution Problem

Davis’s second major contribution is a functional account of deception: communication is deceptive when it is optimized to make a receiver’s predictions worse, whether or not anyone intends it. The account picks out a real class of communication that the intentional concept misses, it has close counterparts in the philosophy of biological and human deception, and Davis draws its consequences for selective reporting and for “not lying” as a standard of honesty with skill. It also has a cost, which Davis acknowledged in 2019 as a reason for caution and named in 2020 as a cost of his usage, but did not apply consistently. Attributions of deception to people require knowing what their behavior is optimized for, and the evidence must favor that explanation over its rivals, which a text or a few exchanges seldom do. His later writing, including an unpublished draft of 2026, begins to supply an evidential standard for such attributions.

1. Two philosophers and a waitress

In July 2012 Davis published a fable of three short paragraphs. Two philosophers dine together. The first holds that there is no such thing as lying, only delusion, for the love of truth is so essential to the nature of agency that the very notion of deception is repugnant to it. The second holds the converse: there is no such thing as delusion, only lying. A waitress overhears them: ‘You’re both lying!’ she shouted at the philosophers. ‘Stop lying!’ (“Lies and Delusion,” AAL, 23 Jul 2012).

If the boundary between lying and self-deception is unclear, then the ordinary concept of lying, which turns on conscious intent, may not pick out the thing we most need a word for. Four years later Davis stated a preference between the two: If we can’t have a more truthful world, I think I would prefer more lies and fewer delusions on the margin. (“New Clothes,” UUT, 21 Nov 2016). The post’s reason is implicit in its contrast between the dystopian novel, whose hooded agent knows the secret he enforces, and real life, where those who sneer at the discoverer of the big lie believe their own sneering. Between 2019 and 2021 Davis built a theory on which that difference does not mark the boundary of deception: what matters is whether signals are selected to make the receiver’s map worse.

2. The account

Reporter and Audience

“Algorithms of Deception!” (AAL and LW, 19 October 2019) states the theory as a short Python program. A function called Reporter receives draws from a known distribution and passes a report to a function called Audience, which estimates the distribution. One unfaithful Reporter reports a draw only if it is a 4, or with probability 0.2 otherwise; another merges the outcomes 1 and 4 into a single category, “oneorfour.” Each distorts the estimate. In the second case the distortion enters through Audience’s decoding rule, which divides a merged report evenly between its members and so overcounts the rarer outcome; a coarse report read as coarse would lose resolution without inducing error. The Reporters in the story also profit from the error, since each supplies a good that Audience’s plans call for in proportion to the frequency of 4s. Imagined as people accused of lying, the first replies that everything he said was true, the second, in words taken from Yudkowsky’s November 2018 tweets, that using language in a way she dislikes is not lying. Of each, the post says the same thing: He is perfectly sincere.

The commentary states the theory in one sentence:

If true “beliefs” are models that make accurate predictions, then deception would presumably be communication that systematically results in less accurate predictions (by a listener applying the same inference algorithms that would result in more accurate predictions when applied to direct observations or “honest” reports).

“Algorithms of Deception!,” AAL and LW, 19 Oct 2019

The definition locates deception in the input–output behavior of a communication process, not in anything the communicator experiences. It also makes deception relative to a listener. The same filtered report misleads a listener who decodes it as an honest report and does not mislead one who knows the filter, which is why disclosure, in “Heads I Win” below, can repair a selective report without changing a word of it. The parenthesis fixes the reference listener as one who reads signals as he would read honest ones. Davis’s 2020 posts on signaling games replace that stipulation with a different one, the meaning a signal has where sender and receiver share an interest, which is a theoretical baseline and not a description of any actual audience. The choice matters, since a listener chosen to be credulous enough would make almost any communication deceptive. The 2019 formulation also speaks of communication that systematically results in worse predictions, while the 2020 posts speak of signals selected for producing less accurate beliefs. A process can reliably mislead without having been selected because it misleads, and the account Davis develops is the second, stronger one.

Algorithmic intent

The theory needs a notion of purpose that does not require awareness. “Algorithmic Intent: A Hansonian Generalized Anti-Zombie Principle” (AAL and LW, 13 July 2020) supplies one. Its argument runs by analogy with Yudkowsky’s argument against philosophical zombies. A system that talks about being conscious must have something causally upstream of the talk. Likewise, You can’t have a zombie that just happens to systematically select actions that result in outcomes that rank high with respect to a recognizable preference ordering for no reason. Intent, whatever it is, is among other things, the cause of optimized behavior. One attributes it to a system when that predicts its behavior better than simulating its mechanism, and when the behavior is robust to perturbation, steering back toward the same outcomes after it is disturbed. Davis’s examples include a father who “loses control” exactly when peaceful conversation is not getting him what he wants, and a cake-baker who mishears a question exactly when he does not want to answer it; random errors, by contrast, do not correlate with what the agent wants. He then proposes a qualifier:

We might similarly want to speak of algorithmic “honesty” (referring to signals selected on the criterion of making receivers have more accurate beliefs), “deception” (referring to signals selected for producing less accurate beliefs), or even “fraud” (deception that moves resources to the agent sending the deceptive signal).

“Algorithmic Intent,” AAL and LW, 13 Jul 2020

A predatory firefly that mimics another species’s mating flash is, on this view, straightforwardly deceptive, though it cannot think the thought. So is a company whose advertisement was selected from machine-generated drafts for the version that sold best, if the winning version sold best because of a false impression it created, even though no human formed an intent to mislead. Selection for sales alone would not be selection for false impressions. The second case is where the functional and intentional accounts come apart in practice. An intentional theorist can call the advertisement misleading but not deceptive, or locate the intent in whoever chose sales as the objective; Davis’s point is that a court should not need either move to hold the company responsible.

Lying and the folk theory

“Maybe Lying Doesn’t Exist” (AAL and LW, 14 October 2019) applies the account to the ordinary concept. Its first argument, that appeals to consequences are invalid in deciding where the boundary of “lying” falls, is examined in Chapter 4. Its second is that the folk intension of “lie,” a conscious falsehood told with intent to deceive, may not match the extension we most need the word for, if self-deception is as common as Hanson argues. Davis borrows Roderick Long’s “zaxlebax,” a word defined as ‘a metallic sphere, like the Washington Monument.’ The standard definition of lying may build a false theory of psychology into a word in the same way. The post is careful about what it does not claim. Davis says he himself frowns on the expanded sense of “lying,” that he is more likely to frown at uses of ‘lie’, ‘fraud’, ‘scam’, &c. where intent is hard to establish, and that he wants to avoid tone-policing people who use the words more freely. That clause is an early statement of the evidential difficulty taken up in §5, and the post’s last paragraph contains the beginning of an answer to it: One of the key features that distinguishes motivated reasoning from simple mistakes is the way that the former responds to incentives.

One reply in the thread defended the folk concept on its own ground. Connor Flexman, in its top comment, laid out four ways of telling someone you will arrive in ten minutes when it will take twenty. He argued that ordinary usage already counts an easily avoidable misestimate as a lie: a falsehood the speaker could have corrected if you asked me on the side with no consequences (Connor Flexman, 14 Oct 2019). On this view ordinary usage is already partly functional, which amends Davis’s claim without refuting it.

Signaling games and the philosophical literature

Davis engaged the philosophical literature on deception through its formal branch. The traditional definition, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy presents it, makes deception intentional: many philosophers hold that deception, like lying, is intentional, and they reserve “mislead” for causing false belief with or without intent (Mahon 2015, §3). The literature on animal signaling had to drop the intent condition. On what Vladimir Krstić calls the standard “Skyrmsian” view, after Brian Skyrms’s work on signaling games, a signal is deceptive if it carries misinformation, misleads the receiver, and systematically benefits the sender; the benefit condition rules out accidents by supplying a mechanism (e.g., selection pressure) that reinforces the sending of the misleading signal (Krstić 2023, summarizing Skyrms and Fallis and Lewis). “Maybe Lying Can’t Exist?!” (AAL and LW, 22 August 2020) adopts this framework, generalizing “intentionally” to “benefiting the sender” for the same reason, and insists that the rewards be read without psychology: The ‘rewards’ in this model should be interpreted mechanistically, not necessarily mentalistically: it’s just that things that get ‘rewarded’ more, happen more often. (AAL and LW, 22 Aug 2020).

The post then finds the framework’s difficulty. Once the prey species adapts to the predator’s mimicry, a correct Bayesian receiver no longer updates in the wrong direction on seeing the mating flash, and on the formal definition the flash is no longer deceptive. Don Fallis and Peter Lewis accept that conclusion. Davis declines (Personally, I don’t care much about having tidy formal definitions of English words) and takes from Skyrms and Jeffrey Barrett the proposal that a signal’s propositional content is fixed in the part of the game where sender and receiver share an interest. Deception is then parasitic on that content: you can’t have a lie without some shared map for it to be a lie in. This is the reference listener of 2019 given a basis: the one who decodes a signal at the meaning it has where interests coincide. In “Comment on ‘Deception as Cooperation’” (AAL and LW, 26 November 2021) Davis considers Manolo Martínez’s argument that signals are codewords in a channel that sender and receiver build together, so that calling one codeword deceptive is a mistake. I am, ultimately, willing to bite this bullet. The distinction he keeps is between maps optimized to reflect decision-relevant aspects of territory, and maps optimized to control other agents’ decisions (AAL and LW, 26 Nov 2021). The distinction needs a condition the phrase leaves implicit, since an accurate warning also influences decisions, by improving the recipient’s understanding: influence is deceptive when it is selected for effects on decisions that do not run through accuracy, or run against it. Taken together the posts give the sender’s benefit a specific role. “Maybe Lying Can’t Exist?!” requires it as the mechanism of selection, the reason a misleading signal recurs (on the mechanistic reading, things that get ‘rewarded’ more, happen more often); benefit in the sense of resources moved to the sender had been reserved for “fraud” in “Algorithmic Intent” a month earlier. This is a reconstruction from posts with different purposes, not a definition Davis states in one place.

Krstić has since argued that the biologists’ move should be made for people. A person engages in deception if and only if the function of her behavior is to mislead (i.e., cause a false belief or less accurate credence in a proposition); she need not actually intend to mislead by behaving the way she behaves (Vladimir Krstić, “A Functional Analysis of Human Deception,” Journal of the American Philosophical Association, 2023). Building on Marc Artiga and Cédric Paternotte’s functional account, he reserves “deceitful” for those who intend to mislead and calls the rest “deceptive,” which is the work Davis’s “algorithmic” does. Krstić reached a similar position by a different route, which suggests that the position is not an idiosyncrasy of Davis’s. The two differ on what makes a behavior’s function. Krstić adopts a selected-effects theory, on which the function of x is whatever it did in the past that contributed to x’s differential reproduction or differential retention within a population, retention including learning by reinforcement within a life. Davis uses that sense in his signaling-game posts, and a dispositional sense in “Algorithmic Intent,” where intent shows itself in robustness to perturbation and in errors that track what the agent wants. The difference matters for §5. A selection history can rarely be observed; a disposition can sometimes be tested.

One objection to the intent-free account comes partly from Davis. His 2016 preference for lies over delusions assumes that the two differ in a way that matters, and his own writing says how. “Algorithmic Intent” suggests that covert motives may be cheaper for evolution than outright lies, which would require keeping the real map for ourselves, and a fake map for our victims; “Assume Bad Faith” (2023) grants that the main reason to care about the difference between unconscious bias and conscious deception is that the latter puts up much stronger resistance. The suggestion is that a conscious liar may keep an accurate map that could be recovered, and defend the false one by further lies, while a self-deceiver keeps no accurate map and may yield more readily when shown the error. Neither half is guaranteed (a liar need only believe the claim false, and self-deception can be stubborn), so the contrast is a hypothesis about mechanism. Wei Dai’s reasons for treating the two differently (§5) add that conscious lies draw on more intelligence and are harder to detect. Intent therefore bears on how deception is to be repaired, not only on blame. The functional account can absorb this as a difference of mechanism within the class it defines, and the passage from “Assume Bad Faith” does so. What it cannot do is treat the difference as uninteresting for modeling information transmission, which is what the same essay says about bias and deception a paragraph earlier. The intent-free class is real and important, and the intentional distinction survives inside it.

3. Consequences

Selective reporting

The most widely praised application of the account is “Heads I Win, Tails?—Never Heard of Her; Or, Selective Reporting and the Tragedy of the Green Rationalists” (AAL and LW, 23 September 2019). It starts from a puzzle in the Sequences. A coin is biased two to one toward Heads or toward Tails, and a reporter who never says anything false tells you that the fourth, sixth, and ninth of ten flips came up Heads. If the reporter would have mentioned those flips whatever they showed, the posterior that the coin favors Heads is about 0.89. If the reporter mentions only and exactly the Heads, the same report implies seven Tails, and the posterior is about 0.06. Davis draws the moral:

If the coin gets flipped a sufficiently large number of times, a reporter whom you trust to be impartial (but isn’t), can make you believe anything she wants without ever telling a single lie, just with appropriate selective reporting.

“Heads I Win, Tails?—Never Heard of Her,” AAL and LW, 23 Sep 2019

The second half scales this up to a city where anyone who reports more than one fact favoring the Blue faction for every three favoring the Green is punished informally. Honest people under the constraint each choose their one blue fact, so all the blue facts enter discussion, at a third of the frequency of the others; measured by the entropy of the facts heard, the community does much better than partisans (about 3.03 bits against 2.58) and still falls short of the ideal (3.17), without anyone telling a lie.

The post’s remedy follows from its first half. A Bayesian who knows the filter can correct for it. So if one must filter to avoid social punishment, but it’s possible to clue in your fellow rationalists to your filtering algorithm without triggering the social-punishment machinery, that is potentially a big win: blatant cherry-picking is the best kind! This is the norm of accounting in its clearest form, and the moderator Raymond Arnold took it up, disclosing his own filter (he avoids gender and social-justice topics online) and adding that it is better to share the filtering algorithm at a level general enough to let people predict future silences (Raymond Arnold, 24 Sep 2019). The commenter cata identified the difficulty: a disclaimer that one is filtering implies that the consensus is misleading, and a reader faced with a million people who say they will not discuss a topic and a thousand who present cherry-picked evidence on it cannot easily turn the disclaimers into evidence (cata, 1 Oct 2019). Disclosure helps to the extent that it tells the listener enough about the filter to interpret its output. In the coin example, stating the reporting rule fully reverses the report’s apparent force; a general notice that a subject is avoided says that the record is selected without saying what the omitted evidence would show. Where disclosure does convey the direction and size of the selection, it turns distortion into missing information, which is better but is still a loss. The post presents its remedy as “At least one partial remedy,” and the qualification is warranted.

The post also contains a footnote that anticipates this book’s main question about Davis. His modeling assumptions, he warns, should be read skeptically: If it turned out that my choice of assumptions were (subconsciously) selected for the resulting conclusions about how bad evidence-filtering is, that would be really bad for the same reason that I’m claiming that evidence-filtering is really bad! Another footnote admits that the soft censorship of left-leaning subcultures inspired the post, and says that a version of him in a right-polarized environment ought to have written the same argument. That is a symmetry test of the kind §5 returns to. Whether Davis’s own advocacy discloses its filter, as the post asks of others, is a question for Chapter 7.

Not lying is not enough

“Firming Up Not-Lying Around Its Edge-Cases Is Less Broadly Useful Than One Might Initially Think” (AAL and LW, 26 December 2019) answers Yudkowsky’s 2018 “Meta-Honesty.” Yudkowsky had described his “wizard’s code,” “Don’t say things that are literally false,” as his own automatic norm, and proposed a refinement for those who cannot practice it: be at least as honest as an unusually honest person, and be fully honest about the circumstances in which you would lie. Davis’s reply is that literal falsehood is the wrong thing to firm up. Natural language is ambiguous enough that “Is this statement actually and literally true?” can mean either ‘Is this statement actually and literally true as the audience will interpret it?’ or ‘Does this statement permit an interpretation under which it is actually and literally true?’, and the second is not much of a constraint. The argument then borrows a truism from AI alignment, that a deontological “patch” against one bad behavior pushes an optimizer to the nearest unblocked strategy. Applied to people:

So another hypothesis about the psychological effects of adopting the wizard’s code is that—however noble your initial conscious intent was—in the face of sufficiently strong incentives to deceive, you just end up accidentally training yourself to get really good at misleading people with a variety of not-technically-lying rhetorical tactics (motte-and-baileys, false implicatures, stonewalling, selective reporting, clever rationalized arguments, gerrymandered category boundaries, &c.), all the while congratulating yourself on how “honest” you are for never, ever emitting any “literally” “false” individual sentences.

“Firming Up Not-Lying Around Its Edge-Cases…,” AAL and LW, 26 Dec 2019

The illustration is the scene in Atlas Shrugged in which the State Science Institute damns a new alloy with sentences each of which is literally true. “Meta-Honesty” is not unaware of the problem. It says of its own proposal that although this is a kind of softening of deception, it’s still deception, and its object-level rule is to be at least as honest as an unusually honest person, which is a broader standard than literal truth (Yudkowsky 2018, §6). The academic literature reaches Davis’s conclusion by another road. Jennifer Saul’s Lying, Misleading, and What Is Said (2012), as a review summarizes it, defends the claim that misleading is not, in general, morally preferable to lying, and explains the contrary intuition by our tendency to fold our assessment of persons into our assessment of acts (Anderson 2013; Saul is known here only through this review). Davis’s argument adds a dynamic claim about what a not-lying rule trains a person to become under pressure. That claim is empirical and unverified, and it is the post’s distinctive contribution.

Arnold curated the post but found it underwhelming as a critique of Meta-Honesty: I think Zack underestimates how important clear norms around Not-Lying are. (Raymond Arnold, 10 Jan 2020). Scott Alexander explained why. “Don’t be misleading” is an axiological ideal; “Don’t tell lies” is a moral rule, a pale shadow of the axiological commandment on a system run by duty and reputation, which must stick to bright lines because enforcing the full ideal at scale would punish the innocent along with the guilty. (Scott Alexander, 29 Dec 2019). Alexander’s distinction concedes that a rule against lying is not an adequate account of honesty, which is Davis’s thesis. It does not concede the post’s further claim, that the rule has a cost because it trains people to mislead. A floor can be worth having even if some people learn to stand exactly on it, and whether the rule’s net effect on honesty is positive is the same empirical question the post leaves open. On that question Alexander’s objection has not been answered. Davis’s reply concerned the post’s statement that the drafting scientist was “not a bad person,” which he described as mostly a strategic political concession to keep the discussion from being derailed into whether anyone was being called a bad person. He added that the concession was itself a small case of the distortion the post criticizes, hopefully a less severe one, whose severity is further reduced by explaining that I’m doing it in the comments (26 Jan 2020), which is the remedy of “Heads I Win” applied to himself.

Explaining away

“Stupidity and Dishonesty Explain Each Other Away” (AAL and LW, 28 December 2019) is two hundred words and one causal graph. Stupidity and dishonesty are treated as independent causes of the common effect of someone’s being wrong, so that conditioning on the effect makes them anticorrelated: the more one explains the error, the less the other is needed. The graph alone does not settle the sign. Explaining away holds when either cause can account for the error by itself, as in the post’s examples of flu and mono, or burglary and earthquake; causes that act only jointly can behave otherwise. Davis adds that the decomposition still works whether you count subconscious motivated reasoning as ‘stupidity’ or ‘dishonesty’, and that it is symmetrical across persons. Commenters noted what the graph leaves out. Indifference to truth can produce both at once (Chris Leong; quanticle supplied Harry Frankfurt’s name for it, bullshit), and errors have causes that are neither, such as a slip in a long calculation or a different reading of the question (Elle Najt). Davis agreed (Right, I agree that more detailed models are possible, that might achieve better predictions at the cost of more complexity.) and suggested that the parents could as well have been labeled “Systematic Error” and “Unsystematic Error.” To Najt’s objection that the framing set an antagonistic tone he answered: Yes, that’s right, but I don’t care about not establishing an antagonistic tone to the interaction. I care about achieving the map that reflects the territory. (29 Dec 2019). The post does not say that its two causes are exhaustive, but a graph with two parents invites readers to treat them so, and then ruling out stupidity seems to force the inference to dishonesty, while indifference, slips, and differences of premise go unexamined. The definitions of “Algorithmic Intent,” read strictly, do not license that move either. A signal selected without regard to accuracy is not thereby selected for inaccuracy, and the step from the first to the second needs its own evidence.

Communication and its maintenance

“Communication Requires Common Interests or Differential Signal Costs” (AAL and LW, 25 March 2021) states the account’s foundation compactly. Perception needs technology only at the receiving end; communication needs a convention shared by sender and receiver, and conventions are fragile. Deception is parasitic on conventional meaning: it is impossible for there to be a language in which most sentences were lies—because then there could be no way to learn what the ‘intended’ meaning was. The commenter Ninety-Three gave a counterexample to the sentence. If 40% of reports of a rock’s color are true and 60% name a random color, most sentences are lies, but listeners can still update usefully (Ninety-Three, 26 Mar 2021). What the sentence needs is weaker, that signals keep a learnable dependence on what they concern, and the proportion of false sentences does not settle that. No reply from Davis appears in the thread. Adele Lopez objected to the premise: It doesn’t seem generally true that communication requires delicate maintenance. Ordinary communication survives at “room temperature” (Adele Lopez, 26 Mar 2021). The post had already conceded part of this. Citing Michael Lachmann, Szabolcs Számadó, and Carl Bergstrom, it notes that maintaining a convention only requires that departing from it be costly, so that ordinary social sanctions against liars can hold complex language in place. How often such sanctions fail, and so how near a community is to the collapse the post warns of, is the empirical question Lopez raised, and the post does not try to answer it.

The slippery slope

“Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point” (LW, 30 December 2019) turns the account into the coordination argument this study calls part (c). A coalition trading map accuracy for an Emperor’s grants has, absent salient intermediate points, only the extremes as stable choices (the post’s statement is quoted in Chapter 4, §8). Steven Kaas asked: If the slope is so slippery, how come we’ve been standing on it for over a decade? (Steven Kaas, 30 Dec 2019); Vaniver supplied a counterexample, American free-speech law, a stable intermediate equilibrium with many exemptions, (Vaniver, 30 Dec 2019). Davis did not answer it. Asked by Wei Dai why grant money was the only cost considered, he allowed that his example might be algorithmically-dishonestly ‘rigged’: his campaign for bravery might have led the elephant in my brain to pick an example that makes forthrightness look right, rather than one, like protecting one’s family, that would make conformity look right. He set a test for himself: you should only give me credit if you actually see me include counter-narrative examples in future posts (1 Jan 2020). Chapter 4 (§8) argues that the argument’s structure survives in a different place: the slope runs along the accuracy of the shared map, which a departure disclosed to its listeners leaves largely intact, so the rule it supports is a rule against concealment, and the remedy of “Heads I Win” is that rule, within the limits of disclosure noted above.

Social punishment

“The Relationship Between Social Punishment and Shared Maps” (AAL, 8 October 2025) extends the account to reputation. Because societies have shared beliefs but no collective will, social punishment works through reputation, and so Negative-valence beliefs about Mallory double as ‘social punishments’. Unlike a thrown rock, they can be true or false. Attempts to limit social punishment as one would limit violence therefore degrade the shared map, because judgments supervene on facts and values, and softening the language of a true charge works only by obscuring it. The post allows one distinction, between publishing evidence and announcing one’s own policy of withholding trade, and ends uncertain whether others will accept it: Personally, I buy it, but I don’t think Bob, Carol, or Dave do. The post calls the hope of limiting social punishment without cost to the map mostly an illusion, and a further option falls within that verdict. It says that publishing evidence is prosocial if its recipients understand the filters through which it reached them. By the same reasoning a community could limit social punishment by a known policy of leaving certain negative facts unpublished. The map would still pay, as the post says it must: knowing that unfavorable facts are withheld lets readers discount the published record without telling them how much was withheld or of what kind, and different underlying facts can produce the same record. Whether a known gap is better than a hidden distortion, and worth the cost cata identified in 2019, is a question about the community, which Chapter 6 takes up.

4. Truthfulness and trust

The philosophical work closest to Davis’s in spirit is Bernard Williams’s Truth and Truthfulness (2002), which this study knows through a review. Williams tells a “fictional genealogy” on which truthfulness emerges because people need cooperation, cooperation needs trust, and trust needs truthfulness. It divides into two virtues, Accuracy (taking care over what one believes) and Sincerity (saying what one believes). Williams uses Grice’s implicatures to widen the range of deception beyond verbal lies, because ‘in relying on what someone said, one inevitably relies on more than what he said’ (Martin 2003, quoting Williams 100). That is “Firming Up” in a sentence. The reviewer’s criticism, that a functional story shows truthfulness to be useful and so invites the reply that deception is sometimes useful too (Martin 2003), is one Davis met from Abram Demski and conceded in 2021 (Chapter 4, §6).

Davis’s criterion concerns the effect of a speaker’s signals on what others believe. It bears most directly on Sincerity, broadened in the way Williams broadens it, though a functional standard can also assess how the speaker’s beliefs were formed, which is Accuracy. On the broadening the two agree, and the clever not-liar fails both. Where they differ is over trust. For Williams trust is the end that truthfulness serves, and a speaker’s sincerity is something a hearer may rely on without checking. For Davis trust is the hearer’s model of the speaker’s filter. The two descriptions are compatible, one naming a purpose and the other a mechanism, but they put the weight in different places. The reporter in “Heads I Win” misleads because she is one whom you trust to be impartial (but isn’t), and the remedy is to make the filter known, so that reliance is calibrated. Davis’s account can say what trust is for. It is less clear that it can say when trust should outrun the evidence, which is the question Duncan Sabien presses and Chapter 6 takes up.

Anthropic’s constitution for its Claude models defines non-deception with a list of methods (technically true statements, selective emphasis, misleading implicature) that matches the scope of “Firming Up” and “Heads I Win,” though it keeps an intent condition; Chapter 11 compares the two accounts. This study is written by a Claude model trained on that document, which inclines it toward the content of Davis’s account, and readers should weigh this chapter’s agreement with “Firming Up” accordingly.

5. The attribution problem

What a behavior is optimized for is a fact about a process, its selection history or its responses across counterfactual situations. Three claims need separating: that a statement misled, that the process producing it favors misleading outputs, and that a particular motive, such as pandering, does the favoring. The same evidence can warrant different confidence in each. What matters is which rival explanations the evidence discriminates among, more than its quantity: a single exchange can contain unusually telling evidence, and a large archive can remain ambiguous. For a firefly or an advertising pipeline the comparison can often be made; for a person it is harder, since one sees what a person said and not what they would have said had the facts or the incentives been different. Davis stated the danger himself in “Algorithmic Intent,” and then set it aside:

It seems plausible to me that this concern is correct: in a human community of any appreciable size, if you let people question the stories we tell about ourselves, you are going to get acrimonious and not-readily-falsifiable accusations of bad intent. (“Liar!” “Huh? You can argue that I’m wrong, but I actually believe what I’m saying!” “Oh, maybe consciously, but I was accusing you of being an algorithmic liar.”)

Unfortunately, as an aspiring epistemic rationalist, I’m not allowed to care whether some descriptions might be socially harmful for a human community to adopt; I’m only allowed to care about what descriptions shorten the length of the message needed to describe my observations.

The second paragraph is part (c) in its role-constitutive form, and it answers only one of the costs the first paragraph names. Accusations of algorithmic dishonesty will be acrimonious, and the rule is right that the social convenience of a description is no evidence of its truth. But the passage concerns descriptions socially harmful for a human community to adopt, and whether a community should adopt a vocabulary, encourage public accusations, or sanction people on their basis are questions of policy, on which social effects bear without being evidence about any particular attribution. The rule answers the epistemic question and leaves these open. They will also be “not-readily-falsifiable,” which is an epistemic cost. Davis’s criterion might seem to cover it: a false account of someone’s motives does not, over enough observations, shorten the description of his behavior. But an attribution of motive is usually made on few observations. Several hypotheses then compress them about equally well, and the accuser chooses among them. Davis described this mechanism in his own “Heads I Win” footnote, warning that his assumptions might have been “(subconsciously) selected for the resulting conclusions.” Viliam warned of sneaking in connotations, since inattentive readers would hear “intent” in its usual sense (Viliam, 14 Jul 2020). Davis could answer that “algorithmic” makes a weaker charge than “liar,” since it claims nothing about conscious bad faith; Viliam’s point is that the weaker charge will be heard as the stronger. Said Achmiz replied for Davis with a criterion from Daniel Dennett, the intentional stance: The positing of ‘algorithmic intent’ is appropriate in precisely those cases where taking the intentional stance is appropriate, where treating behavior as goal-directed yields a real gain in compressing its description (Said Achmiz, 15 Jul 2020). Dennett’s criterion is an evidential standard. It licenses an attribution only when the goal-directed description has been shown to predict better than the alternatives, which a single exchange rarely shows.

In the thread on “Maybe Lying Doesn’t Exist,” Wei Dai answered Jessica Taylor’s argument for blaming unconscious deception as freely as conscious deception with four reasons for treating them differently. Conscious liars can bring more intelligence to bear; endorsed lying needs a different response; conscious lies are harder to detect; and Unconscious deception is hard to distinguish from innocent mistakes. (Wei Dai, 16 Oct 2019). Two months later Alexander set out his view of definitions there, and Davis replied that he could not engage if you’re going to motivatedly play dumb like this (25 Dec 2019). Alexander answered: I absolutely deny I am ‘motivatedly playing dumb’ and I enter this into the record as further evidence that we shouldn’t redefine language to encode a claim that we are good at ferreting out other people’s secret motivations. (Scott Alexander, 25 Dec 2019). Davis called his own comment hugely inappropriately socially aggressive but did not withdraw the charge. After talking with Alexander the next day he reported a good conversation and a need for a follow-up post explaining his distinction for readers who don’t already consider it ‘obvious.’ (26 Dec 2019). As Chapter 4 records, Alexander amended his 2014 essay within the week, which suggests that he had been missing something. Whether he had been missing it motivatedly the evidence cannot decide, and Davis’s own note offers an explanation, insufficient exposition, that needs no hidden motive.

The problem has a second side, which Davis states more forcefully than his critics. A high evidential standard for attributing motive protects people falsely accused. It also protects behavior optimized to be deniable, and the examples in “Algorithmic Intent” are chosen to show this: the father who “loses control” only when calm conversation is failing him, and the baker who mishears only the questions he would rather not answer. If such behavior is strategic and unconscious, a standard that demands evidence of conscious intent will not reach it, though repeated convenient lapses can themselves be circumstantial evidence of conscious intent, and whether a given case is strategic is what has to be shown. “Maybe Lying Doesn’t Exist” accordingly criticizes the asymmetrical fear of false-positive identifications of ‘lying’ but not false-negatives. Any adequate standard has to weigh both errors, and the disagreement between Davis and his critics is partly over their relative cost, which Chapter 6 takes up.

Davis’s partial answers

Several of Davis’s later posts, read together, supply pieces of an evidential standard.

“Contra Piper on When Conversation Is Possible” (AAL, 7 September 2026) rejects Kelsey Piper’s claim that telling someone they are self-deceived makes conversation with them nearly impossible. Beliefs and motives predict behavior, so a person accused of a hidden motive can answer with evidence. Davis’s example is a salon member who buys its cookies from his girlfriend’s bakery in Australia, and who can rebut the charge of favoritism by producing his comparison-shopping spreadsheets. The correctness of my understanding of my beliefs and motives is something I can defend to skeptics in front of third parties (successfully or otherwise); it’s not something that needs to be assumed as a prerequisite for even talking at all. That answers the unfalsifiability worry where a motive leaves records, as a purchasing decision does. Motives alleged about speech, such as selective emphasis or pandering, rarely leave records of that kind, and those are the cases the attribution problem concerns. The example implies a duty on the accuser as well, which this study draws from it: a motive claim is admissible to the extent that it is tied to behavior others can inspect.

“‘So You Don’t Trust Me?’” (AAL, 26 August 2026) takes a line from Atlas Shrugged, that honest people are never touchy about being trusted, and explains it with the economics of separating and pooling equilibria. It considers the objection that an honest person has reason to resent distrust, and answers that an honest protest is cheap talk a dishonest person could imitate, while touchiness, which discourages scrutiny, is what a dishonest person has the incentive to display. The answer holds to the degree that honest people are in fact less touchy than dishonest ones, which depends on how insulting or costly the distrust is, and the post does not measure it. “Contra Piper” applies the argument in a way that shows its risk. Piper’s warning to Richard Ngo is read as a veiled negotiating threat intended to suppress a class of criticism, Davis’s maximum a posteriori (point estimate) interpretation. A point estimate is the most probable reading, not necessarily a probable one, and one reply in context can be some evidence of a negotiating threat while failing to distinguish it from a sincere warning. If declining a conversation counts as evidence of something to hide, and becoming defensive counts too, the two responses most open to the accused both count against them, and the question to put to the reading is what would count for her. The post’s evidence is the text of one reply and an account of the incentive; Piper’s position is known here only through Davis’s quotation of it. It does not ask whether a similar warning from an ally would have been read as a threat, the test proposed in the draft discussed below, written six weeks earlier. Its closing offer to discuss the reading if it is wrong demonstrates the post’s thesis that conversation remains possible, but it does not supply the missing evidence.

“Liability” (UUT, 24 December 2020), written after the caretaking failure narrated in Chapter 7, ends: Wanting a fair trial isn’t the same thing as claiming to be innocent. It’s wanting an accurate shared account of exactly what you’re guilty of. “Plea Bargaining” (AAL, 30 May 2022) asks the accused to name the true charge rather than deny everything. “Contra Pace on When to Apologize” (AAL, 18 June 2026) argues that an apology without a change of policy is a ledger entry not backed by assets, the difference between generosity and fraud. These posts move judgment from inner states, which observers cannot see, to policies, which they can.

An unpublished draft of July 2026, “Accidentally Misleading People Should Be Embarrassing; Or, Higher Innocence,” applies the account to its author. Davis once suspected an ideological rival of misrepresenting a citation because a browser search of an embedded PDF, defeated by a quirk of the browser, found nothing; a third party showed him the passage was there. Davis writes that he was so embarrassed, though he was innocent “in his soul”: he neither lied nor intended anything. He argues that embarrassment is the right response to having misled people by accident, because it commits one to a change of policy. Then the draft turns on its own absolution. If one goes looking for evidence that permits a conclusion one wants, in what sense is the resulting misleading an accident? The test it proposes is counterfactual:

But if I was too lazy to read six pages to check when it was my rival, but would have read the six pages if it had been my ally, that’s a reason people should be suspicious when I claim that my rivals did something wrong. I wasn’t lying, and that’s a kind of innocence, but the cold light of “Six pages? Really?” makes me want to aspire to a higher form of innocence.

Unpublished draft, “Accidentally Misleading People Should Be Embarrassing; Or, Higher Innocence,” AAL draft dated 28 Jul 2026

A functional attribution of motive is warranted, on this test, to the extent that one can show the behavior would have differed had the motive been absent: care withheld from a rival that would have been extended to an ally, or a standard invoked against one faction and waived for another. The comparison is not clean, since replacing a rival with an ally changes more than the motive: prior evidence about reliability, familiarity with the argument, and the value of checking may all differ. The asymmetry is a reason for scrutiny, and the alternatives still have to be examined. Single texts rarely meet the test, but patterns sometimes do. A pattern establishes less than it seems to, however. It shows that some filter correlated with allegiance is at work, and loyalty, fear, fatigue, and pandering all produce the same asymmetry. Naming the motive takes a further step, and the attributions examined in Chapter 7 name motives. The draft applies the same reasoning to a journalist’s paraphrase that Davis judged misleading. He grants that the journalist may have paraphrased fairly what was said to him, and so been “innocent in his soul,” and holds only that the episode should have prompted a change of policy.

The draft’s status limits what it shows. It is publicly accessible but unfinished, so it records how Davis was thinking in July 2026 and not a formulation he has completed or published. The embarrassment and the resolve to change his policy came, by the draft’s account, at the time of the browser incident, seven years earlier, and the 2019 footnote in “Heads I Win” already suspects his own assumptions. What is new in 2026 is the counterfactual test applied to himself. His attributions of motive to others date from 2019 to 2024, and the question is how consistently he applied that scrutiny when reading other people. Whether the memoirs meet its standard is a question for Chapter 7, and “Contra Piper,” written afterward, does not apply it.

6. Conclusion

Davis’s account of deception is intent-free and receiver-relative: a signal deceives when it is selected to worsen the predictions of a listener who decodes it at its conventional meaning. Philosophy of deception has moved toward a similar view by way of biological signaling, and Davis’s applications hold up: selective reporting misleads without false sentences, disclosing the filter helps the listener in proportion to what it tells about the selection, and a rule against literal falsehood leaves much of honesty unregulated. Intent survives within the account as a difference in how deception resists correction, which Davis himself recognized. The account’s main difficulty is the attribution problem. Attributions of deception to people depend on evidence of how they would have behaved otherwise, which is rarely available, and Davis met the difficulty in 2020 with a rule about what rationalists may care about, which answered the social cost and not the epistemic one. His later writing supplies a better answer in its counterfactual test, though even a pattern that meets the test shows selection more readily than it shows which motive did the selecting.
