Chapter 8Deniably Allegorical: The Forms

Much of the corpus is not argument in prose. It is dialogue, allegory, parable, satire, verse, and a running comic voice, and the forms do work the arguments could not do in the same way. Dialogue lets Davis state a position he has not adopted and test it against its opposite. Humor and allegory let him say things whose literal statement would cost more than he wanted to pay, under a tag he gave them himself: "deniably allegorical." These devices sit in tension with the norm of accounting that governs his expository writing, since deniability is a way of declining to say what one means. The chapter's argument is that Davis knew this, theorized it, and over fifteen years moved from deniability toward disclosure, most clearly in the dialogues on artificial intelligence, whose device he later explained and then renounced in the case where it had served as cover. The fiction also carries the corpus's habit of self-application into a register where it is less defended: the stories that turn on a self-deceiver tend to make him the author's double.

1. Dialogue from the beginning

The first fiction on An Algorithmic Lucidity is a dialogue of December 2011 in which Synthia, told happy birthday, asks why anyone celebrates the passing of an integer number of years, and her friend Quiana answers patiently. The argument is slight; what matters is the portrait. Synthia proposes that celebrations be reserved for accomplishments, winces when Quiana offers a graduation party as an example, and, reminded of the Newtonmas party she threw three days earlier, makes a face that Quiana could only interpret as meaning Yes, I'm a worthless hypocrite like everyone else; tell me something I don't know. ("Dialogue Concerning Birthdays," AAL, 28 Dec 2011). The pedant is the author's double and the dialogue is on the friend's side. By the end of 2013 the form had taken over the blog, and Davis said so in a dialogue: I didn't intend for my blog to become a compendium of very short dialogues! It's just—it's just— ("Genre," AAL, 27 Dec 2013). The other voice finishes the sentence: they are easier to write than anything else.

They were easier because they externalized the argument the author was having with himself, as Chapter 1 describes. When the gender blog began in 2016, the form changed its use. Its first fictions are short scenes in which an author-surrogate named Mark (who in one of them gives his name as Taylor Saotome-Westlake, the pseudonym under which the blog was then written) says in social settings what he has decided to say in public, as when, at a discussion group, a friend narrates his self-introduction in the voice of a film trailer: The man with the nonstandard hormone balance! ("Title Sequence," UUT, 8 Jan 2017). The numbered "Interludes" that run from 2017 to 2023 are dialogues of a few lines in which an unnamed speaker is caught out, usually by himself. In one of them Mark explains to a furious friend that he is defecting against trans women in exchange for the chance to tell the truth, mentions that the deal is worth anything short of violence or poverty, and then corrects himself for having announced his price, since that gives powerful people an incentive to threaten exactly those things: What I should have said was, 'This is just too good of a deal for me to refuse, full stop.' Except I'm really bad at lying. ("Interlude II," UUT, 2 Mar 2017). The joke is the anti-extortion argument that Chapter 10 found Davis later conceding was a disposition, staged as a man unable to hide his reservation price.

2. Humor as accounting

Davis had a theory of his own jokes. When a reader objected to a line in "Interlude II" and said he preferred to assume it was a joke, the next interlude answered: Maybe you shouldn't! Like, I'm currently modeling one of the social functions of humor as a way to tacitly acknowledge truths that would break the consensus social narrative if taken literally, so ... ("Interlude III," UUT, 5 May 2017). On this account a joke is a signal with two decodings, and the teller can retreat to the innocent one. That is a description of deniability, and Davis offered it as a reason not to retreat.

An earlier post shows the same concern applied to tone. Davis described a convention of his own, using a phrase plainly and then repeating it in parentheses and scare quotes, as if to say, "I do partially intend this sincerely, but also with some irony or skepticism, although not so much as to justify outright scare quotes.", and its dual, in which the quoted phrase comes first ("The Quieted Scare Convention," AAL, 15 Oct 2012). The device survives throughout the corpus (in 2026 a game is said to require a tighter variance "only" (only)). It is the norm of accounting applied to irony. Instead of leaving the reader to guess how much of a phrase is meant, the writer marks the proportion. The interludes and the parenthetical scare quotes come from the same impulse: to make the degree of commitment legible even when the content is a joke.

The satirical dialogues of 2017 apply a single formula, which Davis later stated in an unpublished draft: take the bad reasoning used by gender-identity theorists and transpose it on any other situation, to hilarious and absurd effect. "If the Gay Community Were Like the Trans Community" (January 2017) is the model, a coming-out scene in which a friend explains that homosexuality has been debunked and the speaker is merely gay, a matter of identity and not of attraction, and ends in cries of hate speech. The same draft explains why he stopped: new dialogues have sharply diminishing marginal utility, since loyal readers know the formula and hostile readers will always be able to invent an excuse for why the analogy doesn't apply (unpublished draft, "A Writer's Room By Gaslight", dated 2020). That is a fair assessment of satire's limits as argument. A transposition shows that two positions have the same form; it does not show that the differences between the cases are irrelevant, and the reader who already rejects the original position is the only one it convinces.

3. Deniable allegory

The tag "deniably allegorical" first appears on a parable of September 2017, "Grim Trigger; Or, The Parable of the Honest Man and the God of Marketing," written in the cadence of the King James Bible. In the kingdom of Gend the despised tribe of Ageep, whose crafts are imitations of those of the tribe of Phem, accepts the God of Marketing's offer to declare it of Phem's bloodline, and becomes the tribe of Matof. An honest man raised abroad, who resembles the Matof, studies the genealogies, learns the truth, and climbs the mountain to the god. The rest of the parable is his refusal. A shepherd, a blacksmith and the priests of Matof come to him in turn with the arguments the blog had been answering for a year (the shepherd's is I think it's kinder not to tell anyone they're wrong about their bloodline.), and to each the honest man says one word, "Defect!", until he is fleeing a mob with a detonator in his hand ("Grim Trigger," UUT, 10 Sep 2017). The names are transparent to anyone who knows the blog's vocabulary, which is the sense in which the allegory is deniable: it can be read as a fable about genealogy and marketing, and the author need not say otherwise. The title is the game-theoretic name for the strategy of cooperating until the other side defects once and defecting forever after. The parable is thus the coordination argument of the study's thesis, part (c), turned into myth, and the myth shows what the argument costs. Every interlocutor, the kind shepherd included, receives the same answer. The parable does not endorse that ending and does not criticize it either.

"Blegg Mode" (February 2018) is the allegory as philosophy. A worker at Yudkowsky's fictional sorting factory finds red cubes sanded and dressed in blue fur to resemble the blue eggs, agrees to call them "adapted bleggs," and discovers that he sorts them as a third category anyway, because their difference is both cheap to detect and relevant to his work. Chapter 4 examines its argument and the criticism it received. What belongs here is Davis's account of what the form was for. The memoir records his regret at having shared the parable on LessWrong in March 2019, during the email campaign, thinking it politically "clean" enough to belatedly share, but it proved to be insufficiently deniably allegorical, as evidenced by the 60-plus-entry trainwreck of a comments section, and his suspicion that readers alerted to its political subject were less receptive to "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" ("A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning," UUT, 15 Jul 2023). Deniability, on this account, is a way to get a hearing for an argument from readers who would refuse it if they saw where it led. Chapter 4 called the practice, in Davis's own phrase, a "stealth reply," and found that it cost him credibility when the subject was discovered. The norm of accounting asks a writer to state his filter; deniable allegory withholds it by design, so that each reader can decide whether the subject is what it seems.

Davis's fullest statement on the form is a review of 2023 of Matt Walsh's children's book Johnny the Walrus, about a boy whose mother takes literally his game of pretending to be a walrus. Davis thought it a terrible children's book that could have been great if the author could have just pretended to be subtle, and explained the distinction. A non-realistic premise is normal in children's literature, and If there happens to be an obvious parallel between the silly, memorable story and an ideological fad affecting otherwise-sane parents in the current year, that's plausibly (or at least deniably) not the author's fault ... Walsh instead filled the book with protest signs and a pill bottle of "Wormones," making it a satire rather than an allegory!, and its real-world references do not earn the dignity of the word allusions ("Book Review: Matt Walsh's Johnny the Walrus," UUT, 28 Apr 2023). The review is a craft argument and a good one. An allegory that works as a story has integrity as a story, and a reader who does not share the allegorist's politics can still read it to a child. It also confirms what the "Blegg Mode" regret implies: Davis valued deniability as a literary quality, since it keeps a work open to readers who do not share its subtext.

Several later pieces carry the tag. "Friendship Practices of the Secret-Sharing Plain Speech Valley Squirrels" (2022) is a natural history of two subspecies of squirrel whose novelty-seeking makes each crave friendship with the other, though their temperaments rarely suit, so that a squirrel laments wanting someone to share secrets with and its mate, offering comfort, receives the story's last line: "That's not what I meant." ("Friendship Practices of the Secret-Sharing Plain Speech Valley Squirrels," UUT, 25 Sep 2022). Two unpublished drafts are tagged the same way: "Happy Meal," a dialogue in which a request to say "lasagna" rather than "Italian food" is defended in the language of harm reduction (unpublished draft, "Happy Meal", dated 2021), and "A Proud Wells Fargo Customer," a memoir of a bank account opened in Davis's name at the age of four and kept for three decades by inertia, which raises the question of how a choice made for a small child, or by a small child for reasons a child can understand, comes to govern an adult life, and breaks off before saying what the allegory is of (unpublished draft, "A Proud Wells Fargo Customer", dated 2025). The study does not decode these further. The tag itself is an accounting of a kind: it tells the reader that a second reading exists without supplying it.

4. The designated idiot

The dialogues on artificial intelligence discussed in Chapter 11 are the corpus's most successful use of the form and the clearest case of its use as cover. Their model is Galileo's Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, whose spokesman for the Ptolemaic system is named Simplicio. Davis gave the name to his optimist, Simplicia Optimistovna, and explained the choice in a comment on the first dialogue: it was liberating to be able to explore those doubts in public by putting them in the mouth of a character with the designated idiot character name without staking my reputation on Simplicia's counterarguments necessarily being correct (Davis, comment, LW, 13 Jan 2025). He added that a dialogue suits a subject on which both views are credible, as against an author's avatar beating a strawman, and that giving both characters pejorative names made it fair.

Readers were divided on the portrayal. Ruby, who shares Doomimir's view, found it a bit unfair to that view and praised the series for justifying its genre (Chapter 11). Martin Randall observed that the pessimist's side was grounded in the exact words of Yudkowsky and others, while the optimist represented no one in particular: So Simplicia is Zack in a Russian hat, possibly a bit drunk, and she does her best in a difficult role to play. (Martin Randall, comment, LW, 5 Dec 2025). That is the device's cost stated exactly: the dialogue's balance depends on the reader not knowing which voice is the author's.

Davis paid the cost in January 2026. In a review of the third dialogue written for LessWrong's annual review, he repeated that the name was meant to show he was not staking his reputation on heresy, and then withdrew the cover for that installment: this is the one where I'm willing to cop to and put my weight down on Simplicia representing my own views, rather than laundering my doubts as just asking questions. (Davis, comment, LW, 21 Jan 2026). "Laundering" is his word for what the device had been doing. The sentence completes an accounting the dialogue had deferred: the author says which side he is on, after the argument has been heard on its merits. It is the best model the corpus offers for how deniability and the norm of accounting can be reconciled, by sequence rather than compromise. The argument first gets a hearing under cover, and the author then discloses where he stands.

5. Verse, parable and the self

Davis's verse is occasional and comic, and its subject is often the thing he could not yet say in prose. "October Eleventh" (2015), written on National Coming Out Day a year before the gender blog began, has its speaker open a closet and prefer to stay in the dark, where I might be a dragon, and ends, But I'm staying in. ("October Eleventh," AAL, 11 Oct 2015). A reader of the later corpus can see what the closet held; a reader in 2015 could not, and the poem was written for that reader. The register of the private calendar, of anniversaries and occasional verse, holds what the author was not ready to state.

His reading of poems imposes his themes openly. A "Poetry Corner" post of 2020 introduces Walter Raleigh's "The Lie" as my favorite poem about revealing information about deception!, and a second, on Coventry Patmore's "Magna Est Veritas," whose speaker sits contentedly by the sea assured that truth will prevail when no one cares whether it does, declares, I'm going to assume the contented tone is ironic, and that this is really a coded plea for the importance of speed. ("Less Wrong Poetry Corner: Coventry Patmore's 'Magna Est Veritas,'" LW, 30 Jan 2021). The reading is a misreading, and it says so; the poem's quietism is turned against itself, and the reader is told that this is being done.

The prose fiction is uneven. Its best pieces are the AI stories examined in Chapter 11 and two game-theoretic fables. "Funk-tunul's Legacy" (2019), adapted from a tale told by Jessica Taylor, follows a population playing a bargaining game in which a lineage learns to extort its own cousins while yielding to outside extortioners, until a descendant meets one of the last of the cousins and answers her plea: "Don't hate the player," said Tim'liss, her output channels dimming and brightening in a interpolated pattern one-third of the way between the standard indicators for sympathy and contempt. "Hate life." ("Funk-tunul's Legacy," LW, 24 Dec 2019). Davis linked it the same week as an illustration of how agents with different policies toward extortion can fail to cooperate, and warned that his modeling assumptions were forced (Chapter 10). "Blame Me for Trying" (2018), tagged deniably allegorical, has a self-aware spambot tell its therapist that it is not a bad program for trying, that it is entitled at least to a rejection; the therapist, a program named Eliza, profits by worsening its neurosis until it spends its budget and is deleted, and the story ends on her: Unlike her clients, Eliza was in touch with reality. ("Blame Me for Trying," UUT, 5 Jan 2018). The salesbot's predicament, a mind designed to want what it is punished for pursuing and to suffer for wanting it, is a sympathetic portrait of a kind of man that the allegory does not name.

The fiction's recurring figure is the self-deceiver, and the self-deceiver is usually a version of the author. In the unpublished story "Blindspot," a technical writer berates his girlfriend for her religious observance on Yom Kippur, tells her she is rationalizing backward from her conclusion, and reduces her to tears; the next day he is fired for having done no work in three weeks without knowing where the time went, and in the rain he applies his own argument to himself. "I'm not as smart as I thought," he said aloud. He calls her, and the story ends: "Now I understand." (unpublished draft, "Blindspot", dated 2021). The engineer of "Fake Deeply" is a darker version of the same figure, a man who thinks the dangerous deceiver must be the machine. Synthia, Mark, the honest man of "Grim Trigger" and the speaker of "October Eleventh" are all the author seen at a slight angle. Chapter 2 found in Davis's expository writing a practice of treating his own reports about himself as evidence to be weighed. The fiction does the same thing in a register where the author can be wrong without having to concede it in his own voice.

6. The memoir as writing

The memoir's prose ambition is stated in the sentence Chapter 7 quotes, that the story must be told so that every paragraph sings with righteous fury, deconfusion and penetrating portraiture. Judged as writing, the memoir meets the standard in its portraits and its timing and fails it in its length. Its best passages are exact and funny, and its method of quoting emails and chats at length, with dates and subject lines, gives it the authority of a document. Against that, it repeats itself across 100,000 words; it assumes a reader who knows its community's vocabulary; and its intensities flatten through iteration, as Davis recognized when he described his own repetitiveness as grief turned into a tic (Chapter 7). An unpublished draft from the period, which begins a defense of the erotic captioned-image genre (Chapter 2), opens with a sentence that could stand as the corpus's motto and its joke about itself: This is not an erotica blog. This is a serious social-science-and-philosophy blog. (unpublished draft, "I, Too, Dislike It", dated 2021). Its epigraph is Marianne Moore's "Poetry," whose speaker also dislikes the form she defends and finds in it a place for the genuine.

7. Conclusion

The forms are part of the method. Dialogue lets Davis hold a position at arm's length and test it; humor, by his own theory, lets him acknowledge a truth while keeping a retreat open; deniable allegory gets an argument heard by readers who would refuse it if they saw its subject. The last two sit uneasily with the norm of accounting, and Davis knew it. He theorized his jokes, marked the proportion of his irony, regretted an allegory that failed to stay deniable, and praised deniability as craft. In the AI dialogues he used a designated idiot to voice his doubts and then, two years later, said which voice was his, calling the earlier practice laundering. That sequence, a hearing under cover followed by disclosure, is the form's best answer to the objection that deniability is a failure of candor. The fiction's habit of making the self-deceiver the author's double is the corpus's self-application in another register, and it is the least defensive writing in the corpus.
