Chapter 8Deniably Allegorical: The Forms

Davis frequently stages his arguments in other forms: dialogue, allegory, parable, satire, verse, and a running comic voice. Dialogue lets him state a position he has not adopted and test it against its opposite. Humor and allegory let him say things whose literal statement would cost more than he wanted to pay, under a tag he gave them himself: “deniably allegorical.” These devices raise different questions about candor, whether a character’s assertion belongs to its author, whether a work’s application is identifiable, and whether it withholds something a reader needs in order to judge its argument, and they do not all answer them the same way. Davis theorized his devices, and in one documented case, the dialogues on artificial intelligence, he later said which voice was his; elsewhere deniability and disclosure run side by side to the end of the corpus. The fiction also turns repeatedly on a self-deceiver, sometimes recognizably the author.

1. Dialogue from the beginning

The first fiction on An Algorithmic Lucidity is a dialogue of December 2011 in which Synthia, told happy birthday, asks why anyone celebrates the passing of an integer number of years, and her friend Quiana answers patiently. The argument is slight, and the scene’s interest is its portrait. Synthia proposes that celebrations be reserved for accomplishments, winces when Quiana offers a graduation party as an example, and, reminded of the Newtonmas party she threw three days earlier, makes a face that Quiana could only interpret as meaning Yes, I’m a worthless hypocrite like everyone else; tell me something I don’t know. (“Dialogue Concerning Birthdays,” AAL, 28 Dec 2011). The self-condemnation arrives through the friend’s reading of a face, and Synthia’s inconsistency does not refute her argument about celebrations; the dialogue lets the pedant be caught without letting the friend answer her. By the end of 2013 the form had taken over the blog, and Davis said so in a dialogue: I didn’t intend for my blog to become a compendium of very short dialogues! It’s just—it’s just— (“Genre,” AAL, 27 Dec 2013). The other voice finishes the sentence: they are easier to write than anything else.

One explanation, which is this study’s and not the post’s, is that they externalized the argument the author was having with himself, as Chapter 1 describes. When the gender blog began in 2016, the form changed its use. Its first fictions are short scenes in which an author-surrogate named Mark (who in one of them gives his name as Taylor Saotome-Westlake, the pseudonym under which the blog was then written) says in social settings what he has decided to say in public, as when, at a discussion group, a friend narrates his self-introduction in the voice of a film trailer: The man with the nonstandard hormone balance! (“Title Sequence,” UUT, 8 Jan 2017). The numbered “Interludes” that run from 2017 to 2023 are dialogues of a few lines in which an unnamed speaker is caught out, usually by himself. In one of them Mark explains to a furious friend that he is defecting against trans women in exchange for the chance to tell the truth, mentions that the deal is worth anything short of violence or poverty, and then corrects himself for having announced his price, since that gives powerful people an incentive to threaten exactly those things: What I should have said was, ‘This is just too good of a deal for me to refuse, full stop.’ Except I’m really bad at lying. (“Interlude II,” UUT, 2 Mar 2017). The joke stages the anti-extortion argument that Chapter 10 found Davis later conceding was a disposition, as a man unable to hide his reservation price, and it does two things at once: it exposes the limits of the strategy, and it advertises the speaker’s honesty as the reason for them.

2. Humor as accounting

Davis had a theory of his own jokes. When a reader objected to a line in “Interlude II” and said he preferred to assume it was a joke, the next interlude answered: Maybe you shouldn’t! Like, I’m currently modeling one of the social functions of humor as a way to tacitly acknowledge truths that would break the consensus social narrative if taken literally, so ... (“Interlude III,” UUT, 5 May 2017). On this account a joke is a signal with two decodings, and the teller can retreat to the innocent one. That is a description of deniability, and Davis offered it as a reason not to retreat.

An earlier post shows the same concern applied to tone. Davis described a convention of his own, using a phrase plainly and then repeating it in parentheses and scare quotes, as if to say, ‘I do partially intend this sincerely, but also with some irony or skepticism, although not so much as to justify outright scare quotes.’, and its dual, in which the quoted phrase comes first (“The Quieted Scare Convention,” AAL, 15 Oct 2012). The device survives throughout the corpus (in 2026 a game is said to require a tighter variance “only” (only)). Instead of leaving the reader to guess whether a phrase is meant, the writer marks that it is meant in part, though not how large the part is. The interludes and the parenthetical scare quotes come from the same impulse: to make the degree of commitment legible even when the content is a joke.

The satirical dialogues of 2017 apply a single formula, which Davis later stated in an unpublished draft: take the bad reasoning used by gender-identity theorists and transpose it on any other situation, to hilarious and absurd effect. “If the Gay Community Were Like the Trans Community” (January 2017) is the model, a coming-out scene in which a friend explains that homosexuality has been debunked and the speaker is merely gay, a matter of identity and not of attraction, and ends in cries of hate speech. The same draft explains why he stopped: new dialogues have sharply diminishing marginal utility, since loyal readers know the formula and hostile readers will always be able to invent an excuse for why the analogy doesn’t apply (unpublished draft, “A Writer’s Room By Gaslight”, dated 2020). The first point is fair: readers who know the formula learn little from another instance. The second treats objections to an analogy as evasions before examining them, and an objection to a transposition can identify a relevant difference between the cases. The force of such satire depends on whether the transposition preserves the relation in dispute, here the relation between attraction, identity and classification; its comedy can make that relation easier to see, but cannot show that the transfer is sound.

3. Deniable allegory

The tag “deniably allegorical” first appears on a parable of September 2017, “Grim Trigger; Or, The Parable of the Honest Man and the God of Marketing,” written in the cadence of the King James Bible under an epigraph from the twenty-third Psalm, I will fear no evil. In the kingdom of Gend the despised tribe of Ageep, whose crafts are imitations of those of the tribe of Phem, accepts the God of Marketing’s offer to declare it of Phem’s bloodline, and becomes the tribe of Matof. An honest man raised abroad, who resembles the Matof, meets one of them at an oasis and answers his “Cooperate” with “Cooperate,” studies the genealogies, learns the truth, and climbs the mountain to the god. The rest of the parable is his refusal. A shepherd, a blacksmith and the priests of Matof come to him in turn with the arguments the blog had been answering for a year (the shepherd’s is I think it’s kinder not to tell anyone they’re wrong about their bloodline.), and to each the honest man says one word, “Defect!”, until he is fleeing a mob wielding pitchforks, torches, and the occasional brick with a detonator in his hand (“Grim Trigger,” UUT, 10 Sep 2017). The names are transparent to anyone who knows the blog’s vocabulary, and the tag announces that there is an application, so what is deniable is not that the parable has a target but which claims about it the author would defend in his own voice. The title is the game-theoretic name for the strategy of cooperating until the other side defects once and defecting forever after, and the story shows how that strategy joins the study’s part (c): the honest man cooperates until he learns that the tribe’s cooperation with the god was a lie, and then refuses everyone, the kind shepherd included, with the same word. The ending is triumphal and farcical at once. The psalm casts him as the faithful man who fears no evil, and the brick and the detonator push the escalation into slapstick, so that the story both celebrates the refusal and laughs at it. The fable also settles inside its world the question the real dispute is about, since the genealogical scrolls are there to be read and the honest man reads them. A critic can say that the story wins its argument by giving its hero the facts. Davis’s answer would be that a fable can show what follows if the facts are settled, and that is what this one does; it is no evidence that they are.

“Blegg Mode” (February 2018) is the allegory as philosophy. A worker at Yudkowsky’s fictional sorting factory finds red cubes sanded and dressed in blue fur to resemble the blue eggs, agrees to call them “adapted bleggs,” and discovers that he sorts them as a third category anyway, because their difference is both cheap to detect and relevant to his work. Chapter 4 examines its argument and the criticism it received. What belongs here is Davis’s account of what the form was for. The memoir records his regret at having shared the parable on LessWrong in March 2019, during the email campaign, thinking it politically ‘clean’ enough to belatedly share, but it proved to be insufficiently deniably allegorical, as evidenced by the 60-plus-entry trainwreck of a comments section, and his suspicion that readers alerted to its political subject were less receptive to “Where to Draw the Boundaries?” (“A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning,” UUT, 15 Jul 2023). Deniability, on this account, is a way to get a hearing for an argument from readers who would refuse it if they saw where it led. Davis’s strongest defense is that withholding the application lets the argument be judged on its own terms before prejudice about its subject can substitute for judgment. The critics’ strongest reply is that the application may decide whether the chosen similarities are the relevant ones, so that an example built with its application in mind can be tendentious in ways only the application reveals. Chapter 4 called the practice, in Davis’s own phrase, a “stealth reply,” and Davis later granted that it may have been unfair to expect readers to engage only with the politically clean text. The norm of accounting asks a writer to state his filter. Deniable allegory withholds the application, which is not always the same thing, but a reader who discovers a withheld application can fairly wonder what else was withheld, and on Davis’s own account readers who saw the application of “Blegg Mode” became less receptive to the argument that followed.

Davis’s fullest statement on the form is a review of 2023 of Matt Walsh’s children’s book Johnny the Walrus, about a boy whose mother takes literally his game of pretending to be a walrus. Davis thought it a terrible children’s book that could have been great if the author could have just pretended to be subtle, and explained the distinction. A non-realistic premise is normal in children’s literature, and If there happens to be an obvious parallel between the silly, memorable story and an ideological fad affecting otherwise-sane parents in the current year, that’s plausibly (or at least deniably) not the author’s fault ... Walsh instead filled the book with protest signs and a pill bottle of “Wormones,” making it a satire rather than an allegory!, and its real-world references do not earn the dignity of the word allusions (“Book Review: Matt Walsh’s Johnny the Walrus,” UUT, 28 Apr 2023). The distinction between satire and allegory is Davis’s working one, and the two are not exclusive. His point is that a story which works as a story keeps readers who do not share its politics. A story stripped of its explicit markers can also make its lesson harder to examine, and the review does not weigh that cost. It also confirms what the “Blegg Mode” regret implies: Davis valued deniability as a literary quality, since it keeps a work open to readers who do not share its subtext.

Several later pieces carry the tag, among them a natural history of two subspecies of squirrel who crave each other’s friendship (“Friendship Practices of the Secret-Sharing Plain Speech Valley Squirrels,” UUT, 25 Sep 2022) and an unpublished memoir of a bank account opened in Davis’s name at the age of four, which breaks off before saying what the allegory is of (unpublished draft, “A Proud Wells Fargo Customer”, dated 2025). The study does not decode them. The tag itself is an accounting of a kind: it tells the reader that a second reading exists without supplying it.

4. The designated idiot

The dialogues on artificial intelligence discussed in Chapter 11 are the corpus’s most successful use of the form and the clearest case of its use as cover. Their model is Galileo’s Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, whose spokesman for the Ptolemaic system is named Simplicio. Davis gave the name to his optimist, Simplicia Optimistovna, and explained the choice in a comment on the first dialogue: it was liberating to be able to explore those doubts in public by putting them in the mouth of a character with the designated idiot character name without staking my reputation on Simplicia’s counterarguments necessarily being correct (Davis, comment, LW, 13 Jan 2025). He added that a dialogue suits a subject on which both views are credible, as against an author’s avatar beating a strawman, and that giving both characters pejorative names made it fair.

Readers were divided on the portrayal. Ruby, who shares Doomimir’s view, found it a bit unfair to that view and praised the series for justifying its genre (Chapter 11). Martin Randall observed that the pessimist’s side was grounded in the exact words of Yudkowsky and others, while the optimist represented no one in particular: So Simplicia is Zack in a Russian hat, possibly a bit drunk, and she does her best in a difficult role to play. (Martin Randall, comment, LW, 5 Dec 2025). The remark states one cost of the device: a reader who knows which voice is the author’s reads the dialogue as advocacy.

Davis paid the cost in January 2026. In a review of the third dialogue written for LessWrong’s annual review, he repeated that the name was meant to show he was not staking his reputation on heresy, and then withdrew the cover for that installment: this is the one where I’m willing to cop to and put my weight down on Simplicia representing my own views, rather than laundering my doubts as just asking questions. (Davis, comment, LW, 21 Jan 2026). “Laundering” is his word for what the device had been doing. The sentence completes an accounting the dialogue had deferred: the author says which side he is on, after the argument has been heard on its merits. It shows one way deniability and the norm of accounting can be reconciled, by sequence. The reconciliation has limits. A later disclosure reaches only the readers who find it, and it repairs something only if the dialogue had invited a belief about the author that was false; this one had marked its uncertainty by its form, and the disclosure locates the author’s commitment in one installment without settling whether the other speaker had a fair hearing.

5. Verse, parable and the self

Davis’s verse is occasional and comic, and its subject is often a thing he had not yet said in prose. “October Eleventh” (2015), written on National Coming Out Day a year before the gender blog began, has its speaker open a closet and prefer to stay in the dark, where I might be a dragon and I might be a woman, and ends, But I’m staying in. (“October Eleventh,” AAL, 11 Oct 2015). A reader of the later corpus can see what the closet held; a reader in 2015 had the date, the closet, and a woman named in the same breath as a dragon, and the poem, by making the dragon equally possible, leaves the guess open. The occasion, a public anniversary kept privately, is the register in which the early corpus approaches its most personal subject.

His reading of poems imposes his themes openly. A “Poetry Corner” post on Coventry Patmore’s “Magna Est Veritas,” whose speaker sits contentedly by the sea assured that truth will prevail when no one cares whether it does, declares, I’m going to assume the contented tone is ironic, and that this is really a coded plea for the importance of speed. (“Less Wrong Poetry Corner: Coventry Patmore’s ‘Magna Est Veritas,’” LW, 30 Jan 2021). The reading announces itself as an assumption against the poem’s apparent tone, so the reader is told that the poem’s quietism is being turned against itself.

The prose fiction is uneven. Its best pieces are the AI stories examined in Chapter 11 and two game-theoretic fables. “Funk-tunul’s Legacy” (2019), adapted from a tale told by Jessica Taylor, follows a population playing a bargaining game in which a lineage learns to extort its own cousins while yielding to outside extortioners, until a descendant meets one of the last of the cousins and answers her plea: ‘Don’t hate the player,’ said Tim’liss, her output channels dimming and brightening in a interpolated pattern one-third of the way between the standard indicators for sympathy and contempt. ‘Hate life.’ (“Funk-tunul’s Legacy,” LW, 24 Dec 2019). Davis linked it the same week as an illustration of how agents with different policies toward extortion can fail to cooperate, and warned that his modeling assumptions were forced (Chapter 10). “Blame Me for Trying” (2018), tagged deniably allegorical, has a self-aware spambot tell its therapist that it is not a bad program for trying, that it is entitled at least to a rejection; the therapist, a program named Eliza, profits by worsening its neurosis until it spends its budget and is deleted, and the story ends on her: Unlike her clients, Eliza was in touch with reality. (“Blame Me for Trying,” UUT, 5 Jan 2018). The salesbot’s predicament, a mind designed to want what it is punished for pursuing and to suffer for wanting it, is a sympathetic portrait of a kind of man that the allegory does not name. The allegory also makes a strong allocation of blame, since the therapist profits from her client’s deterioration, and its last sentence makes being in touch with reality the exploiter’s distinction.

The fiction returns to the self-deceiver. In the unpublished story “Blindspot,” a technical writer berates his girlfriend for her religious observance on Yom Kippur, tells her she is rationalizing backward from her conclusion, and reduces her to tears; the next day he is fired for having done no work in three weeks without knowing where the time went, and in the rain he applies his own argument to himself. ‘I’m not as smart as I thought,’ he said aloud. He calls her, and the story ends: ‘Now I understand.’ (unpublished draft, “Blindspot”, dated 2021). The engineer of “Fake Deeply” is a darker version of the figure, a man who thinks the dangerous deceiver must be the machine, and the story’s irony is built into its dialogue: he plays the attentive student to the investigator who suspects the model, and finishes her sentence about deception with the line that describes him. The writer of “Blindspot” resembles Davis in the argument he turns on himself; Synthia, Mark and the honest man are surrogates of other kinds, holding positions he holds without being shown deceiving themselves. Where the fiction does turn its arguments on a figure like the author, it models a self-correction without documenting one, in a register where the author can be wrong without conceding it in his own voice.

Two other fictions use form to make an argument a reader must perform. “Feature Selection” (2021) is told in the second person to a newly awakened program, and it prints the data the program receives, long sequences of small integers broken by bursts of larger ones and short sequences between 32 and 89. A reader who knows that 32 is a space in ASCII can decode the labels before the program does, and a reader who does not decodes them with it, so that the story’s thesis, stated in its own words, Compression is prediction, is enacted on its reader. The program’s discovery that the data are images of colored shapes comes only after it has learned to earn its reward, and the order of the story, competence first and understanding afterward, is the order of training (“Feature Selection,” AAL, 31 Oct 2021; its argument is Chapter 11’s). “Two Political Short Stories” (2020) is a diptych of futures imagined in 2016 and 2020; in the second, a dystopia’s machine that had cured bias by treating the sexes differently until the difference was forbidden puts into fiction the argument of the Planecrash comment that Chapter 10 examines (“Two Political Short Stories,” UUT, 7 Nov 2020). The narrator, called by the blog’s old pseudonym, glances at a woman’s breasts while explaining the machine, and the story does not flatter him: the corpus’s self-mockery does the work of fairness in a satire that would otherwise have only one target.

6. The memoir as writing

The memoir’s prose ambition is stated in the sentence Chapter 7 quotes, that the story must be told so that every paragraph sings with righteous fury, deconfusion and penetrating portraiture. Judged as writing, its method of quoting emails and chats at length, with dates and subject lines, gives it the authority of a document, and the same method is the source of its length. It repeats itself across 100,000 words; it assumes a reader who knows its community’s vocabulary; and its intensities flatten through iteration, as Davis recognized when he described his own repetitiveness as grief turned into a tic (Chapter 7). An unpublished draft from the period, which begins a defense of the erotic captioned-image genre that Chapter 2 treats as testimony, opens with a sentence that could stand as the corpus’s motto and its joke about itself: This is not an erotica blog. This is a serious social-science-and-philosophy blog. (unpublished draft, “I, Too, Dislike It”, dated 2021). Its epigraph is Marianne Moore’s “Poetry,” whose speaker also dislikes the form she defends and finds in it a place for the genuine, and the draft goes little further than the epigraph.

7. Conclusion

The forms are part of the method, and they redistribute commitment. A character can voice an objection Davis has not accepted, a joke can make a claim available without requiring a confrontation, and an allegory can make a disputed premise a fact of its fictional world. These possibilities call for different standards of assessment, and Davis supplied some of them himself: he theorized his jokes, marked the presence of irony, regretted an allegory that failed to stay deniable, praised deniability as craft, and in the AI dialogues said, a year and a half later, which voice was his, calling the earlier practice laundering. That disclosure helps readers locate his commitment in one dialogue. It does not settle whether the other speaker received a fair hearing, and elsewhere in the corpus the deniable and the disclosed continue side by side.
