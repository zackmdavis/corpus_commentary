Chapter 10The Egalitarian Schelling Point: Politics and Race

Davis's politics are less a set of positions than a refusal: he will not accept egalitarian doctrines as descriptions of human beings, and he offers a theory of why other people do. On that theory, which he built from Cailin O'Connor's game-theoretic work and set out in his 2020 review of Charles Murray's Human Diversity, the belief that groups do not differ is a coordination point that protects against group conflict, and its defenders are not foolish. Davis then argued openly for abandoning it, giving his reasons and his personal stake. That argument is the norm of accounting at its best. It rests on a dichotomy between two equilibria of belief that leaves out a third, true belief combined with a rule about treatment, and Davis's own coordination argument, applied to law, supports that rule better than it supports his rejection of it. On the empirical question of race and intelligence, Davis's claim that the American black–white gap is probably partly genetic goes beyond what the literature shows, and his confidence in it rose over four years without new argument. His charge that the New York Times was right about Scott Alexander for the wrong reasons depends on a use of the word racist that his own philosophy of categories should have rejected.

1. A politics of description

The corpus contains almost no policy. Its first political statement is a quatrain of 2012 whose third line is I don't know how to run a goddamned country ("A Political Orientation (Provisional)," AAL, 1 Nov 2012), and a post of 2016 compares voters' favored interventions to a chess novice's intuitive moves: the interventions that sound intuitively appealing aren't necessarily the same as the interventions that will actually work! ("Missing Refutations," AAL, 17 Feb 2016). In March 2017 Davis revised the quatrain into a maxim, Being smart is more important than being good (for humans). All ideologies are false; some are useful. ("Dreaming of Political Bayescraft," AAL, 3 Mar 2017), and recorded in two lines his departure from the coalition he had assumed was his: You know, maybe I'm just ... not part of the Blue Team? Maybe I can live with that? ("Brand Rust," AAL, 26 Mar 2017).

The memoir dates the break to the gender dispute and explains its mechanism. Davis had read heterodox writing on race and sex for years without doubting the progressive account, because he did not think it his place to doubt claims made on behalf of groups he did not belong to. When the sympathetic group became men like himself, the account could be checked against his own case, and he concluded that he had standing ("Blanchard's Dangerous Idea and the Plight of the Lucid Crossdreamer," UUT, 8 Jul 2023). He was registered as a Libertarian; in a story of 2020 his narrator votes for Jo Jorgensen. The aims he states are liberal ones. He wants all people to be free to maximize their potential, unencumbered by oppressive cultural institutions specifying roles and destinies in advance, and the essay that says so ends on a transhumanist resolve, I want to make the stupid dream real. ("There's a Land That I See," UUT, 30 Dec 2017). An unpublished account of a meeting with a neoreactionary writer gives the shortest summary: I just want to restore the moral spirit of 2008 liberalism but with better epistemology (unpublished draft, "East Coast Trip", dated 2025).

What distinguishes this from ordinary liberalism is the second clause. Davis's grievance against the left is epistemic, and an early statement of it is a post of January 2017 that sets two quotations side by side: Anne Fausto-Sterling explaining that her political commitment to equality led her to demand the highest standards of proof for claims of biological difference, and Steven Goldberg replying that the consequences of a belief have nothing to do with its truth. Davis's comment is the corpus's political autobiography in a sentence: I just hate hate hate it when people saying the good things turn out to be bad at epistemology, and people who are good at epistemology turn out to say the bad things. ("And Yet None More Blameable," UUT, 28 Jan 2017). Fausto-Sterling's practice is an instance of what Chapter 5 called a disclosed filter, and the corpus's quarrel with it is the quarrel of the whole political writing: whether an asymmetric standard of proof, openly declared, is a legitimate response to the risk that a finding will be abused.

2. The arguments for speech

Davis's defenses of free inquiry are the most widely accepted part of his political writing and the least specific to his own causes. The first, of 2017, models speech as the sum of an information-carrying component and a component of social control, and argues that a coalition does better if it lets its members share information, while a rule against the social-control component is dangerous unless it is very specific, since you probably won't notice when members of the coalition begin to slowly gerrymander the hate speech category boundary in the service of their own values ("An Intuition on the Bayes-Structural Justification for Free Speech Norms," AAL, 8 Mar 2017). The argument connects the philosophy of categories in Chapter 4 to the politics: a category whose boundary is set by interested parties will drift.

The second is an analogy of 2019, made against a researcher at MIRI who doubted that public argument about gender had done anything for the safety of artificial intelligence. A calculator that never displays 13 is harder to build than a correct one, because the simplest implementation is a correct calculator plus a special case, and a research community that must avoid the Pope's displeasure has a harder problem than one that need only be right: you have to somehow both get the right answer, and throw out all of the steps of your reasoning that the Pope doesn't want you to say ("Free Speech and Triskaidekaphobic Calculators," LW, 21 Dec 2019). Scott Alexander's reply, the most upvoted comment on the post, turned the analogy around. The special case is exactly what he proposed: We discuss whatever we want, we use logic which (we hope) would output the correct (taboo) answer on controversial questions, but if for some reason those questions come up (which they shouldn't, because they're pretty different from AI-related questions), we instead don't talk about them. (Alexander, comment, LW, 21 Dec 2019). Davis answered that real problems pass through forbidden intermediate results, so that a calculator barred from 13 also fails on every expression whose computation reaches 13, and that he thought we do end up with the analogue of nonsense-math in large areas of psychology, sociology, political science, history (Davis, reply, LW, 24 Dec 2019). When Alexander later said that academics who appease the taboo in public get everything 100% right in private, Davis's reply was the shortest statement of his case: Getting the right answer in private can only help those you talk to in private. (Davis, reply, LW, 6 Jan 2020).

The exchange is a fair test, and each side holds part of the ground. Alexander's version works where the forbidden questions are isolated from the work, as cosines never equal 13. Davis's works where they are entangled with it, and his examples, psychology and the social sciences, are the fields where entanglement is most plausible. The empirical question between them was put by steven0461, who looked at a year of the forum to which such topics had been exiled and reported that If none of it had been posted, as far as I can tell, the rationalist community wouldn't have been any more wrong on any major issue. (steven0461, comment, LW, 28 Dec 2019). Neither side settled it.

The same reply contains a disclosure that bears on the study's thesis. Davis had often defended his refusal to yield to offense by appeal to decision theory, the anti-extortion form of the coordination argument. In a footnote he withdrew the pretense: he had very recently realized that this was kind of stupid and/or intellectually dishonest of me, since he would probably not change his behavior if the mathematics came out otherwise, and the real explanation is a raw emotional disposition that might be usefully summarized in English as, "Do your worst, you motherfuckers!" Of his conjecture that the unilateralist's curse argues against censorship of ordinary speech, the next footnote says: But I have pretty intense psychological reasons to want to believe this, so maybe you shouldn't believe me until I actually come up with the math. This is the norm of accounting applied to part (c) itself. The exceptionless stance is acknowledged as a disposition, not a theorem, and the reader is told to discount it. The post on the heckler's veto that followed (LW, March 2020) supplied part of the argument, observing that Nick Bostrom and his coauthors had defined the unilateralist's curse to cover a single member's power to spoil an initiative as well as to undertake one, so that any one pre-reader with a veto is as much a unilateralist as the author who publishes.

3. The Schelling-point theory

The idea for which Davis's political writing is most worth reading is a theory of why egalitarian beliefs persist despite evidence against them. Its materials come from O'Connor's The Origins of Unfairness (2019), which Davis reviewed in January 2020. O'Connor models gender roles as conventions that solve coordination problems in the division of labor, with visible group markers serving to break the symmetry, and shows that such conventions tend to favor one group under minimal assumptions. Ann Cudd's review in Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews calls the results "how possibly" arguments whose force depends on how well the models fit typical interactions, and judges that they hit the mark (Cudd 2019). What Davis took from the book was its darkness: O'Connor shows inequality arising without malice, and concludes, in a sentence he quotes, that social justice is an endless battle. He praised her for not softening the models: That's how you know it's a good book. The map that reflects the territory is equally useful to good people and to bad men. ("Book Review: Cailin O'Connor's The Origins of Unfairness," UUT, 20 Jan 2020).

The Murray review three months later applies the framework to belief. It begins as a summary of the science, which Davis said he would prefer to write, and turns to the question of why no one can write such a summary without positioning it in a political war. Murray's own positioning, a challenge to orthodoxy accompanied by assurances that nothing learned will threaten human equality properly understood, Davis judges sympathetic but ultimately ineffective, since a claim to be non-disprovable is what one invents when one fears disproof. His alternative is to ask what the blank-slate doctrine does:

Notice how the "not allowing sex and race differences in psychological traits to appear on shared maps is the Schelling point for resistance to sex- and race-based oppression" actually gives us an explanation for why one might reasonably have a sense that there are dread doors that we must not open. Undermining the "everyone is Actually Equal" Schelling point could catalyze a preference cascade—a slide down the slippery slope to the the next Schelling point, which might be a lot worse than the status quo on the "amount of rape and genocide" metric, even if it does slightly better on "estimating heritability coefficients." The orthodoxy isn't just being dumb for no reason.

"Book Review: Charles Murray's Human Diversity," UUT, 28 Apr 2020

He adds that religious authorities were right to fear heliocentrism and evolution: if the prevailing coordination equilibrium depends on lies, then telling the truth is a threat and it is disloyal. The theory has two virtues. It explains the moral seriousness of the taboo without imputing stupidity or bad faith to those who keep it, which is rarer in writing on this subject from Davis's side than from the other. And it treats the dispute as a choice between equilibria, each with costs, which is the form a disagreement must take before the norm of accounting can be applied to it.

Davis then applied it. After explaining why a reader should suspect anyone who raises these questions, he gave his own reasons, saying that it was up to the reader to judge for herself how credible I am when I say this: he wants to stay loyal to the egalitarian axiology and also wants a world that's not lying to me. The review ends by naming the choice and the chooser's stake:

You can't brainwash a human with random bits; they need to be specific bits with something good in them. I would still be with the program, except that the current coordination equilibrium is really not working out for me. So it is with respect for the good works enabled by the anti-oppression Schelling point belief, that I set my sights on reorganizing at the other Schelling point of just tell the goddamned truth—not in spite of the consequences, but because of the consequences of what good people can do when we're fully informed.

"Book Review: Charles Murray's Human Diversity"

Of the places where part (b) of Davis's position displaces part (c), this is the clearest in the corpus. The departure from the prevailing equilibrium is argued on consequentialist grounds, with the opposing equilibrium credited for its goods, and the author's interest in the outcome is declared in the same sentence as the decision. A commenter who said he had shared Murray's view wrote that the review had upended significant portions of that worldview and singled out the Schelling-point framing (K Smith, comment, Isso, 12 Aug 2021). That the theory came out of the gender dispute, and that its author's stake was personal, is stated by the author, and on the study's standing rule it explains the timing and not the validity.

4. Belief and treatment

The theory has a gap, and the gap is in the menu. The review recognizes two Schelling points of belief, that groups are equal and that one should tell the truth, and treats the second as carrying a norm of treatment with it. It quotes, from Murray, Steven Pinker's formula, Equality is not the empirical claim that all groups of humans are interchangeable; it is the moral principle that individuals should not be judged or constrained by the average properties of their group., and asks whether it can be taken literally: Technically?—no! That's actually not how epistemology works! Getting to know anyone is probabilistic inference from what one observes, and there is no principled difference between updating on an Emacs T-shirt and updating on sex. Davis supports the point with a 1990 study by Nelson and colleagues in which, as he describes it, people judging heights from photographs used the subjects' sex as a prior even when told that the photographs were height-matched and offered money for accuracy. He concludes that the moral spirit of egalitarianism survives as a demand for better individual information, and that injustice to outliers is a problem that realistically needs to be solved with better tests and better signals, not by pretending not to have a prior.

The Planecrash comment of 2022 takes the argument from belief to law. Davis objects to a scene in which the hero of Yudkowsky's fiction proposes that a country's laws should not mention sex, even for conscription: What we call "discrimination" in America on Earth is actually just Bayesian reasoning, and is Governance supposed to be less Bayesian because it's Governance?! ("Comment on a Scene from Planecrash," UUT, 12 Jun 2022). His arithmetic is correct: with a sex difference in muscle mass of 2.6 standard deviations, a strength threshold set one standard deviation below the male mean admits men and women in a ratio of about 15 to 1. He grants that for most jobs "always test the individual's aptitude, never use sex as a proxy" is a fine rule, and diagnoses the American intuition against conditioning on sex as an idea born of distrust in reasoning in an adversarial environment.

That diagnosis contains the answer to the rhetorical question. A rule against conditioning on certain categories is not an epistemic claim and need not pretend to be one. It is a constraint on the inputs to a decision procedure, adopted because the procedure's operator is powerful and not trusted, as the rules of evidence bar a jury from hearing a defendant's prior convictions though they are informative. One commenter put this in a sentence: You want yourself to be able to measure things and make different decisions based on the measurements in full generality, but not your enemy. (pto, comment, Isso, 20 Jun 2023). Yudkowsky, replying two years later, gave the hero's reason, that any information carried by sex would appear in the prediction markets on military performance, and asked what remains to be gained by creating a law that asymmetrically treats different sapient beings in a way based not on predicted outcomes? (Yudkowsky, comment, Isso, 22 Sep 2024). Davis had anticipated a version of this, in a parenthesis that suggests that law might lay out timeless rights and principles, whereas day-to-day decisions about the empirical world are farmed out to the latter, meaning public policy, and did not pursue it.

Davis's own coordination argument is the strongest case for such a rule. "Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point," discussed in Chapter 4 and Chapter 5, holds that on a continuous trade-off with no salient intermediate points, only the extremes are stable. The extent to which a state may condition its treatment of citizens on race or sex is such a trade-off, and "never" is its salient extreme. A legal rule of that kind does not require anyone to believe that groups are identical. It requires the state not to act on the belief, at a cost that can be stated: some decisions will be worse than a Bayesian would make. That is a disclosed, costed departure from optimal inference, which the study's thesis holds is not a defection, and it makes available the third equilibrium the review omits, true belief held publicly together with a fence around treatment. Pinker's sentence describes that equilibrium. Read as a rule rather than a theorem about epistemology, it survives Davis's objection.

The corpus comes close to this position several times. A post of 2022 on the far-right objection to tolerating homosexuality ends on the question whether the libs got it right the first time, and it's possible to just—defy the signaling incentives? ("The Signaling Hazard Objection," UUT, 17 Sep 2022). And the unpublished review of Murray's Facing Reality (2021) states the difficulty for the third equilibrium more plainly than any critic. Murray hopes that the center will reaffirm equality before the law; Davis is strongly in favor of this, and doubts it will hold, because part of what made equality before the law such an effective marketing promise was the unstated premise that it would lead to, you know, actual equality. If that's empirically not true—if people don't believe that it's true—what happens to our Society? His answer is I don't know. (unpublished draft, "Book Review: Charles Murray's Facing Reality", dated 2021). This is the right question, and it is empirical: whether a rule of equal treatment can keep its authority once the belief that seemed to underwrite it has gone. The published review assumed an answer by treating the belief as the only fence. The draft, which was never published, admits that the answer is unknown.

A further objection came from tailcalled, one of Davis's most technically engaged readers. Davis had said there is no principled difference between inferring from a T-shirt and inferring from sex. tailcalled proposed one: a measurement caused by the trait in question tracks the individual, while a proxy merely correlated with it does not, so that if your measurement is causally downstream from your variable of interest, it's treating people as individuals, while if it's confounded with or causally upstream from your variable of interest, it's stereotyping. (tailcalled, comment, Isso, 20 Apr 2021). The distinction gives a causal content to "treating people as individuals" that survives Bayesian criticism, since a downstream measurement captures the exceptions that a correlated category misses, and it is the kind of causal refinement Davis himself was making in his work on categories in the same years (Chapter 4). The corpus does not answer it.

5. What Davis claims about race and intelligence

Davis's claims on this subject need to be stated exactly, because they changed. The Murray review of 2020 is hedged. It gives the American black–white gap in tested IQ as about one standard deviation, rehearses the standard caveats (within-group heritability does not establish the cause of between-group differences; the tests could be biased; environments differ), and then describes several lines of argument that it says make purely environmental explanations look less compelling when you look into the details: that test bias would show up in item statistics, that controlling for parental socioeconomic status reduces the gap by only a third, that the within-group heritability constrains how different the groups' environments would have to be. Its claim is that genetically mediated differences between ancestral populations are probably going to be part of the explanation for patterns of inequality. And it disclaims authority: I'm not (that?) interested in this particular topic, and without having mastered the technical literature, my assessment would be of little value.

The unpublished review of Facing Reality a year later is more critical of Murray than the published one. Murray's book argues that group differences in tested ability and in violent crime are real while staying agnostic about their causes, and Davis objects that this is causally incoherent: Murray's claims about what produces imprisonment and educational outcomes are already causal, and this causality-blindness—or not-quite-coherently attempted causality-blindness—is profoundly unsatisfying, since any sophisticated advocate of structural explanations can grant the statistics and hold that the differences would vanish in a different society. He calls plea bargaining a perversion of justice and Murray's confidence that the authorities are trying to get it right a blindness bordering on complicity, drawing on his own experience of psychiatric detention (Chapter 9). His verdict is that his impression of the book is favorable but restrained.

By 2023 and 2024 the claim had hardened. The unpublished sequel to the memoir recalls the Murray review as having set out the technical reasons for believing that the gap is real and partly genetic, while distinguishing it from the position of a hereditarian blogger who held that anyone denying a genetic cause was a mind-killed idiot (unpublished draft, "Guess I'll Die", dated 2023). The last published installment of the memoir goes further, saying of the community's response to the Times that instead of pointing out that being a racist like Charles Murray is the obviously correct position that sensible people will tend to reach in the course of being sensible, we disingenuously deny everything ("Agreeing With Stalin in Ways That Exhibit Generally Rationalist Principles," UUT, 2 Mar 2024). In four years "probably part of the explanation," offered by an author who said his assessment had little value, became "the obviously correct position." The corpus contains no new argument in between.

The literature does not support the stronger claim. That a gap in tested IQ exists is not in dispute among mainstream researchers; Eric Turkheimer, Kathryn Paige Harden and Richard Nisbett, replying in 2017 to Murray and Sam Harris, accept that it exists, that IQ predicts outcomes and that it is partly heritable, and reject the inference that the gap must therefore be partly genetic: We believe there is currently no strong evidence to support this conclusion, whereas Murray presents it as a near certainty (Turkheimer, Harden & Nisbett 2017). The 2012 review of intelligence research by Nisbett and six coauthors, published in American Psychologist, repeated the American Psychological Association task force's verdict of 1996, “There is not much direct evidence on this point, but what there is fails to support a genetic hypothesis.” That conclusion stands today: There has been no new direct evidence on the question. (Nisbett et al. 2012). The direct evidence it cites is of two kinds: studies of children of mixed ancestry, in which European ancestry brought no measurable advantage (Eyferth's study of children fathered by black and white American soldiers in postwar Germany; Moore's adoption study of 1986), and the narrowing of the gap, by 5.5 IQ points between 1972 and 2002 in William Dickens and James Flynn's analysis of standardization samples, a figure that Philippe Rushton and Arthur Jensen contested.

The arguments Davis rehearses are answered in this literature. The constraint from within-group heritability depends on assuming that the environmental factors that differ between groups act like those that vary within them; the Flynn effect, which Turkheimer and his coauthors put at about 18 points in American scores between 1948 and 2002, shows that environmental change can move a highly heritable trait by more than the size of the gap, and they cite adoption into better-off homes as raising IQ by 12 to 18 points. The new genomic methods, which Murray's book and Davis's review expected to settle the question, have so far not supported a genetic explanation. Kevin Bird's 2021 test, using polygenic scores for educational attainment in African and European samples, found no evidence of diversifying selection when within-family effect sizes were used, and estimated that under a neutral model the expected genetic contribution to a difference was much smaller than hereditarians propose (Bird 2021). Bird also notes how poorly such scores, built from samples of European ancestry, predict outcomes in people of African ancestry, which makes them unreliable for comparisons between those groups in either direction.

The hereditarian case is mostly indirect. Its best form, in Bo Winegard, Ben Winegard and Jonathan Anomaly's "Dodging Darwin" (2020), is that populations which evolved in different environments differ in many traits and that it would be surprising if cognition were the exception, so that a partial genetic hypothesis is the one most consistent with Darwinism. Davis's version is the same: We could certainly imagine that none of the relevant selection pressures on humans happened to touch the brain—but why? Wouldn't that be kind of a weird coincidence? The argument establishes that some difference in some direction is a priori possible, which no one denies. It does not establish the sign or the size of a difference in a particular trait between two particular populations, and Bird's result is evidence that for cognitive performance the neutral expectation is small. Surveys of expert opinion, which hereditarians cite, are compromised by response rates of around five percent, according to Turkheimer and his coauthors, and by the absence of the option they consider the only reasonable one, that the proportion cannot yet be meaningfully estimated.

The study's verdict is that the cause of the gap is unknown, that the direct tests available so far tell against a substantial genetic contribution or are uninformative, and that "probably partly genetic" is a defensible minority opinion only when held as loosely as Davis held it in 2020. "Obviously correct" is not defensible. Davis's own best methodological remark tells against his later confidence: the unpublished Facing Reality review insists that the difference between a statistical and a causal model is the whole question, and the hereditarian claim is a causal claim of exactly the kind that the existing statistics do not identify. His draft on COVID-19 outcomes shows the more careful habit. Against a journalist who wrote that there is nothing about being black that affects susceptibility, it argues only that the claim is too strong, granting that structural factors are almost certainly going to be a significant part the explanation and proposing a specific biological candidate, vitamin D and skin pigmentation, that could be tested (unpublished draft, "The Causality of Ethnic Differences in COVID-19 Outcomes Is an Open Empirical Question", dated 2021). That is the posture the evidence permits on intelligence as well.

6. The charge against Alexander

The New York Times article of February 2021 on Alexander's blog, by Cade Metz (whose dealings with Davis are the subject of Chapter 7), said that Alexander had aligned himself with Charles Murray in a post that in fact mentioned Murray only as a fellow skeptic that poor workers could be retrained, and supported a basic income. Alexander called this a weirdly brazen type of falsehood for a major newspaper, describing the article as implying that he agreed with Murray's offensive views on race (Alexander 2021). Davis agreed that the inference was invalid and objected to the reply: in invalidating the Times's charge of guilt by association with Murray, Alexander validates Murray's guilt. He argued from Alexander's published writing, a review accepting twin-study evidence and an essay accepting a genetic explanation of Ashkenazi intelligence, that Alexander probably finds genetic group differences plausible, and concluded that the Times had made a "Gettier attack": the charge is essentially correct, even though the evidence used to prosecute the charge before a jury of distracted New York Times readers is completely bogus.

Two claims are joined here. The factual one, that Alexander privately or implicitly holds views closer to Murray's than his public statement suggested, is supported by the essays Davis cites and by the content of a 2014 email from Alexander that Topher Brennan published a few days after the article, which Davis summarizes as saying that biologically mediated group differences are probably real. (The email was private correspondence published without consent; this study relies only on Davis's description of it.) The evaluative claim is that the charge was therefore "essentially correct." The charge was racism, a word that in the Times's use and its readers' understanding carries animus or the endorsement of unjust treatment. Davis's sentence keeps the word and changes its extension to a belief about group averages held with the standard caveats, and then credits the newspaper's verdict under the new extension. By the account of categories that Davis defended against Alexander for a decade (Chapter 4), this is a gerrymander. A category drawn to include both a person who hates a group and a person who accepts a psychometric estimate, while still carrying the condemnation appropriate to the first, predicts badly about the second. It is the noncentral fallacy, which Davis accused Alexander of praising when it ran in a sympathetic direction. Elsewhere Davis was careful. The memoir's account of a Facebook comment he wrote that week says that the charge that Alexander and his readers were "crypto-sexists" was absolutely correct if "sexism" included "It's an empirical question whether innate statistical psychological sex differences of some magnitude exist, it empirically looks like they do, and this has implications about our social world", and that conditional states the definition and leaves the reader free to reject it. The Gettier sentence does not.

The section of the memoir in which these passages appear makes a broader claim about motive: that in conflict with progressives, the defensive strategy of the "rationalists" is one of deception, its leaders privately believing what they publicly deny. The attribution problem of Chapter 5 applies, and for once the counterfactual evidence exists in the form of the leaked email, which shows a difference between Alexander's private and public statements. It does not show that the public statement was false, since a person may find a hypothesis plausible and also think it right to say that a newspaper's inference was invalid. Davis applied the accusation to himself in the next breath: I fear my love of Truth is not so great that if I didn't have Something to Protect, I would have happily participated in the cover-up. The sentence concedes that his own departure from the community's strategy was driven by his particular stake, which the Murray review had also said, and it is the kind of disclosure the study has credited throughout. It also weakens the claim it accompanies. If Davis would have joined the cover-up had his own interest not been at stake, then the others' silence is explained by their interests and their judgment of risk, which is a weaker charge than deception.

7. Conclusion

Davis's political writing makes one durable contribution and several overreaches. The contribution is the theory that egalitarian belief is a coordination point that protects against group conflict, which takes the orthodoxy seriously on its own terms and turns a quarrel about facts into a choice between equilibria. The Murray review makes that choice in the open, states its costs and its author's stake, and is part (b) of Davis's position at its best. The overreaches follow from a dichotomy the theory does not need. Davis treats true belief and differential treatment as a package, when his own Schelling-point argument, applied to the state, supports a fence around treatment that leaves belief free, and the question he raised in the unpublished Facing Reality review, whether such a fence can stand without the belief that seemed to support it, is the one his published work should have asked. On race and intelligence specifically, the thesis frame does not decide anything: the question is empirical, the study's verdict rests on the literature, and that verdict is that Davis's loosely held claim of 2020 was defensible and his confident claim of 2024 was not. His treatment of Alexander shows the same drift, from a stated conditional to an unstated redefinition.

The disclosure in the triskaidekaphobia footnote is the passage to set against these. There Davis said that his refusal to bargain with offense was a disposition and not a calculation, and told readers to discount his arguments for it until he had done the mathematics. Most of the political writing is held to that standard, and the parts that are not are the parts where the stance outran the argument.
