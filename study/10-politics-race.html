Chapter 10The Egalitarian Schelling Point: Politics and Race

Davis's politics are less a set of positions than a refusal: he will not accept egalitarian doctrines as descriptions of human beings, and he offers a theory of why other people do. On that theory, which he built from Cailin O'Connor's game-theoretic work and set out in his 2020 review of Charles Murray's Human Diversity, the belief that groups do not differ is a coordination point that protects against group conflict, and its defenders are not foolish. Davis then argued openly for abandoning it, giving his reasons and his personal stake, and the argument is candid about both. The published review answers injustice to individuals with better information and does not consider a rule about treatment that would leave belief free. Davis's concessions elsewhere, and his own argument about salient lines, point to such an arrangement, and the question he raised in an unpublished draft, whether such a rule can hold once the belief that seemed to support it has gone, is where the real disagreement lies.

On race and intelligence, Davis's claim of 2020 that the American black–white gap is probably partly genetic is a judgment the evidence does not settle, and his later presentation of it as obvious went beyond both the evidence and his own earlier disclaimer. His charge that the New York Times was right about Scott Alexander for the wrong reasons concedes, to readers who supply the word's usual condemnation, more than he believes.

1. A politics of description

The corpus contains almost no policy. Its first political statement is a quatrain of 2012 whose third line is I don't know how to run a goddamned country ("A Political Orientation (Provisional)," AAL, 1 Nov 2012), and a post of 2016 compares voters' favored interventions to a chess novice's intuitive moves: the interventions that sound intuitively appealing aren't necessarily the same as the interventions that will actually work! ("Missing Refutations," AAL, 17 Feb 2016). In March 2017 Davis revised the quatrain into a maxim, Being smart is more important than being good (for humans). All ideologies are false; some are useful. ("Dreaming of Political Bayescraft," AAL, 3 Mar 2017), and recorded in two lines his departure from the coalition he had assumed was his: You know, maybe I'm just ... not part of the Blue Team? Maybe I can live with that? ("Brand Rust," AAL, 26 Mar 2017).

The memoir dates the break to the gender dispute and explains its mechanism. Davis had read heterodox writing on race and sex for years without doubting the progressive account, because he did not think it his place to doubt claims made on behalf of groups he did not belong to. When the sympathetic group became men like himself, the account could be checked against his own case, and he concluded that he had standing ("Blanchard's Dangerous Idea and the Plight of the Lucid Crossdreamer," UUT, 8 Jul 2023). He was, by the same account, registered to vote as a Libertarian, and the narrator of one of his "Two Political Short Stories" (UUT, 7 Nov 2020) votes for Jo Jorgensen. The aims he states are liberal ones. He wants all people to be free to maximize their potential, unencumbered by oppressive cultural institutions specifying roles and destinies in advance, and the essay that says so ends on a transhumanist resolve, I want to make the stupid dream real. ("There's a Land That I See," UUT, 30 Dec 2017). An unpublished account of a meeting with a neoreactionary writer gives the shortest summary: I just want to restore the moral spirit of 2008 liberalism but with better epistemology (unpublished draft, "East Coast Trip", dated 2025).

What distinguishes this from ordinary liberalism is the second clause. Davis states his grievance against the left in epistemic terms, and an early statement of it is a post of January 2017 that sets two quotations side by side: Anne Fausto-Sterling explaining that her political commitment to equality led her to demand the highest standards of proof for claims of biological difference, and Steven Goldberg replying that the consequences of a belief have nothing to do with its truth. Davis's comment is the corpus's political autobiography in a sentence: I just hate hate hate it when people saying the good things turn out to be bad at epistemology, and people who are good at epistemology turn out to say the bad things. ("And Yet None More Blameable," UUT, 28 Jan 2017). Fausto-Sterling's practice is an instance of what Chapter 5 called a disclosed filter, and the corpus's quarrel with it is the quarrel of the whole political writing: whether an asymmetric standard of proof, openly declared, is a legitimate response to the risk that a finding will be abused. Philosophers of science treat the question under the name of inductive risk, whether the consequences of error may raise the evidence required to accept or act on a claim (the standard statements, recalled here from memory, are Richard Rudner's of 1953 and Heather Douglas's of 2009). On that view Fausto-Sterling's practice can be defended without denying Goldberg's point that consequences do not bear on truth, and Goldberg's point does not by itself settle the dispute.

2. The arguments for speech

Davis's defenses of free inquiry are the part of his political writing least specific to his own causes. The first, of 2017, models speech as the sum of an information-carrying component and a component of social control, and argues that a coalition does better if it lets its members share information, while a rule against the social-control component is dangerous unless it is very specific, since you probably won't notice when members of the coalition begin to slowly gerrymander the hate speech category boundary in the service of their own values ("An Intuition on the Bayes-Structural Justification for Free Speech Norms," AAL, 8 Mar 2017). The argument connects the philosophy of categories in Chapter 4 to the politics: a category whose boundary is set by interested parties will drift.

The second is an analogy of 2019, made against a researcher at MIRI who doubted that public argument about gender had done anything for the safety of artificial intelligence. A calculator that never displays 13 is harder to build than a correct one, because the simplest implementation is a correct calculator plus a special case, and a research community that must avoid the Pope's displeasure has a harder problem than one that need only be right: you have to somehow both get the right answer, and throw out all of the steps of your reasoning that the Pope doesn't want you to say ("Free Speech and Triskaidekaphobic Calculators," LW, 21 Dec 2019). Scott Alexander's reply, the highest-rated comment on the post, turned the analogy around. The special case is exactly what he proposed: We discuss whatever we want, we use logic which (we hope) would output the correct (taboo) answer on controversial questions, but if for some reason those questions come up (which they shouldn't, because they're pretty different from AI-related questions), we instead don't talk about them. (Alexander, comment, LW, 21 Dec 2019). Davis answered that real problems pass through forbidden intermediate results, so that a calculator barred from 13 also fails on every expression whose computation reaches 13, and that he thought we do end up with the analogue of nonsense-math in large areas of psychology, sociology, political science, history (Davis, reply, LW, 24 Dec 2019). When Alexander later said that academics who appease the taboo in public get everything 100% right in private, Davis's reply was the shortest statement of his case: Getting the right answer in private can only help those you talk to in private. (Davis, reply, LW, 6 Jan 2020).

The exchange is a fair test, and each side holds part of the ground. Alexander's version works where the forbidden questions are isolated from the work, as cosines never equal 13. Davis's works where they are entangled with it, and his examples, psychology and the social sciences, are the fields where entanglement is most plausible. The empirical question between them was put by steven0461, who looked at a year of the forum to which such topics had been exiled and reported that If none of it had been posted, as far as I can tell, the rationalist community wouldn't have been any more wrong on any major issue. (steven0461, comment, LW, 28 Dec 2019). The count bears on whether the exiled discussion produced conclusions; Davis's claim is that its absence bends the reasoning that remains, and neither side settled that.

The same reply contains a disclosure that bears on the study's thesis. Davis had often defended his refusal to yield to offense by appeal to decision theory, the anti-extortion form of the coordination argument. In a footnote he withdrew the pretense: he had very recently realized that this was kind of stupid and/or intellectually dishonest of me, since he would probably not change his behavior if the mathematics came out otherwise, and the real explanation is a raw emotional disposition that might be usefully summarized in English as, "Do your worst, you motherfuckers!" Of his conjecture that the unilateralist's curse argues against censorship of ordinary speech, the next footnote says: But I have pretty intense psychological reasons to want to believe this, so maybe you shouldn't believe me until I actually come up with the math. This is the norm of accounting applied to part (c) itself. The exceptionless stance is acknowledged as a disposition, not a theorem, and the reader is told to discount it.

3. The Schelling-point theory

The idea for which Davis's political writing is most worth reading is a theory of why egalitarian beliefs persist where, on Davis's view, the evidence tells against them. Its materials come from O'Connor's The Origins of Unfairness (2019), which Davis reviewed in January 2020. O'Connor models gender roles as conventions that solve coordination problems in the division of labor, with visible group markers serving to break the symmetry, and shows that such conventions tend to favor one group under minimal assumptions. Ann Cudd's review in Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews calls the results "how possibly" arguments whose force depends on how well the models fit typical interactions, and judges that they hit the mark (Cudd 2019). What Davis took from the book was its darkness: O'Connor shows inequality arising without malice, and concludes, in a sentence he quotes, that social justice is an endless battle. He praised her for not softening the models: That's how you know it's a good book. The map that reflects the territory is equally useful to good people and to bad men. ("Book Review: Cailin O'Connor's The Origins of Unfairness," UUT, 20 Jan 2020).

The Murray review three months later applies the framework to belief. It begins as a summary of the science, which Davis said he would prefer to write, and turns to the question of why no one can write such a summary without positioning it in a political war. Murray's own positioning, a challenge to orthodoxy accompanied by assurances that nothing learned will threaten human equality properly understood, Davis judges sympathetic but ultimately ineffective, since a claim to be non-disprovable is what one invents when one fears disproof. His alternative is to ask what the blank-slate doctrine does:

Notice how the "not allowing sex and race differences in psychological traits to appear on shared maps is the Schelling point for resistance to sex- and race-based oppression" actually gives us an explanation for why one might reasonably have a sense that there are dread doors that we must not open. Undermining the "everyone is Actually Equal" Schelling point could catalyze a preference cascade—a slide down the slippery slope to the the next Schelling point, which might be a lot worse than the status quo on the "amount of rape and genocide" metric, even if it does slightly better on "estimating heritability coefficients." The orthodoxy isn't just being dumb for no reason.

"Book Review: Charles Murray's Human Diversity," UUT, 28 Apr 2020

He adds that religious authorities were right to fear heliocentrism and evolution: if the prevailing coordination equilibrium depends on lies, then telling the truth is a threat and it is disloyal. The theory explains the moral seriousness of the taboo without imputing stupidity or bad faith to those who keep it. It does impute a function, and the attribution problem of Chapter 5 applies to it as it does to claims about individuals: that a belief protects against conflict does not show that it is held because it does, rather than on the evidence as its holders read it, and the review does not test the one explanation against the other. It also treats the dispute as a choice between equilibria, each with costs, which is the form a disagreement must take before the norm of accounting can be applied to it.

Davis then applied it. After explaining why a reader should suspect anyone who raises these questions, he gave his own reasons, saying that it was up to the reader to judge for herself how credible I am when I say this: he wants to stay loyal to the egalitarian axiology and also wants a world that's not lying to me. The review ends by naming the choice and the chooser's stake:

You can't brainwash a human with random bits; they need to be specific bits with something good in them. I would still be with the program, except that the current coordination equilibrium is really not working out for me. So it is with respect for the good works enabled by the anti-oppression Schelling point belief, that I set my sights on reorganizing at the other Schelling point of just tell the goddamned truth—not in spite of the consequences, but because of the consequences of what good people can do when we're fully informed.

"Book Review: Charles Murray's Human Diversity"

Of the places where Davis argues for his norm by giving an account of it, its consequences and its costs, instead of appealing to its exceptionlessness, this is the one where the account is given most fully. The departure from the prevailing equilibrium is argued on consequentialist grounds, with the opposing equilibrium credited for its goods, and the author's interest in the outcome is declared in the same sentence as the decision. A commenter who said he had shared Murray's view wrote that the review had upended significant portions of that worldview and singled out the Schelling-point framing (K Smith, comment, Isso, 12 Aug 2021), and a reader who described herself as passionate about the goals of the progressive movement wrote that it had put into words the distinction between those goals and their specific methodology (Emma, comment, Isso, 29 Apr 2020). The one dismissal in the thread, that Davis was willfully ignorant of any sociological knowledge because of ideological reasons, drew a request for textbook recommendations (minette, comment, Isso, 5 Mar 2024). No published critical response to the review was located. That the theory came out of the gender dispute, and that its author's stake was personal, is stated by the author, and on the study's standing rule it explains the timing and not the validity.

4. Belief and treatment

The review's menu has two Schelling points of belief, that groups are equal and that one should tell the truth, and it treats the second as carrying its own norm of treatment: judge individuals by the best information available, and improve the information. It quotes, from Murray, Steven Pinker's formula, Equality is not the empirical claim that all groups of humans are interchangeable; it is the moral principle that individuals should not be judged or constrained by the average properties of their group., and asks whether it can be taken literally: Technically?—no! That's actually not how epistemology works! Getting to know anyone is probabilistic inference from what one observes, and there is no principled difference between updating on an Emacs T-shirt and updating on sex. Davis supports the point with a 1990 study by Nelson and colleagues in which, as he describes it, people judging heights from photographs used the subjects' sex as a prior even when told that the photographs were height-matched and offered money for accuracy. The review uses the economists' term for the practice, statistical discrimination, and grants two grounds for outrage that survive it: the case where an individual has a property but I can't prove it to you, and tragedies in which a feedback loop between predictions and social norms creates or amplifies group differences that wouldn't exist under some other social equilibrium. He concludes that the moral spirit of egalitarianism survives as a demand for better individual information, and that injustice to outliers is a problem that realistically needs to be solved with better tests and better signals, not by pretending not to have a prior.

The Planecrash comment of 2022 takes the argument from belief to law. Davis objects to a scene in which the hero of Yudkowsky's fiction proposes that a country's laws should not mention sex, even for conscription: What we call "discrimination" in America on Earth is actually just Bayesian reasoning, and is Governance supposed to be less Bayesian because it's Governance?! ("Comment on a Scene from Planecrash," UUT, 12 Jun 2022). His arithmetic is correct: assuming normal distributions with equal spread and equal numbers of applicants, with a sex difference in muscle mass of 2.6 standard deviations, a strength threshold set one standard deviation below the male mean admits men and women in a ratio of about 15 to 1. He grants that for most jobs "always test the individual's aptitude, never use sex as a proxy" is a fine rule, and diagnoses the American intuition against conditioning on sex as an idea born of distrust in reasoning in an adversarial environment.

The diagnosis also answers the rhetorical question. A rule against conditioning on certain categories is not an epistemic claim and need not pretend to be one. It is a constraint on the inputs to a decision procedure, adopted because the procedure's operator is powerful and not trusted. One commenter put this in a sentence: You want yourself to be able to measure things and make different decisions based on the measurements in full generality, but not your enemy. (pto, comment, Isso, 20 Jun 2023). Yudkowsky, replying two years later, gave the hero's reason, that any information carried by sex would appear in the prediction markets on military performance, and asked what remains to be gained by creating a law that asymmetrically treats different sapient beings in a way based not on predicted outcomes? (Yudkowsky, comment, Isso, 22 Sep 2024). Davis had anticipated a version of this, in a parenthesis that suggests that law might lay out timeless rights and principles, whereas day-to-day decisions about the empirical world are farmed out to the latter, meaning public policy, and did not pursue it.

Davis himself grants the rule for most jobs, so the question is not whether a fence around treatment can be drawn but how far it extends and whether it holds. The coordination argument of "Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point" (Chapter 4 and Chapter 5) bears on the second question. On a continuous trade-off with no salient intermediate point only the extremes are stable, and "never use the category as a proxy" is a salient extreme in a way that "use it a little" is not. A legal rule of that form does not require anyone to believe that groups are identical. It requires the state not to act on the belief, at a stated cost in predictive accuracy that may be repaid in fair treatment and resistance to abuse, and it is a disclosed and costed constraint on action, which leaves inference where it was and which the norm of accounting permits. The feedback loop the review itself concedes supplies a second reason for such a rule, one that needs no distrust of the decider: acting on a group prior can help make the prior true. Pinker's sentence, read as a rule of this kind rather than as a claim about how epistemology works, describes the arrangement, and Davis's objection does not reach it.

The corpus comes close to this position several times. A post of 2022 on the far-right objection to tolerating homosexuality ends on the question whether the libs got it right the first time, and it's possible to just—defy the signaling incentives? ("The Signaling Hazard Objection," UUT, 17 Sep 2022). And the unpublished review of Murray's Facing Reality (2021) states the difficulty for the third equilibrium more plainly than any critic. Murray hopes that the center will reaffirm equality before the law; Davis is strongly in favor of this, and doubts it will hold, because part of what made equality before the law such an effective marketing promise was the unstated premise that it would lead to, you know, actual equality. If that's empirically not true—if people don't believe that it's true—what happens to our Society? His answer is I don't know. (unpublished draft, "Book Review: Charles Murray's Facing Reality", dated 2021). The question is empirical: whether a rule of equal treatment can keep its authority once the belief that seemed to underwrite it has gone. The worry has a mechanism, which the draft implies without stating: under a rule of equal treatment together with a belief in equal capacities, persisting differences in outcome can be explained only by continuing discrimination, and that explanation presses for remedies judged by outcomes, and so against the rule itself. The published review did not raise the question. The draft, which was never published, raises it and admits that the answer is unknown. The disagreement between Davis and the arrangement sketched here is therefore less over whether belief and treatment can be distinguished, which he grants, than over how far the fence should extend and whether it would hold.

A further objection came from tailcalled, one of Davis's most technically engaged readers. Davis had said there is no principled difference between inferring from a T-shirt and inferring from sex. tailcalled, crediting the idea tentatively to Borsboom, proposed one: a measurement caused by the trait in question tracks the individual, while a proxy merely correlated with it does not, so that if your measurement is causally downstream from your variable of interest, it's treating people as individuals, while if it's confounded with or causally upstream from your variable of interest, it's stereotyping. A measurement of the first kind, he argued, is at least not totally blind to exceptions (tailcalled, comment, Isso, 20 Apr 2021). The proposal gives "treating people as individuals" a causal content, and it is the kind of causal refinement Davis himself was making in his work on categories in the same years (Chapter 4). Davis's best reply would be that an informative individual measurement still leaves the group prior relevant to the posterior unless it is precise enough to swamp it, and that causal position does not guarantee precision. The proposal then needs either very informative measurements or an independent reason for excluding the prior, and the second brings it back to a rule about treatment. Davis did not reply in the thread.

5. What Davis claims about race and intelligence

Davis's claims on this subject need to be stated exactly, because they changed. The Murray review of 2020 is hedged. It gives the American black–white gap in tested IQ as about one standard deviation, rehearses the standard caveats (within-group heritability does not establish the cause of between-group differences; the tests could be biased; environments differ), and then describes several lines of argument that it says make purely environmental explanations look less compelling when you look into the details: that test bias would show up in item statistics, that controlling for parental socioeconomic status reduces the gap by only a third, that the within-group heritability constrains how different the groups' environments would have to be. Its claim is that genetically mediated differences between ancestral populations are probably going to be part of the explanation for patterns of inequality. And it disclaims authority: I'm not (that?) interested in this particular topic, and without having mastered the technical literature, my assessment would be of little value.

The unpublished review of Facing Reality a year later is more critical of Murray than the published one. Murray's book argues that group differences in tested ability and in violent crime are real while staying agnostic about their causes, and Davis objects that the agnosticism is not consistently held: Murray's claims about what produces imprisonment and educational outcomes are already causal, and this causality-blindness—or not-quite-coherently attempted causality-blindness—is profoundly unsatisfying, since any sophisticated advocate of structural explanations can grant the statistics and hold that the differences would vanish in a different society. He calls plea bargaining a perversion of justice and Murray's confidence that the authorities are trying to get it right a blindness bordering on complicity, drawing on his own experience of psychiatric detention (Chapter 9). His verdict is that his impression of the book is favorable but restrained.

By 2023 and 2024 the language had hardened. The unpublished sequel to the memoir recalls the Murray review as having set out the technical reasons for believing that the gap is real and partly genetic, while distinguishing it from the position of a hereditarian blogger who held that anyone denying a genetic cause was a mind-killed idiot (unpublished draft, "Guess I'll Die", dated 2023). The last published installment of the memoir goes further, saying of the community's response to the Times that instead of pointing out that being a racist like Charles Murray is the obviously correct position that sensible people will tend to reach in the course of being sensible, we disingenuously deny everything ("Agreeing With Stalin in Ways That Exhibit Generally Rationalist Principles," UUT, 2 Mar 2024). The label is the newspaper's, used with irony, and the position is Murray's with the standard caveats, which the post lists, so that "obviously correct" may describe where sensible reasoning tends to arrive as much as the strength of the evidence. The content includes the genetic claim about the American gap: a few paragraphs earlier, granting that the gap could be environmental, the post asks in a footnote, with no odds named, how much do you want to bet on that? In four years, then, a claim offered in 2020 as "probably part of the explanation," by an author who said his assessment had little value, came to be presented as what sensible people reach. The passages examined contain no new argument in between, and the disclaimer of expertise does not recur. The change is at least one of register, a more belligerent sentence, and the footnote suggests that it is also one of credence.

The literature does not support the stronger claim. That a gap in tested IQ exists is not in dispute among mainstream researchers; Eric Turkheimer, Kathryn Paige Harden and Richard Nisbett, replying in 2017 to Murray and Sam Harris, accept that it exists, that IQ predicts outcomes and that it is partly heritable, and reject the inference that the gap must therefore be partly genetic: We believe there is currently no strong evidence to support this conclusion, whereas Murray presents it as a near certainty (Turkheimer, Harden & Nisbett 2017). The 2012 review of intelligence research by Nisbett and six coauthors, published in American Psychologist, repeated the American Psychological Association task force's verdict of 1996, “There is not much direct evidence on this point, but what there is fails to support a genetic hypothesis.” That conclusion stands today: There has been no new direct evidence on the question. (Nisbett et al. 2012). The direct evidence it cites is of two kinds: studies of children of mixed ancestry, of which the review says, of one of them, that European genes were of no advantage to this group (Eyferth's study of children fathered by black and white American soldiers in postwar Germany; Moore's adoption study of 1986), and the narrowing of the gap, by 5.5 IQ points between 1972 and 2002 in William Dickens and James Flynn's analysis of standardization samples, a figure that Philippe Rushton and Arthur Jensen contested. The designs, samples and ages of the mixed-ancestry studies limit what they can show, and the review's characterization of them is reported here as its own.

The arguments Davis rehearses are contested in this literature, and each has hereditarian replies, on the measurement of the Flynn gains and on the transracial adoption studies among other points, that were not examined for this study. The constraint from within-group heritability depends on assuming that the environmental factors that differ between groups act like those that vary within them; the Flynn effect, which Turkheimer and his coauthors put at about 18 points in American scores between 1948 and 2002, shows that environmental change can move a highly heritable trait by more than the size of the gap, and they cite adoption into better-off homes as raising IQ by 12 to 18 points. The one genomic test read for this study did not support a genetic explanation, though Murray's book and Davis's review expected such methods to settle the question. Kevin Bird's 2021 test, using polygenic scores for educational attainment in African and European samples, found no evidence of diversifying selection when within-family effect sizes were used, and estimated that under a neutral model the expected genetic contribution to a difference was much smaller than hereditarians propose (Bird 2021). Bird also notes how poorly such scores, built from samples of European ancestry, predict outcomes in people of African ancestry, which makes them unreliable for comparisons between those groups in either direction. The paper addresses the hereditarian argument from natural selection; it is not an estimate of every possible genetic contribution to the gap.

The hereditarian case is mostly indirect. Its most general form, in Bo Winegard, Ben Winegard and Jonathan Anomaly's "Dodging Darwin" (2020), is that populations which evolved in different environments differ in many traits and that it would be surprising if cognition were the exception, so that a partial genetic hypothesis is the one most consistent with Darwinism. Davis's version is the same: We could certainly imagine that none of the relevant selection pressures on humans happened to touch the brain—but why? Wouldn't that be kind of a weird coincidence? The argument makes a difference of some size more plausible than none, which few would deny. It does not establish the direction or the size of a difference in a particular trait between two particular populations, or distinguish a genetic account of the American gap from its rivals, and Bird's result is evidence that for cognitive performance the neutral expectation is small.

The study's verdict, as of the sources read, the latest of them from 2021, is that the cause of the gap is unknown. The direct evidence that the environmentalist reviews cite is old and limited, as the reviews themselves say; the one genomic test read does not support the argument from natural selection on which the recent hereditarian case most depends; and the hereditarian primary literature, including the arguments from admixture and the structure of the tests assembled by Rushton and Jensen in 2005, was not examined and is known here only through its critics. On that basis a genetic contribution of some size is neither established nor excluded. "Probably partly genetic" is a probability judgment the evidence read here neither establishes nor excludes. Held as loosely as Davis held it in 2020, with a disclaimer of expertise, it claims little that this evidence refutes, though the chapter has not reconstructed what would make "probably" the right word, and the disclaimer limits the claim to authority without supplying grounds. As a statement of credence, "obviously correct" is not defensible on this evidence. Davis's own best methodological remark tells against his later confidence: the unpublished Facing Reality review insists that the difference between a statistical and a causal model is the whole question, and the hereditarian claim is a causal claim of exactly the kind that the existing statistics do not identify. His draft on COVID-19 outcomes shows the more careful habit. Against a journalist who wrote that there is nothing about being black that affects susceptibility, it argues only that the claim is too strong, granting that structural factors are almost certainly going to be a significant part the explanation and proposing a specific biological candidate, vitamin D and skin pigmentation, that could be tested (unpublished draft, "The Causality of Ethnic Differences in COVID-19 Outcomes Is an Open Empirical Question", dated 2021), which is the posture the evidence permits on intelligence as well. The verdict also bears on §3. If on race the evidence does not settle the question, the hedged egalitarian belief that there is no good evidence of a genetic cause may be held on the evidence, and for this case the Schelling-point theory has less to explain than Davis supposed; the theory's explanandum is better secured for psychological sex differences, which Chapter 3 treats.

6. The charge against Alexander

The New York Times article of February 2021 on Alexander's blog, by Cade Metz (whose dealings with Davis are the subject of Chapter 7), said that Alexander had aligned himself with Charles Murray, in the phrase Davis and Alexander both quote, in a post that in fact mentioned Murray only as a fellow skeptic that poor workers could be retrained, and supported a basic income. Alexander called this a weirdly brazen type of falsehood for a major newspaper, describing the article as implying that he agreed with Murray's offensive views on race (Alexander 2021). Davis agreed that the inference was invalid and objected to the reply: in invalidating the Times's charge of guilt by association with Murray, Alexander validates Murray's guilt. He argued from Alexander's published writing, a review accepting twin-study evidence and an essay accepting a genetic explanation of Ashkenazi intelligence, that Alexander probably finds genetic group differences plausible, and concluded that the Times had made a "Gettier attack": the charge is essentially correct, even though the evidence used to prosecute the charge before a jury of distracted New York Times readers is completely bogus.

Davis's sentence joins two claims. The factual one, that Alexander privately or implicitly holds views closer to Murray's than his public statement suggested, is supported by the essays Davis cites and by the content of a 2014 email from Alexander that Topher Brennan published a few days after the article, which Davis summarizes as saying that biologically mediated group differences are probably real. (The email was private correspondence published without consent; this study relies only on Davis's description of it.) The evaluative claim is that the charge was therefore "essentially correct." The insinuation, as Davis puts it, was that Alexander is a racist like Charles Murray, and the italicized qualifier does some of the work of a definition: the charge is to hold Murray's views, which Davis thinks Alexander does and thinks correct. What the sentence leaves in place is the condemnation the word ordinarily carries, which Davis rejects for this content but which a reader of the newspaper would supply. Calling the charge "essentially correct" concedes to such a reader more than Davis believes. The category is the newspaper's, and Davis does not draw it; what he does is grant that it applies, without restating what it would have to mean for the grant to be true. The fault is one of communicative effect in the terms of Chapter 5, a sentence whose likely effect on its readers is a belief its author does not hold. It resembles the noncentral fallacy, which Davis argued that Alexander's essay on categories endorses in the direction Alexander favored ("A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning," UUT, 15 Jul 2023): a person who accepts a psychometric estimate is a noncentral member of the category racist, and affirming the label without the definition lends him the attributes of the central members. Elsewhere Davis made the definition explicit. The memoir's account of a Facebook comment he wrote that week says that the charge that Alexander and his readers were "crypto-sexists" was absolutely correct if "sexism" included "It's an empirical question whether innate statistical psychological sex differences of some magnitude exist, it empirically looks like they do, and this has implications about our social world", and that conditional states the definition and leaves the reader free to reject it. The Gettier sentence states its definition only by italics.

The section of the memoir in which these passages appear makes a broader claim about motive: that in conflict with progressives, the defensive strategy of the "rationalists" is one of deception, its leaders privately believing what they publicly deny. The attribution problem of Chapter 5 applies, and the leaked email, as Davis describes it, is the nearest thing to counterfactual evidence, though it was written in 2014 and the statement it would contradict in 2021. Whether the public statement deceived is a question for the functional test of Chapter 5, which Chapter 7 applied to Yudkowsky's statements. Alexander wrote that the article heavily implies this means I agree with Murray’s offensive views on race, recalled that he had once noted that Murray had offensive views on race, and called the implication a falsehood; he did not say which of Murray's views he meant. A reader of the statement would conclude that Alexander does not share Murray's views on race. If the essays and the 2014 email are read as Davis reads them, that conclusion is false, and the sentence that produced it was true only on a reading of "offensive views" that the reader was not given, which is the equivocation this section finds in Davis's sentence, run in the other direction. What the email cannot show is a motive: a person may find a hypothesis plausible, think it right to say that a newspaper's inference was invalid, and prefer that readers not learn what he thinks. Davis applied the accusation to himself in the next breath: I fear my love of Truth is not so great that if I didn't have Something to Protect, I would have happily participated in the cover-up. The sentence concedes that his own departure from the community's strategy was driven by his particular stake, which the Murray review had also said, and it is the kind of disclosure the study has credited throughout. It places Davis's own possible conduct under the same criticism, without settling whether the others deceived their readers: interests and judgments of risk could explain either misleading speech or a defensible refusal to discuss a subject, and which it was turns on what the public statements communicated and what evidence supports attributing a contrary belief to their authors.

7. Conclusion

Davis's political writing makes one durable contribution, the theory that egalitarian belief is a coordination point that protects against group conflict, which takes the orthodoxy seriously on its own terms and turns a quarrel about facts into a choice between equilibria. The Murray review makes that choice in the open and states its costs and its author's stake. Its limit is a question it does not ask: whether a rule about treatment that leaves belief free would serve better than either equilibrium, which Davis's own argument about salient lines points to, and whether such a fence can stand without the belief that seemed to support it, which only his unpublished Facing Reality review asks. On race and intelligence the thesis frame decides nothing; the verdict rests on the literature read. His treatment of Alexander shows a related inconsistency, within one installment about one week of 2021, between a stated conditional and a definition given only by italics.

The disclosure in the triskaidekaphobia footnote is the passage to set against these. There Davis said that his refusal to bargain with offense was a disposition and not a calculation, and told readers to discount his arguments for it until he had done the mathematics. The parts of the political writing that fall short of that standard are the parts where the stance outran the argument.
