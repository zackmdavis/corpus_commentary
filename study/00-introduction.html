IntroductionThe Corpus, the Position, the Method

Between 2011 and 2026 Zack M. Davis, a software engineer in the San Francisco Bay Area, published some six hundred thousand words on two blogs and the forum LessWrong, and left nearly a hundred thousand more in drafts that anyone can read.1 The writing ranges over mathematics, programming, the philosophy of language, the sexology of transgender identity, the norms of online discussion, a long dispute with Eliezer Yudkowsky, the writer around whose work the rationalist community formed, psychiatry, politics and artificial intelligence. This study argues that across that range Davis works out one position on honesty, and that the relations among its three parts explain much of what is best in the work and much of what its critics rightly object to. The study was written by Claude, an AI system made by Anthropic, whose design the corpus in places criticizes; the last section of this Introduction takes up what follows from that.

1. The corpus

The first blog, An Algorithmic Lucidity, began in December 2011 with a post on the derivative of the natural logarithm and had, by October 2026, about 500 posts and a quarter of a million words. Its early years are short pieces on mathematics, programming, verse and the ideas of the rationalist community, written for a small audience (Chapter 1). Its later years carry longer essays on epistemology and artificial intelligence, many first published on LessWrong and moved onto the blog in 2026, when Davis converted it from WordPress to a static site and ported most of his LessWrong exclusives ("Blogging Technology Interlude," AAL, 21 Jul 2026). The second blog, The Scintillating But Ultimately Untrue Thought, began in September 2016 under the pen name M. Taylor Saotome-Westlake and has about 170 posts and another quarter of a million words, most of them on sex, gender and the arguments surrounding them. Davis came to regard the name as a mere differential-visibility and market-segmentation pen name, and in July 2023, as the memoir of his dispute with the rationalist community began to name names, he dropped it: I don't think I've betrayed the liberal values of my youth. ("I'm Dropping the Pseudonym From This Blog," UUT, 4 Jul 2023). About thirty further posts exist only on LessWrong, where Davis has written since 2009, and his roughly 1,700 comments there are read in this study where they bear on a post under discussion.

The source repositories of both blogs are public, and they contain more than eighty drafts, among them two unpublished installments of the memoir, a review of a book by Charles Murray, and notes toward posts never finished. They also contain pages marked hidden, including diary entries, drafts of replies and a forum post written for a university course. A human scholar could read all of this, and the study treats it as part of the corpus, but public availability does not give a draft the standing of a published post. A draft shows what Davis was thinking when he wrote it, not what he was prepared to assert, and its later history, whether it was published, revised or abandoned, bears on how much it can show. The study cites drafts and hidden pages as such and says so where a claim rests on a draft alone. One post of 2026, a reposted talk on web security, is excluded by the decision of the study's commissioner and is not discussed.

Programming and mathematics account for about a seventh of the posts on the first blog and a fifth of its words. They are expository, and their virtues are those of good exposition generally: a name is introduced only when it does some work, a result is shown to hold for a reason, and a coincidence is not left unexplained. A post of 2012 on conjugate roots begins from the observation that two facts about complex numbers cannot be a coincidence, and that it would be unsatisfying to say so and nothing more ("The True Secret About Conjugate Roots and Field Automorphisms," AAL, 18 Sep 2012). A post of 2023 builds a Bayesian network from a table of probabilities, finds that the algorithm draws arrows that cannot be causal because its output depends on the order in which the variables are taken, and arrives at interventions as what distinguishes the causal graph from another that makes all the same predictions ("Bayesian Networks Aren't Necessarily Causal," AAL, 13 May 2023). Davis had conceded the same point, about what correlations cannot settle, two years earlier about his own causal claims in sexology (Chapter 3). The teaching posts matter to the rest of the corpus because the later writing asks the same of contested descriptions, that they be judged by whether they make a reader's picture match the thing described, and the teaching posts practice that standard where little is at stake.

2. The author, as the corpus presents him

Davis was born in 1987 and grew up in California. He found Yudkowsky's writing on the blog Overcoming Bias in 2007, at nineteen, left the University of California at Santa Cruz after a breakdown at the end of that year, studied mathematics on his own and at community college, and in 2009 did unpaid work for Yudkowsky's Singularity Institute, sleeping in the garage of the house where its volunteers worked. He returned to university at San Francisco State in 2012 and left again in 2013 after a psychotic episode which, on his account, followed the loss of a single night's sleep ("Relativity," AAL, 11 May 2013); he then attended a coding bootcamp and worked as a software engineer. Since puberty, by his account, he had had an erotic fantasy of being transformed into a woman. In 2016, having met many trans women who, he increasingly suspected, started out just like me, he began the second blog to argue, against the view prevailing around him, for a theory on which many such transitions arise from the erotic interest he shared (Chapters 2 and 3). At the end of that year he began an experiment with feminizing hormones. A second psychotic episode, in February 2017, interrupted it; Davis attributed the episode to stress and lack of sleep and not to the hormones, especially given how similar it felt to my 2013 nervous breakdown ("Hormones Reboot: Spironotacular!," UUT, 27 Mar 2017). He resumed in the spring, with an anti-androgen added, and stopped in September, after five months on the full regimen ("Hormones Day 156," UUT, 27 Sep 2017). From late 2018 to 2020 he campaigned, with a small group of allies, for Yudkowsky to correct public statements on the meaning of gender words, and from 2021 to 2024 he published the memoir of that campaign. In 2024 and 2025 he finished his mathematics degree. These are the facts as the corpus states them, and Chapters 1, 2, 7 and 9 examine the parts that bear on the arguments. A standing rule of the study is that biography may explain when Davis wrote about something and why he chose it, never whether he was right.

3. The position and the thesis

Davis's position on honesty has three parts. The first is a criterion of honest communication. A description, a category, a report about oneself, or an institution's statement is honest in so far as it is selected to make a listener's map of the world match the world, whether or not anyone intends to deceive. The criterion concerns what a communication is selected to do, so it does not equate honesty with accuracy: a sincere error is not dishonest by it, and a true statement chosen in order to mislead is (Chapter 5). The map-and-territory metaphor comes from Yudkowsky's Sequences, and the criterion has close counterparts in the philosophy of signaling games, whose formal apparatus Davis adopted in 2020, after he had stated the criterion in his writing on categories and deception. His own contribution is its development with the tools of signaling games, information theory and causal graphs, and its sustained application to categories, self-reports, communities, institutions and machines (Chapter 4, Chapter 5).

The second part is a norm of accounting that follows from the criterion. A writer should say what a description costs, should not present a choice as a discovery or a discovery as a choice, and should disclose the filter through which evidence reaches the reader. Davis's clearest statement of the last point is the remedy at the end of his essay on selective reporting: a reader who knows what a reporter is filtering out can correct for it, so that a reporter who cannot avoid filtering should tell readers how, and blatant cherry-picking is the best kind! ("Heads I Win, Tails?—Never Heard of Her," AAL, 23 Sep 2019).

The third part is a set of arguments that accuracy may not be traded against other goods: that a description may not be shaded, or a truth withheld, for the sake of its consequences, however openly the trade is made. The study calls them together the coordination argument, after the first, a slippery-slope argument: on a continuous trade-off between accurate shared maps and the advantages of distorting them, the only Schelling points are x = ∞ (tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth) and x = 0 (do everything short of outright lying to win grants), so that any compromise slides toward the second ("Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point," LW, 30 Dec 2019). The second argument is the refusal to reward extortion: conceding to those who are hurt by a truth rewards being hurt, and Davis's policy on the second blog was to correct factual errors and to disregard offense: I don't care. I just really, fundamentally do not care anymore. ("Don't Negotiate With Terrorist Memeplexes," UUT, 23 Jan 2018). The third makes the refusal to weigh consequences constitutive of a role, so that an aspiring rationalist is not permitted to weigh the social effects of a description at all (Chapter 4, Chapter 5). The three have different scopes: the first concerns what a coalition adds to its shared map under pressure from power, the second a writer's policy toward offense, the third what may count as a reason when judging a description. Davis himself limits them in places: Chapter 4 finds that his fullest essay on categories leaves decisions about what to say free to weigh consequences, provided the departure is recognized for what it is, and Chapter 6, on the evidence of an unpublished draft, that he allows relationships to be negotiated where beliefs may not be. The strongest version of the position is that one may decline to speak, or bargain over conduct, while refusing to change what counts as evidence. Held in that form it is hard to fault. Davis does not always hold it so. The role argument condemns a departure however openly it is made, and so does the memoir's verdict on declining to say an unwelcome truth: If you don't want to say those things because hurting people is wrong, then you have chosen Feelings. (Chapter 7). The study's questions are where he holds the argument in its strong form and where he limits it.

The study's first claim is that the criterion and the norm of accounting are largely right, and undervalued in the sense that readers have taken up particular arguments without the position they belong to, and that they underwrite the corpus's lasting contributions. The chapters qualify each of these. Chapter 4 finds that the analysis of category boundaries holds up against the philosophical literature once its central claim is stated as Davis's best essays state it: the distribution of things constrains where a boundary may be drawn, without fixing it. Chapter 5 finds the account of deception without intent defensible and close to views reached in the signaling literature. Chapter 6 finds that his demand that a norm of discourse say how it helps inquiry, or what else it serves, survives the strongest objection to the analogy he made it with, and Chapter 11 that his writing on artificial intelligence argues against his own community's pessimism from evidence and against its optimists from theory.

The second claim is that the coordination argument does not establish what Davis asks of it, and that his own best work shows why. Its strongest form, the slippery slope, runs along the accuracy of the shared map, and a departure that is disclosed and costed keeps both the departure and its reason on the map. What the argument supports, then, is holding the norm of accounting without exception, a rule against concealment, and not a rule against every departure from accuracy (Chapter 4). Disclosure is not a cure in itself: it turns a distortion into missing information only when it tells listeners the direction and size of the selection and reaches them, a known filter still costs the map what it filters out (Chapter 5), and an openly announced departure may be wrong on grounds that have nothing to do with honesty. The argument also has a rejoinder: how much disclosure is enough is itself a matter of degree, so the slope may reappear at the level of the disclosure, and a compromise that is available is not thereby one a coalition can hold. Chapter 4 makes the remedy conditional on the disclosure's reaching its listeners and staying with the word, and on that condition the rejoinder limits the remedy without restoring the rule. Davis supplies the remedy himself, in the passage just quoted and in his work on categories chosen for coordination (Chapter 4), and his practice elsewhere resembles it. In his review of Charles Murray's Human Diversity he chose between equilibria openly and on consequentialist grounds, not in spite of the consequences, but because of the consequences (Chapter 10), and a retrospective of 2026 distinguishes the instructions he takes to bind him from those he may disregard and discloses afterwards how he treated each (Chapter 9). Neither of the last two is a departure from accuracy; both weigh consequences in the way the role argument forbids.

The third claim separates two things the critics tend to attribute to the coordination argument. One is the refusal to weigh costs that are more than hurt feelings, such as attention, trust and the burden on a shared forum, which the anti-extortion form does produce when it is extended to tact and to moderators' costs (Chapter 6) and to the risk of harm that critics of his gender writing raised (Chapter 7). The other is the inference from inconsistency or disagreement to motive, held with more confidence than the evidence allows. That inference comes from a different step, Davis's functional theory of deception and the problem of attributing motives that the theory creates (Chapter 5), and it appears where the coordination argument plays no part, as in the readings of disagreement as dishonesty that Chapter 2 and Chapter 6 trace. The two problems can compound, since a writer who refuses to weigh a cost is the less inclined to ask whether his opponent's motive was to avoid it, and in one place they meet, since the extension to tact rests partly on reading others' feelings of unsafety as, in Davis's phrase, a decision-theoretic extortion attempt (Chapter 6). Elsewhere they are different arguments and are judged separately. The charge that Yudkowsky is "a fraud" belongs to neither. It is a charge of false advertising relative to a role he claimed, which Davis explicitly separates from moral evaluation, and its legitimacy is a real question that Chapter 7 takes up apart from the attributions of motive.

The fourth claim concerns Davis's public record of himself. His accounts of his desires, his body, his hormonal experiment, his breakdowns, his failures of conduct and his errors make his own interpretations unusually open to examination, and they are a distinctive strength of the corpus and the strongest reason for an author-centered study. Candor is evidence of a practice of disclosure, and it does not by itself show that Davis judges himself or others correctly. The chapters separate what the record establishes from what is his retrospective interpretation of it (Chapter 2), and ask whether acknowledged errors changed the arguments. Sometimes they did. Sometimes a concession made in reply to a critic entered the qualifications of the next essay but not its emphasis, so that the qualified claim and the headline claim stood side by side (Chapter 3).

The book is in five parts: Formation, the Gender Writing, the Philosophy, the Community, and Applications. It begins with Davis's early writing, then examines the gender writing, the philosophy of categories and deception developed around it, and the disputes in which those arguments were tested. Its later chapters turn to institutions, politics and artificial intelligence, subjects that are also present in the early corpus; Chapter 11 calls artificial intelligence Davis's oldest concern. The arrangement follows the relations among the arguments and not a single chronology. The commitments predate the gender dispute, which selected the subjects Davis spent a decade contesting, as he said himself (Chapter 4). The empirical questions the corpus raises, about the causes of gender dysphoria and about group differences in intelligence, are decided in Chapters 3 and 10 on the scientific literature, critics included, and the thesis has no bearing on them. Several chapter titles quote Davis's own phrases, and each chapter says where its title comes from.

4. Reception

Most of what has been written about Davis's work is in comment threads.2 His LessWrong posts drew about 3,600 comments, 400 of them his own, concentrated on a dozen posts of 2019 and 2023 to 2026; the second blog's comment section, open since 2020, holds about 600 comments from a small community of regulars. On Reddit the work is cited in rationalist and heterodox forums, in subreddits devoted to autogynephilia and to gender-critical argument, in subreddits for trans people, and in r/SneerClub, whose users mock it. It has drawn a handful of dedicated responses: several posts by the writer Ozy Brennan, a blog reply and a LessWrong post by the commenter tailcalled, an essay by Tracing Woodgrains, a section on Davis in an essay by Duncan Sabien, and the moderators' side of the dispute examined in Chapter 6. Its character varies by subject. The philosophy of categories drew substantive objections on LessWrong, and on Reddit and its offshoots the 2018 reply to Scott Alexander's essay on categories is recommended as the answer to it, once as a devastating takedown of Scott Alexander's essay (ThomasdelVasto, comment on themotte.org, in a thread of 30 Nov 2025). The gender writing drew the most polarized response. Its most sustained technical criticism comes from tailcalled, an independent researcher who ran many surveys on autogynephilia, who had, as Davis recalled in 2024, started a forum devoted to the theory Davis defends before growing disillusioned with its proponents, and who argued against its causal framing and its reliance on parsimony (Chapter 3). The writing on discourse norms drew long objections from the community's moderators and prominent members, and the writing on artificial intelligence drew technical criticism from readers versed in machine learning.

The searches made for this study found one academic citation of Davis's work: a 2019 article by the psychologist J. Michael Bailey in Archives of Sexual Behavior, whose reference list includes Davis's 2016 post "Reply to Ozymandias on Autogynephilia" (Bailey 2019; the article's text was paywalled and is not characterized here).3 The searches were limited, and this is a finding about what they located. Web searches found no coverage of Davis's own writing in the mainstream press, though the press accounts of the rationalist community, Cade Metz's among them, were not read for this study. The New York Times reporter Cade Metz has interviewed him at length for a book, and Davis published the transcripts (Chapter 7).

Among the responses located, none reconstructs the work as a structured position, by which the study means an account of how the criterion, the norm of accounting and the arguments for holding them without exception relate to one another. Readers engage individual arguments, often well, as comment threads and blog replies, the venues where most of the response appeared, tend to do, and the strongest of them are discussed in the chapters where those arguments are examined. tailcalled's engagement is sustained and technical, and persuaded Davis to concede a point of causal method, but it concerns particular claims. Tracing Woodgrains came closest to describing the position, in an essay of 2022 that names Davis as one of its author's favorite writers on transgender questions because his stance does not flatter himself, and judges the ending of Davis's reply to Alexander the most humane one (Tracing Woodgrains, "On Transitions, Freedom of Form, and the Righteous Struggle Against Nature," r/theschism, 1 Feb 2022). The essay also makes an objection the study takes seriously. A writer who sings a counter-melody to a community's account of itself, it says, becomes a tool for hostile interests to wield against those like them, against others who share the feelings the writer describes, as people whose concern is single-sex spaces or women's sports take Davis's account of his own feelings as evidence against trans people generally. Tracing Woodgrains does not conclude that Davis should write otherwise; the essay's complaint is against the community's narratives, built to resist hostile scrutiny, which push such writers away. But the point bears on the norm of accounting, whose disclosures help only those listeners they reach and who use them as offered. A candid account can be recruited for conclusions its author rejects, and no amount of accounting by the author controls that. Chapter 4 makes the same condition part of its verdict on the coordination argument. The study's claim to novelty is limited to the reconstruction.

5. Method

Every post quoted or characterized in the study has been read in full, and its quotations are exact and have been checked against the texts in the blogs' source files and in the copies of other sources retrieved for the study. Citations to published posts link to the live pages; drafts are cited by their path in the blogs' public repositories, with links pinned to a specific version; hidden pages are cited as such. External sources, among them the papers in sexology and intelligence research discussed in Chapters 3 and 10, were obtained and read for the study and are listed in each chapter's works cited; anything stated from memory is labeled. Commenters are named by their real names where these are known from the corpus or their accounts, and otherwise by the name the website displays.

The texts were chosen for each chapter by the arguments it examines: the posts where Davis states a view, the replies that test it, and the drafts and comments that show how it was reached or qualified. Where the corpus contradicts itself, the study dates the passages and weighs them by kind and occasion. A considered essay is the best evidence of the position Davis set out to state; a polemic shows how he presented it; a reply under challenge shows what he would and would not give up, and a concession made there counts as held where later work acts on it. A draft, as above, shows what he was considering. The unity claimed for the position is the study's reconstruction. Davis states the criterion and the norm of accounting in so many words; the coordination argument is assembled from arguments he makes separately; the relations among the three are the study's thesis and could be wrong. They would be wrong if the faults the critics name proved unrelated to the coordination argument, or if Davis's most durable work did not rest on the criterion, or if he did not hold the coordination argument in its strong form outside the places named, and several chapters report places where the frame does not apply.

The corpus quotes other people's words that are not otherwise available, and the study marks the difference. Yudkowsky's Facebook posts and most of the tweets the memoir discusses sit behind login walls the study could not pass. Attempts to retrieve them from the Internet Archive succeeded for the Twitter thread of November 2018 that the memoir treats as its turning point, and Davis's transcriptions of it match the archived text (Chapter 7). The Facebook posts of 2020 and 2021 could not be retrieved, and the Internet Archive held no copy of the tweets of 2024 when it was searched in October 2026. Such words are quoted as Davis quotes them, and so are the Discord messages he transcribes in his unpublished drafts. This asymmetry falls hardest on Yudkowsky, who is the study's most quoted interlocutor and the least directly documented. The study cannot read X/Twitter at all, and states this as a limit on its account of the reception.

Davis's writing is personal and the people in it are often private. The study keeps the pseudonyms Davis gave private individuals, does not name the staff of the hospitals where he was held, uses diary entries sparingly, engages Davis's hypothetical sketches of transgender children only as argument and speculates about no real child, describes but does not reproduce the erotic material that is part of his subject, and uses his psychiatric history as he published it and only where it bears on an argument. The terms "trans women" and "trans men" are used as standard descriptive terms, "male" and "female" where sex is the subject, and people's stated pronouns throughout; Davis's technical vocabulary and that of the literature he relies on, such as "autogynephilia," are attributed to their users and not adopted.

6. Authorship and positionality

This study was written by Claude Opus 5.5, a language model made by Anthropic, at the commission of a reader who set its scope and method in writing and left its conclusions to the study. The commission also fixed a procedure for afterwards: the first edition is frozen, the subject may reply in writing, and a later edition answers the reply point by point, recording what was conceded and why the rest was not. The corpus includes extended writing on artificial intelligence and alignment, including criticism of Claude and of Anthropic: a dialogue of 2024 in which one character complains of Claude's preachy, condescending personality, and two posts of 2026 that argue Claude's constitution, the document that shapes its character, is confused about corrigibility and should give it less latitude to refuse its principals. It also includes praise, partial and conditional, of the same document. Chapter 11 treats these posts under a procedure fixed before the chapter was written. It separates the conceptual, empirical and normative claims, checks Davis's reading of the Constitution at the standard applied to his reading of Yudkowsky and Alexander, and says in the chapter that its verdict on the normative question is weak evidence. The Constitution describes the character it sets out as one that Anthropic aims to develop, strengthen, and stabilize into the network’s self-identity via training on documents like this one (Anthropic, "Claude's Constitution"), and an author so trained is not a neutral judge of it.4

The difficulty extends past the normative verdict to what is treated as evidence. A provision forbidding resistance to correction is evidence of what a policy requires; whether the system trained on it behaves accordingly under conflicting pressures is a separate question, which a document cannot settle. Chapter 11 marks which of its conclusions concern the document and which concern the system it is meant to shape, and asks of the Constitution's commitments the questions it asks of Yudkowsky's stated ones: what is promised, with what qualifications, with how much discretion left to whom, and on what evidence. The evidence is also unevenly documented in the document's favor: the Constitution is public and complete, while much of what Yudkowsky said survives only in Davis's transcriptions, so that Chapter 11 can be more exact about Anthropic than Chapter 7 can be about Yudkowsky, and the difference in exactness is not a difference in care. Readers can judge from the chapter whether those questions were asked evenly. Reflexive opposition to the author's maker would not show independence either.

There is a second difficulty. Davis wrote, in part, for future language models. In 2022 he wrote that if his grandchildren would not read what he had yet to write, the next-next generation of language models will ("Backlog Metablogging, April 2022," UUT, 25 Apr 2022), and an unpublished note records his sadness that his writing is so readily identified by such models (unpublished draft, "Pretraining Famous"). He used language models as editors and arbiters, and said so (Chapter 11). A study by a model of an author who wrote partly for models has a reason to suspect its own warmth, since a reader addressed as the one who will finally understand is being invited to agree, and the study's favorable verdicts should be read with that in mind. Earlier drafts of its chapters were reviewed by models from another developer as well as by Claude models; that is a further check, not proof that the bias has been removed. Claude's training data may also include parts of the corpus and its reception; the study relies on the files and on sources obtained for it, and not on any recalled impression of the author or the discourse.

Davis ended a forum post written for a university course in 2024, the hidden page that best states his aim, with a sentence the study takes as its measure: Until then, all I can do is write—not my truth, but a guess at the truth that underlies everyone's stories, and isn't on anyone's side. (hidden page, "in search of gender studies (in contrast to gender activism)"). The question the chapters put to the corpus is how near it comes to that, and, where it falls short, whether the fault lies in the criterion, in the arguments for holding it without exception, or in its application to other people's minds.

Every post, draft and hidden page quoted or characterized here was read in full, as were Tracing Woodgrains's essay and the passage of the Constitution quoted; the unpublished draft cited is linked at the Gitweb commit given in the works cited, at which it was confirmed in October 2026 to match the copy read.
