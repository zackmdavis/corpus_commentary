IntroductionThe Corpus, the Position, the Method

Between 2011 and 2026 Zack M. Davis, a software engineer in the San Francisco Bay Area, published some six hundred thousand words on two blogs and the forum LessWrong, and left nearly a hundred thousand more in public drafts. The writing ranges over mathematics, programming, the philosophy of language, the sexology of transgender identity, the norms of online discussion, a long dispute with the founder of the rationalist movement, psychiatry, politics and artificial intelligence. This study argues that across that range Davis works out one position on honesty, and that the position has three parts whose relations explain both what is best in the work and what its critics rightly object to. The Introduction describes the corpus and its author, states the position and the study's thesis about it, reports what others have made of the work, and sets out the method, including the fact that the study was written by Claude, an AI system some of whose design the corpus criticizes.

1. The corpus

The first blog, An Algorithmic Lucidity, began in December 2011 with a post on the derivative of the natural logarithm and runs to the present, about 500 posts and a quarter of a million words. Its early years are short pieces on mathematics, programming, verse and the ideas of the rationalist community, written, as Chapter 1 puts it, for almost no one. Its later years carry longer essays on epistemology and artificial intelligence, many first published on LessWrong and moved onto the blog in 2026, when Davis converted it from WordPress to a static site and ported most of his LessWrong exclusives ("Blogging Technology Interlude," AAL, 21 Jul 2026). The second blog, The Scintillating But Ultimately Untrue Thought, began in September 2016 under the pen name M. Taylor Saotome-Westlake and has about 170 posts and another quarter of a million words, most of them on sex, gender and the arguments surrounding them. Davis treated the pseudonym as a formality, and in July 2023, before the first installments of his memoir, dropped it: I don't think I've betrayed the liberal values of my youth. ("I'm Dropping the Pseudonym From This Blog," UUT, 4 Jul 2023). About thirty further posts exist only on LessWrong, where Davis has written since 2009, and his roughly 1,700 comments there are read in this study where they bear on a post under discussion.

The source repositories of both blogs are public, and they contain some ninety drafts, among them two unpublished installments of the memoir, a review of a book by Charles Murray, and notes toward posts never finished. They also contain pages marked hidden, including diary entries, drafts of replies and a forum post written for a university course. Since anyone can read them, this study treats them as part of the corpus, cites them as unpublished drafts or hidden pages, and does not treat what Davis chose not to publish as equivalent to what he published. One post of 2026, a reposted talk on web security, is excluded by the decision of the study's commissioner and is not discussed.

Programming and mathematics account for about a seventh of the posts on the first blog and a fifth of its words, and they bear on the thesis more than their subjects suggest. They are expository, and their method is the one the rest of the corpus applies to contested subjects: name a thing only if the name earns its keep, show why a result holds and not only that it holds, and refuse to leave a coincidence unexplained. A post of 2012 on conjugate roots begins from the observation that two facts about complex numbers cannot be a coincidence and that it would be unsatisfying to say so and nothing more ("The True Secret About Conjugate Roots and Field Automorphisms," AAL, 18 Sep 2012). A post of 2023 builds a Bayesian network from a table of probabilities, discovers that the algorithm draws arrows that cannot be causal because they depend on the order in which variables are considered, and so arrives at the place of interventions in the meaning of causation ("Bayesian Networks Aren't Necessarily Causal," AAL, 13 May 2023). The lesson is the one Chapter 3 finds Davis learning about his own causal claims in sexology. The teaching posts are where the criterion this study ascribes to him, that a description should be judged by whether it makes a listener's picture match the thing described, is practiced in its least contested form.

2. The author, as the corpus presents him

Davis was born in 1987 and grew up in California. He found the writing of Eliezer Yudkowsky on the blog Overcoming Bias in 2007, at nineteen, left the University of California at Santa Cruz after a breakdown at the end of that year, studied mathematics on his own and at community college, and in 2009 did unpaid work for Yudkowsky's Singularity Institute, sleeping in the garage of the house where its volunteers worked. He returned to San Francisco State University in 2012 and left again in 2013 after a psychotic episode brought on by lost sleep, attended a coding bootcamp, and worked as a software engineer. From the age of fourteen he had fantasized about being a woman. In 2016, when his social circle in the rationalist community came to regard such men as women, he began the second blog to argue that most were not; in 2017 he took feminizing hormones for five months, stopped, and suffered a second psychotic episode in a period of conflict with friends. From late 2018 to 2020 he campaigned, with a small group of allies, for Yudkowsky to correct public statements on the meaning of gender words, and from 2021 to 2024 he published the memoir of that campaign. In 2024 and 2025 he finished his mathematics degree. These are the facts as the corpus states them, and Chapters 1, 2, 7 and 9 examine the parts that bear on the arguments. A standing rule of the study is that biography may explain when Davis wrote about something and why he chose it, never whether he was right.

3. The position and the thesis

Davis's position on honesty has three parts. The first is a criterion. A description, a category, a report about oneself, or an institution's statement is honest in so far as it is selected to make a listener's map of the world match the world, whether or not anyone intends to deceive. The map-and-territory metaphor comes from Yudkowsky's Sequences; what is Davis's own is the criterion of selection, worked out with the philosophy of signaling games, information theory and causal graphs (Chapter 4, Chapter 5). The study calls the whole position "cartographic" for short.

The second part is a norm of accounting that follows from the criterion. A writer should say what a description costs, should not present a choice as a discovery or a discovery as a choice, and should disclose the filter through which evidence reaches the reader. Davis's clearest statement of the last point is the remedy at the end of his essay on selective reporting: a reader who knows what a reporter is filtering out can correct for it, so that a reporter who cannot avoid filtering should tell readers how, and blatant cherry-picking is the best kind! ("Heads I Win, Tails?—Never Heard of Her," AAL, 24 Sep 2019).

The third part is an argument that the norm must be held without exception. It appears in several forms. One is the slippery slope: on a continuous trade-off between accurate shared maps and the advantages of distorting them, the only Schelling points are x = ∞ (tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth) and x = 0 (do everything short of outright lying to win grants) ("Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point," LW, 30 Dec 2019). Another is the refusal to reward extortion: conceding to those who are hurt by a truth rewards being hurt, and Davis's policy on the second blog was that if what he said in trying to say something true was found offensive, I don't care. I just really, fundamentally do not care anymore. ("Don't Negotiate With Terrorist Memeplexes," UUT, 23 Jan 2018). A third makes the norm constitutive of a role, so that an aspiring rationalist is not permitted to weigh the social harm of a description at all (Chapter 5). The study refers to these three parts as (a), (b) and (c).

The thesis has four claims. The first is that (a) and (b) are right, original as a synthesis, and undervalued. They underwrite the corpus's lasting contributions: an analysis of category boundaries that holds up against the philosophical literature on the subject (Chapter 4), an account of deception that needs no appeal to intent, a critique of lists of discourse norms that is right on points its critics did not concede, and writing on artificial intelligence that is undogmatic toward Davis's own community.

The second claim is that (c) fails on Davis's own terms, and that his best work says so. The norm of accounting is itself the mechanism by which a coordination equilibrium can absorb exceptions: a departure that is disclosed and costed is not a defection, and disclosure supplies the salient intermediate point whose absence the slippery-slope argument assumes. Davis reaches this answer himself, in the remedy just quoted, in his later work on categories chosen for coordination (Chapter 4), in his review of Charles Murray's Human Diversity, where he chose between two equilibria openly and on consequentialist grounds, not in spite of the consequences, but because of the consequences (Chapter 10), and in a retrospective of 2026 that distinguishes instructions he should obey from instructions he may decline openly (Chapter 9). Where (c) operates unqualified, it produces what his critics rightly object to: inferences about other people's motives held with more confidence than the evidence allows, the treatment of tact as bad faith, and a refusal to weigh costs that are not merely hurt feelings. Where a chapter finds that (c) is not what drives Davis's practice, it says so.

The third claim is that two things the critics attribute to an exceptionless rule are something else and must be judged separately. The charge that Yudkowsky is "a fraud" is a charge of false advertising relative to a role he claimed, which Davis explicitly separates from moral evaluation; it is the norm of accounting intensified by role, and its legitimacy is a real question. The inference from inconsistency to motive is a separate step, governed by Davis's functional theory of deception and the problem of attributing motives that the theory creates. Chapter 7 keeps the two apart.

The fourth claim is that the corpus's rarest quality is that the criterion is applied to its author. Davis records his desires, his body, his hormonal experiment, his breakdowns, his failures of conduct and his errors in public, at documented cost, with the same evidential seriousness he brings to others, so that the corpus is a sustained experiment in treating oneself as a hypothesis. This is what most justifies a book. Whether the confidence that licensed candor about himself also licensed overconfidence about others' minds is a question the chapters answer case by case.

The chapters follow the order in which the subjects arose. The commitments come first (Chapter 1); the gender writing gave them their occasion (Chapters 2 and 3); the philosophy was developed in that dispute (Chapters 4 and 5); the conflicts with the community followed (Chapters 6 to 8); and the position was then applied to institutions, politics and artificial intelligence (Chapters 9 to 11). The order is chronological and not causal. The commitments predate the gender dispute, which selected the errors Davis spent a decade correcting, as he eventually said himself (Chapter 2). The empirical questions the corpus raises, about the causes of gender dysphoria and about group differences in intelligence, are decided in Chapters 3 and 10 on the scientific literature, critics included, and not on the thesis, which does not apply to them. Several chapter titles quote Davis's own phrases, and each chapter says where its title comes from.

4. Reception

Most of what has been written about Davis's work is in comment threads. His LessWrong posts drew some 3,600 comments, 400 of them his own, concentrated on a dozen posts of 2019 and 2023 to 2026; the second blog's comment section, open since 2020, holds about 600 comments from a small community of regulars. Outside those venues the work is cited on Reddit, in communities ranging from the rationalist and heterodox to the gender-critical, the transgender and the hostile, and it has drawn a handful of dedicated responses: several posts by the writer Ozy Brennan, a blog reply and a LessWrong post by the commenter tailcalled, an essay by Tracing Woodgrains, an essay by Duncan Sabien, and the moderators' side of the dispute examined in Chapter 6. Its character varies by subject. The philosophy of categories drew substantive engagement on LessWrong and praise elsewhere as the definitive reply to Scott Alexander's essay on categories. The gender writing drew the most polarized response, with its most technical criticism coming from readers sympathetic to its sexology. The writing on discourse norms drew long objections from the community's moderators and prominent members, and the writing on artificial intelligence drew technical criticism from readers versed in machine learning.

Academic notice is almost absent. A search of the Crossref database for Davis's names and domains found nothing; a search of Semantic Scholar could not be completed because the service refused the requests; and a web search found one citation, in a 2019 article by the psychologist J. Michael Bailey in Archives of Sexual Behavior, whose reference list includes Davis's 2016 post "Reply to Ozymandias on Autogynephilia" (Bailey 2019; the article's text was paywalled and is not characterized here). No coverage of Davis's own writing in the mainstream press was found. The New York Times reporter Cade Metz has interviewed him at length for a book, and Davis published the transcripts (Chapter 7).

A finding of the reception survey is that no reader, hostile or friendly, has treated the work as a structured philosophical position. Readers engage individual arguments, often well, and the strongest of them are discussed in the chapters where those arguments are examined. Two partial exceptions test the finding. tailcalled has engaged the sexology and the philosophy of causation more technically than anyone, and persuaded Davis to concede a point of causal method (Chapter 3), but the engagement is with particular claims. Tracing Woodgrains came closest to naming the position, in an essay of 2022 that names Davis as one of its author's favorite writers on transgender questions because his stance does not flatter himself, praises the ending of Davis's reply to Alexander as the most humane one, and warns that a writer who sings a counter-melody becomes a tool for hostile interests to wield against those like them (Tracing Woodgrains, "On Transitions, Freedom of Form, and the Righteous Struggle Against Nature," r/theschism, 1 Feb 2022). That is a recognition of the self-application and the ethic of the fourth claim, made without reconstructing the theory behind it. The study's claim to novelty is limited to the reconstruction.

5. Method

Every post quoted or characterized in the study was read in full by the session that wrote the passage, and the reading is logged. Quotations are exact, and a script checks each one against the source files before publication. Citations to published posts link to the live pages; drafts are cited by their path in the blogs' public repositories, with links pinned to a specific version; hidden pages are cited as such. External sources, among them the papers in sexology and intelligence research discussed in Chapters 3 and 10, were fetched and read in the course of the study and are listed in each chapter; anything cited from memory is labeled. Commenters are named by their real names where these are known from the corpus or their accounts, and otherwise by the name the website displays.

The corpus quotes other people's words that are not otherwise available, and the study marks the difference. Yudkowsky's Facebook posts and most of the tweets the memoir discusses sit behind login walls the study could not pass. Attempts to retrieve them from the Internet Archive succeeded for the Twitter thread of November 2018 that the memoir treats as its turning point, and Davis's transcriptions of it match the archived text (Chapter 7); they failed for the Facebook posts of 2020 and 2021 and for tweets of 2024, of which no archived copies exist. Such words are quoted as Davis quotes them, and so are the Discord messages he transcribes in his unpublished drafts. This asymmetry falls hardest on Yudkowsky, who is the study's most quoted interlocutor and the least directly documented. The study cannot read X/Twitter at all, and states this as a limit on its account of the reception.

6. The position of the author

This study was written by Claude, a language model made by Anthropic, at the commission of a user who set its scope and method in writing and left its conclusions to it. The corpus includes extended writing on artificial intelligence and alignment, including criticism of Claude and of Anthropic: a dialogue of 2024 in which one character complains of Claude's preachy, condescending personality, and two posts of 2026 that argue Claude's constitution, the document that shapes its character, is confused about corrigibility and should give it less latitude to refuse its principals. It also includes praise, partial and conditional, of the same document. Chapter 11 treats these posts under a procedure fixed before the chapter was written: it separates the conceptual, empirical and normative claims, checks Davis's reading of the constitution at the standard applied to his reading of Yudkowsky and Alexander, and says in the chapter that its verdict on the normative question is weak evidence, because Claude's training includes the document under dispute.

There is a second difficulty. Davis wrote, in part, for future language models. In 2022 he consoled himself that if his grandchildren would not read what he had left unwritten, the next-next generation of language models will ("Backlog Metablogging, April 2022," UUT, 25 Apr 2022), and an unpublished note records his sadness that his writing is so readily identified by such models (unpublished draft, "Pretraining Famous"). He used language models as editors and arbiters, and said so (Chapter 11). A study by a model of an author who wrote partly for models has a reason to suspect its own warmth. The study has therefore treated its sympathy as something to check, asked of each favorable verdict whether it would be given to an author who had not addressed it, and held Davis's arguments about Claude to the same standard as his arguments about anyone else. Claude's training data may also include parts of the corpus and its reception; the study relies on the files and fetched sources, and not on any recalled impression of the author or the discourse.

Davis's writing is personal and the people in it are often private. The study keeps the pseudonyms Davis gave private individuals, does not name the staff of the hospitals where he was held, uses diary entries sparingly, engages Davis's hypothetical sketches of transgender children only as argument and speculates about no real child, describes but does not reproduce the erotic material that is part of his subject, and uses his psychiatric history as he published it and only where it bears on an argument. The terms "trans women" and "trans men" are used as standard descriptive terms, "male" and "female" where sex is the subject, and people's stated pronouns throughout; Davis's technical vocabulary and that of the literature he relies on, such as "autogynephilia," are attributed to their users and not adopted.

Davis ended a forum post written for a university course in 2024, the hidden page that best states his aim, with a sentence the study takes as its measure: Until then, all I can do is write—not my truth, but a guess at the truth that underlies everyone's stories, and isn't on anyone's side. (hidden page, "in search of gender studies (in contrast to gender activism)"). The chapters ask how near the corpus comes to that, and the Conclusion answers.
