Chapter 1The Apprentice: An Algorithmic Lucidity, 2011–2016

The first five years of Davis's blog were written for almost no one, and much of them is mathematics, programming and private jokes. They matter to this study for two reasons. The commitments that later organize the gender writing and the philosophy are present from the first posts: that definitions are conventions which cannot change facts, that words earn their keep by carrying information, that honesty includes being candid about what one is doing to others, and that one's own feelings are to be explained like anyone else's. What changes over the period is what Davis allows himself to do with those commitments. In 2012 and 2013 he records, in his own words, a decision to give up moral outrage and political argument as a waste of thought, and after a psychiatric hospitalization in early 2013 a retreat from philosophy into engineering. In 2016 he records the reverse decision. The concerns were not new in 2016, though what Davis made of them developed afterward; what changed in 2016 was his willingness to argue for them, and the argument took place in the one arena his 2012 renunciation had exempted, a small community where one voice could matter.

1. The blog

An Algorithmic Lucidity began in the last week of 2011. Its first three posts were a dialogue about birthdays, a two-line puzzle for New Year's Eve, and an exposition of why the derivative of the natural logarithm is defined where the logarithm is not. Over the next five years it published about 360 posts, most of them short. The topics are mixed in a way that later readers of the gender blog would not expect: real analysis and number theory; programming in Python, Ruby, Clojure and Rust; diaries of a coding bootcamp; reviews of conference talks; fan observations on Star Trek and My Little Pony; verse; and, in a few hundred words at a time, the rationalist subculture's ideas about evidence, agency and artificial intelligence. Davis was in his early and middle twenties. The blog records him studying mathematics at San Francisco State University, which he left in 2013 without a degree, being hospitalized after a psychotic episode in early 2013, attending App Academy in the autumn of 2013, and working afterward as a software engineer in San Francisco. In 2016 he moved to Berkeley and started a second, pseudonymous blog about gender.

There was almost no audience. Reviewing 2014, Davis wrote that the blog had seen 45 posts and 40 comments, and joked that he probably did not have any readers who believed in a particular theory of time (since I probably don't have any readers, full stop?) ("2014 Year in Reverse," AAL, 4 Jan 2015). The survey of reception for this study found almost nothing written about these years outside the blog's own comments; a reference page by Andrea James notes only that the blog began in 2011 and was later used to promote the term autogynephilia (Andrea James, reference page on Davis, transgendermap.com, retrieved October 2026). A small audience is not no audience, and the dialogues and mock addresses of these years are written for readers, imagined or real. The value of the early blog as evidence is narrower than candor: its formulations are dated, so they limit what can be attributed entirely to the later disputes.

2. The commitments, early

The first technical post states a view of definitions that the later philosophy of language develops at length. The complex exponential is not injective, so strictly it has no inverse function, and the essay resolves the difficulty by changing the name rather than the mathematics: There are reasons for defining it that way, but it is ultimately only a definition, a convention for what we mean when we use the world function, and mere definitions can't coerce true facts into being something other than what they are. The object that inverts the exponential should be called a multifunction, the post continues, because for clear communication, we should avoid calling the inverting-thing a function ("The Derivative of the Natural Logarithm," AAL, 25 Dec 2011). Both halves of the later position are here in one paragraph about complex analysis: words are conventions, and the choice among conventions is answerable to clear communication about facts that the convention does not alter. Chapter 4 follows the long working-out of that second clause.

The same view appears in less technical settings. Asked by a workbook in a summer course on contemporary sexuality to define virginity, Davis wrote that It's pointless to argue about the definitions of words; once you know what behavior someone has engaged in, then calling it 'virginity' or 'non-virginity' doesn't give you more information ("Contemporary," AAL, 7 Aug 2012). A reply to an insult turns on the same point: Words are useful insofar as they summarize information about the world. ("Cached Reply Upon Being Told That I'm an Idiot," AAL, 11 Sep 2012). Both are applications of a 2008 post in Yudkowsky's Sequences, which argued that an argument over whether an object belongs to a category is usually a disguised argument about some further question: The question “Is this object a blegg?” may stand in for different queries on different occasions. (Yudkowsky, "Disguised Queries," 2008). The young Davis drew the deflationary moral, that once the facts are known nothing is left to dispute. The later Davis drew what looks like the opposite one from the same post, that because categories serve inference some boundaries serve it worse than others (Chapter 4 argues that the two are one position). The 2012 answer is strongest under the condition it states, that the behavior is already known, when a label adds nothing; the later categorization essays concern how labels support inference when the facts are only partly known, which is a different case. The tension lies in the answer's opening generalization, that it is pointless to argue about definitions, which dismisses such disputes more broadly than its own explanation supports, and which Davis's opponents later used against him in the form he had once used it himself.

A dialogue from the first summer gives the commitment a moral form. Quiana asks her friend Synthia whether it is wrong to ring a restaurant's bell, put there for satisfied customers, when her visit was not satisfactory. Synthia offers to weigh costs and benefits and then declines, because Quiana's morality, as she describes it, is based on finding the 'correct' interpretation of verbal rules you read or heard. But if there isn't a uniquely preferable interpretation, if the words fail to capture the structure of reality, you get an unresolvable dilemma. ("The Morality of Ringing a Bell," AAL, 4 Jul 2012). The thought that an ethics of verbal rules breaks down where the words do not track anything recurs a decade later in Davis's objection to discourse norms that cannot say what they are for (Chapter 6).

Honesty appears early as a theme and, more interestingly, as a demand that people be candid about what they are doing to others. A 2012 parable has two philosophers argue that there is no such thing as lying and no such thing as delusion, each reducing one to the other, until a waitress shouts at them that they are both lying ("Lies and Delusion," AAL, 23 Jul 2012). The parable is a joke about a question the 2019 essays on deception take seriously (Chapter 5). A 2014 dialogue about Star Trek: Voyager is closer to the later position. Captain Janeway had separated Seven of Nine from the Borg collective against her will, on the ground that she had been human before her assimilation. The speaker thinks Seven's protest correct, and then revises the complaint: the trouble is less the decision than the justification. He wishes that Janeway could just be honest about what she was doing, and supplies the honest version, in which she admits that she is depriving Seven of autonomy, does not care, and expects her to come to like it, instead of appealing to some bizarre teleology of humanness ("Yet Another Idle Wish for a Future Star Trek Series," AAL, 17 Nov 2014). The objection is to coercion described as care, the complaint Chapter 9 finds at the center of Davis's writing on psychiatry. It is also an early instance of the norm of accounting: the act may be defensible, but the account of it must be true. The next day's post makes the same point about a single word, distinguishing the apology that promises a change of behavior from the apology that regrets a cost without promising anything, since Both can be sincere, but they mean different things. ("Missing Words VI," AAL, 18 Nov 2014).

Several later arguments appear in miniature. "Education and Indoctrination Feel the Same From the Inside" (AAL, 10 Jan 2013) holds that the experience of learning from a book is the same whether the book is true, so that one must judge the reliability of the processes that produced the book (link), the problem the 2026 essay on selection effects in communities takes up (Chapter 6). "Conversational Overhead" (AAL, 19 Nov 2013) describes the cost of pointing out that an argument does not support a conclusion one may share, Policy debates should not appear one-sided; I don't think that your Argument A actually supports Policy X (although I agree that X could be desireable for reasons independent of A), because the other party cannot be sure the objection does not conceal enmity (link). In 2013 the cost was a reason to stay out of such conversations. In 2019 the same act, disputing a bad argument for a conclusion he believed, was something Davis said it was especially important for me to loudly and publicly dispute ("On the Argumentative Form 'Super-Proton Things Tend to Come In Varieties,'" UUT, 13 Dec 2019). "Counterfactual Social Thought" (AAL, 28 Dec 2012) wishes for Pearl's causal graphs to clarify political claims, which are mostly claims about causes, a method the 2022 graph of transition uses (Chapter 3). "Goodhart's World" (AAL, 23 Nov 2012) proposes a history of the world as agents gaming incentive systems and ends with artificial intelligence gaming the utility function we gave it, instead of the one we should have given it (link), the oldest of the concerns Chapter 11 takes up.

What the early blog lacks is as telling. It has materials of the criterion and something like the norm of accounting, but nothing of the argument that the norm must be enforced without exception, and nothing of the will to enforce it on others. On the contrary, its most consistent theme in 2012 and 2013 is a decision not to.

3. The renunciation, 2012–2013

In November 2012 Davis quoted the passage in The Fountainhead in which Howard Roark tells the Dean that he does not care whether the Dean agrees with him, and called the attitude a very important skill that I really need to learn. He was careful about its grounds. The reason to renounce what he called the activist frame of mind was not that activism is ignoble: Because activism is painful and it doesn't work very well. I'm tired of hating Society (whatever that means) for not being what I wish it were. The plan was to concentrate on what he could control: to bury my head in a subculture of my kind of people, to master valuable skills, to make piles of money and donate a fraction of it to organizations that are doing valuable work ("Egoism as Defense Against a Life of Unending Heartbreak," AAL, 22 Nov 2012). Two weeks earlier a one-line post had listed cigarettes, alcohol, heroin, procrastination of boring-but-genuinely-worth-doing tasks, moral outrage ("Things That Are Hard to Quit (Non-exhaustive List)," AAL, 7 Nov 2012), and three days after the Roark post Davis compared the two directly: I suspect that relinquishing ideologically-induced moral outrage is actually harder than getting over many chemical dependencies, because there is no bright line to draw; one has to change the way you think, which is to say excise a part of your soul ("Speaking of Addiction," AAL, 25 Nov 2012).

The outrage Davis was trying to quit had a specific object, which the posts of December 2012 name. "Can't Break Clean" describes five years of shouting the Authorities lied to me; why why why why did they lie to me?! and the failure to replace it with the calmer recognition that institutions are flawed in knowable ways (AAL, 9 Dec 2012). A draft letter to a former teacher, published with a note that it was not sent, is the longest statement of the grievance against schooling, and it opens with the renunciation: So, I'm trying (mostly unsuccessfully) to stop being bitter, because I'm powerless to change anything. It closes on the same note: Really, I need to learn the skill of not paying any attention to mainstream society. (AAL, 3 Dec 2012). The grievance itself belongs to Chapter 9. Its shape matters here: a sense of having been lied to by authorities about what education was for, the same shape as the 2016 sense of having been lied to about gender (a little bit betrayed, as he put it then, quoted in Chapter 2).

The theory behind the renunciation is decision-theoretic. "Counterfactual Social Thought" argues that a policy opinion is implicitly a counterfactual about what would happen if one acted, and that most people with policy opinions cannot act on them, so that thinking about good outcomes in the absence of actual planning about how to achieve those outcomes from the present state is wasted cognition except insofar as the thinking-about-good-outcomes is valuable for its own sake (AAL, 28 Dec 2012). He applied it the following autumn, telling a television reporter who asked about a transit strike that I think it's a waste of cognition to have opinions about things you don't have any control over ("App Academy Diary, Week Five," AAL, 14–20 Oct 2013). The argument has an exception, which "Counterfactual Social Thought" states and which turned out to matter more than the rule: In a small group, an individual voice can change the outcome. The renunciation applied to Society, to countries of millions, to institutions too large to hear him. It did not apply to the subculture into which the Roark post proposed to bury his head.

The posts of early 2013 show the renunciation working and then giving way. In January Davis recorded feeling less bitter, because Naturalistic explanations feel better than hatred: he was not outraged that a supermarket or a bank fell short of its ideals, and resolved to regard the university the same way ("Clearness," AAL, 3 Jan 2013). In February he was hospitalized after a psychotic episode, which Chapter 9 treats. The posts after it record a further retreat, from particular kinds of inquiry: I don't want to do philosophy or social science or futurism anymore; I've lost the ability to do it sanely, if I ever had it., and Better to take up an engineering mindset. ("Prodrome," AAL, 1 Apr 2013). "Retirement" (May 2013) lists what to read instead: pure math and compsci if I need something intellectual. But no philosophy, history, current events, futurism, social science, biology, or game theory. (AAL, 16 May 2013). A post of June, on giving up meat, ends with the speaker promising himself once more that he will quit philosophy; it's beyond my reach and tithe a tenth of his income instead ("The Demandingness Objection," AAL, 30 Jun 2013).

The retreat had a practical side that the blog records with evident relief. In the autumn of 2013 Davis attended a coding bootcamp, and a year later he was writing in the second person about the cost to a colleague of a forced push to a shared Git repository, in a mock-sermon whose moral is not mock: the comforts of a technological civilization exist only by of the grace of people being sufficiently non-incompetent at their jobs, and small errors add up ("Lethal --force," AAL, 17 Mar 2014). He began calling 2014 the first year of my life (that I feel comfortable admitting to) ("Pumpkin Spice!," AAL, 11 Sep 2014), and in 2016, introducing himself to students he was to mentor, he wrote that everything in my life before December 2013 is non-canon ("Group Introduction Redux," AAL, 3 Apr 2016). The joke proposes a division, the angry autodidact before the bootcamp and the competent engineer after it, who did not have opinions about strikes. The blog of 2014 and 2015 complicates it, since the engineer went on publishing dialogues about coercion and honesty and a tirade against Pi Day.

Two features of this record bear on how the later corpus should be read. The renunciation was of outrage and of opinions on matters beyond his reach, never of the beliefs the outrage expressed. "Clearness" considers, and does not reject, saying calmly, without malice or outrage, but just as a fact that existing institutions are bad, with the caveat that I don't actually know how to fix anything myself. And the renunciation was argued on grounds of efficacy and self-preservation, not on grounds that the outrage was mistaken. Both features mean the later return need not have been a change of mind about anything the early blog believed. It was at least a change in what Davis thought he could affect, and in what he was willing to pay.

4. The method applied to himself

The habit Chapter 2 found in the gender writing, of explaining his own feelings and motives with the same naturalistic tools he applied to others, is present in the earliest personal posts. Told he could stop living on his father's money if he wanted, the speaker of a 2013 dialogue declines and corrects the record of his own complaints: the amount that I complain about my social position is more reflective of ideologically-induced madness rather than actual preferences; I really do like having time to study. My constant petulance is a distortion, a mistake. ("Hustle," AAL, 2 Jan 2013). Describing his custom of reading Evelyn Fox Keller during the Super Bowl, a symbol of what he was in contrast to what American culture expected of men, he notices that the pride is the jock's pride inverted: Nerds being proud that they don't know what jocks know, and jocks being proud that they don't know what nerds know, are both expressions of the same underlying psychology, just anchored on different subcultures. ("Tradition," AAL, 8 Mar 2013). A post on why disagreeing with people is hard sets the question of the other side's error against the question of their values and then, in a parenthesis, turns it on himself: (Of course, it is also instructive to consider whether you're making a mistake. But that is not the subject of this post.) ("Idiot or Alien? Incompetence or Evil?," AAL, 2 Oct 2012). The parenthesis is honest about where the post's interest lies, and it acknowledges the possibility of the speaker's error without bringing it into the analysis that follows; whether that becomes a pattern is a question for the later chapters, where the motives of others get essays (Chapter 5) and Davis's own conduct gets, in the memoir, an unusually full record (Chapter 7).

The explanations of the 2013 breakdown, which Davis wrote in dialogue form within weeks of it, use the same method on his madness. The delusions are attributed to an evolved disposition rather than to the ideas that filled them: futurist ideas had begun hitting the God-shaped hole in my psychology really hard, and someone from another subculture would have had demons instead ("Religious," AAL, 4 Mar 2013). Four years later, after a second episode, he revised the account in a post titled "Religious, Redux": the hole had not been empty before; It's actually the feeling of acquiring a new religion, getting eaten by someone else's egregore. (AAL, 11 Mar 2017). The revision anticipates the frame Davis later applied to his whole relation to the rationalist community, in which Yudkowsky is a religious leader, and I was—am—a devout adherent of the religion he made up ("Blanchard's Dangerous Idea and the Plight of the Lucid Crossdreamer," UUT, 8 Jul 2023), a frame Chapter 7 examines.

5. The change of license, 2016

The posts of 2016 do not announce new beliefs. They announce the end of the restraint. The first signs are dialogues of exasperation. In July a speaker voices concern about the socially undesirable implications of a causal graph and is asked whether the arrows might point the socially desirable way; he answers "Yes, I have considered that! I consider it extremely implausible!" ("Concerns," AAL, 10 Jul 2016). In September the sequel has his interlocutor object that the correlations in the published studies are less than one, and the speaker, despairing, cites the argumentative theory of reasoning ("Concerns II," AAL, 29 Sep 2016). The day after the first, a one-line post: "You don't get to decide what I am! ... for the same reason that I don't get to decide what I am! 'What I am' is an empirical question to be settled by evidence and reasoning ("Identity," AAL, 11 Jul 2016). In August Davis described his trouble as a surprising kind of prescriptivism: I wasn't expecting people to redefine a top-20 noun out from under my feet. ("Prescription," AAL, 28 Aug 2016). The posts are oblique. The blog's readers were not told what the causal graph was about, and the gender blog that explained it began in September.

The decisive post is a single sentence of October 2016, which the 2023 memoir later tied to a particular argument and a particular morning (Chapter 7): the moment of liberating clarity when you resolve the tension between being a good person and the requirement to pretend to be stupid by deciding not to be a good person anymore ("Late-Onset," AAL, 7 Oct 2016). The title is a pun on the clinical term for the kind of gender dysphoria Davis believed he shared, and the sentence names the trade the 2012 renunciation had made. Being a good person, in the sense of the agreeable and accommodating person the 2013 "Strategy Overhaul" had resolved to become (I certainly need to learn to fit in better with the other crazy monkeys by being more empathetic and agreeable; AAL, 28 Mar 2013), now required, as the sentence frames it, pretending to be stupid. The resolution, as Davis states it, was to give up the goodness. What accommodation was actually being asked of him, false assertion, silence, a form of address or a softer tone, the sentence does not say, and the later chapters dispute whether tact and distortion coincide.

The posts of the surrounding weeks show the new terms. "The Parable of the Honest Man and the Thing" (AAL, 5 Oct 2016) is about a man who declines to do the thing all his friends are doing because it requires signing a loyalty oath that is not scientifically accurate. "Resisting the Narrative" (AAL, 4 Dec 2016) describes a culture war against a map rather than a law, and ends on the problem the later philosophy would try to solve, why accuracy matters when nothing practical depends on it: And it being the case that no one will die if she gets the size of Antarctica wrong, what can I say to that? A report of attending a Star Trek convention dressed as Jadzia Dax, with breastforms and liquid-eyeliner spots, ends with the formula of the year: I am done pretending to be stupid. And maybe I don't want you to pretend, either. ("Joined," AAL, 19 Dec 2016). A post about My Little Pony at the end of December ends Just—stop fucking lying! ("An Element Which Is Nameless," AAL, 28 Dec 2016). The year's review records the cost and anticipates more: had my trust in the sanity of the people around me re-shattered into a thousand thousand bloody fragments, with a joke about trying to minimize the sum of number of friends lost and bricks thrown through my window ("2016 Year in Reverse," AAL, 2 Jan 2017).

Two posts of early 2017 state the new position compactly. In "A Common Misunderstanding" (AAL, 9 Feb 2017) the narrator is accused of ignoring the psychological harm of a word usage, contrary to a rationalist's duty to choose actions by their consequences, and replies that there are two kinds of rationalist, the instrumental and the epistemic: "A common misunderstanding. You're thinking of the good kind," I said. "I'm the bad kind." The joke, from just after this chapter's period, is a precursor of the role rule that this study calls the role-constitutive form of the coordination argument: that a person committed to describing the world truly is, by that commitment, not allowed to weigh the consequences of a description. The argument about exceptions that would make it a coordination argument is not yet there. Two years later it was stated without the joke, in the 2019 essay "Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist," as a rule against caring how descriptions sound, which Chapter 4 (§6) quotes and examines. "Dreaming of Political Bayescraft" (AAL, 3 Mar 2017) gives the new political philosophy as "Being smart is more important than being good (for humans). All ideologies are false; some are useful.", and its commentary states a principle the later corpus applies to Davis's own egalitarianism and feminism: Any time you find yourself being tempted to be loyal to an idea, it turns out that what you should actually be loyal to is whatever underlying feature of human psychology makes the idea look like a good idea. The Murray review of 2020 and "Sexual Dimorphism" (2021) both use it to explain how he could give up beliefs about sex differences while keeping the values that made them attractive (Chapter 10).

The return to argument took place in the arena the 2012 rule had exempted. The rule was that thinking about outcomes one cannot affect is wasted; its stated exception was the small group in which one voice can change the outcome. The rationalist community of the Bay Area, the subculture of his kind of people in which the Roark post had proposed to bury himself, was such a group, and its founder was the author Davis credited with rewriting his personality. When it seemed to him that the community was getting a question of fact wrong for reasons of kindness, the question was no longer about Society. It was about the people around him, and one voice might reach them. The renunciation had never covered that case. Read this way, the 2016 change is consistent with the 2012 renunciation's exception. The exception concerned whether speaking could make a difference, though, and the renunciation was also about the costs of outrage and of dependence on it, which proximity to a community's leaders did not remove. Whether Davis weighed those costs before the campaign is a question for Chapter 7.

That reading leaves a question open, which the early record can pose but not answer. The renounced outrage had been about being lied to by authorities. The new campaign was also about being lied to, by a community and its leaders, and its rhetoric in 2016 (Just—stop fucking lying!) is the rhetoric of 2012. The early record establishes both a concern with truthful description and a dissatisfaction with the costs of sustained outrage, and their recurring together in 2016 does not show which drove the other; both can direct attention and sustain work, and both can distort judgments of particular people. The later campaign permits more specific questions, whether Davis applied his standards to arguments that favored him, acknowledged contrary evidence, and revised his judgments of other people when criticism warranted it, and Chapter 7 takes them up.

6. The voice

The early blog also forms the voice of the later corpus, which Chapter 8 treats as method rather than ornament. The dialogues between Synthia and Quiana in 2011 and 2012 give a position to a voice that can be overruled; the unlabeled two-voice dialogues of 2013 let Davis argue with himself on the page; and the exasperated dialogues of 2016 put the opponent's objection into a mouth that cannot answer back. The mock-formal address (dear reader), the italics, the coinages (the "American coffee hegemon"), and the tirade against Pi Day as blasphemy, treason, superstitious superficiality, degenerate folderol, and frivolous depravity! (AAL, 14 Mar 2015) are a comic register for a serious complaint: that a coincidence of digits is marketed as mathematics, so that a symbol is detached from any meaning. The complaint anticipates, on a small scale, the later one about words.

The verse says things the prose of those years did not. "October Eleventh" (AAL, 11 Oct 2015) is a poem for National Coming Out Day in which the speaker sits in a dark closet and declines to come out: I might be a dragon
I might be a woman, ending Singing "Happy Coming Out Day,
But I'm staying in." A year later, on the evening before the same day, he posted on Facebook that he was a male with mild gender dysphoria which is almost certainly causally related to my autogynephilic sexual/romantic orientation, which I am genuinely proud of!, as the 2023 memoir records ("Blanchard's Dangerous Idea"). "I Want to Be the One" (AAL, 7 Dec 2015) puts the AI concern in a pop lyric's form, and the joke in a few lines about electrolysis (AAL, 3 Jul 2015) puts the gender theme in a pun. The comic register lets Davis say a thing and disown it in one breath, and in the early blog that is mostly how the personal subjects appear.

7. Conclusion

The early blog supports a simple account of continuity and change. Materials of the criterion of honesty that the later corpus develops, that descriptions answer to their usefulness for conveying the truth and that definitions cannot alter facts, are present in December 2011 in a post about complex logarithms, though the functional account of deception that the criterion becomes is not, and the demand that people be candid about what they do to others is present in 2014 in a post about a television captain. So is the method of explaining his own feelings naturalistically. None of these was invented for the gender dispute; what the later work added were mechanisms, evidential standards, a theory of misleading communication and arguments about what a community must enforce. The coordination argument is absent: the young Davis held his commitments for himself and gave up, in writing and on argued grounds, the attempt to make others share them. The change of 2016 was a change in what he permitted himself. He described it as giving up being good in order to stop pretending to be stupid, and he exercised it inside the small community that his own earlier rule had left open.

The early record also bears on how much weight his later accounts of himself can carry. The 2023 memoir describes his life before 2016 as one in which the autogynephilic fantasy was a private matter he had no reason to write about. The early blog is consistent with that account, though it corroborates only how little he published on the subject, not his explanation of why. Before the middle of 2016 the subject appears only obliquely, in a joke of 2012 about a trans first officer in a future Star Trek (AAL, 17 Nov 2012), the electrolysis pun, a poem about staying in the closet and a handful of similar posts, and when it arrives in earnest it arrives as a complaint about other people's words. The writing that followed was argued from commitments the blog shows him holding years before the public dispute, though not before he had a personal stake in its subject.

Note on method

Every post quoted or characterized was read in full. The chapter distinguishes what the posts of 2011–2016 say from what the memoir of 2021–2024 later says about the same years, and uses the first to check the second. The reception survey covered LessWrong threads, the blogs' comment sections, Reddit and a web search; for these years it found almost nothing beyond the comments. Davis's spelling and punctuation are quoted as published. An earlier draft of this chapter was reviewed by GPT-6-Astra (OpenAI); the chapter was revised in response to its criticisms, several of its corrections are the reviewer's, and some passages adapt wording it proposed.
