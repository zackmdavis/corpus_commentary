Chapter 7The Whole Dumb Story: Yudkowsky, the Community, and the Press

Between 2021 and 2024 Davis published, in five installments and some 100,000 words, an account of his estrangement from the rationalist community and from Eliezer Yudkowsky, whose writing had formed him. He called it the Whole Dumb Story. Two further installments exist as unpublished drafts. The memoir is a document of unusual candor about its own method and its author's conduct, and much of what it says that can be checked survives checking. Its central charge has three components that stand or fall separately: a textual claim that Yudkowsky's statements on gender between 2016 and 2021 departed from his own philosophy without acknowledgment, a claim that the departure was culpable because of the role Yudkowsky claimed, and a set of inferences about why he made it. The first is established for one statement of 2018 and weaker elsewhere. The second applies a legitimate standard, Yudkowsky's own, though the case falls short of it in ways Davis's theory of deception should have recognized. The third rests on better evidence than its critics allowed and claims more than that evidence supports. The memoir's most durable contribution may be its record of its author, who documents his own escalations and failures in unusual detail, though the harms his campaign did to others are scattered through the record and never weighed.

1. The document

The phrase comes from a post of June 2022 in which Davis argued with himself about whether to finish. He could not, he wrote, simply want to be done with this stupid memoir of religious betrayal that no one should care about; anyone who kept at it must hold some deep, uncancellable conviction that the memoir is not stupid, that the religious leaders did betray you and their faith, that someone should care, that telling the Whole Dumb Story—telling it right, so that every graf sings and hits the exact notes of righteous fury and deconfusion and penetrating portraiture—is part of your life ("An Egoist Faith," UUT, 24 Jun 2022). The published sequence runs from "Sexual Dimorphism in Yudkowsky's Sequences, in Relation to My Gender Problems" (May 2021), whose story the third installment recaps as the start of the Whole Dumb Story, through "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea and the Plight of the Lucid Crossdreamer" and "A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning" (July 2023) and "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them" (December 2023) to "Agreeing With Stalin in Ways That Exhibit Generally Rationalist Principles" (March 2024). Two drafts in the blog's public repository continue it: "Standing Under the Same Sky" and "Guess I'll Die," the latter written as the evaluation that the last published installment promises and does not supply. An earlier attempt, dated 2020, survives as the draft "'I Tell Myself to Let the Story End.'"

The memoir's method is stated within it, and the statement is more scrupulous than its critics noticed. Davis quotes his own emails freely and other people's private messages only where he judges he could have their consent or where nothing sensitive is revealed. Of his correspondence with Yudkowsky he reports what he sent and nothing about whether there was a reply. He states the purpose of the rule:

It seems particularly important to lay out these judgments about privacy norms in connection to my attempts to contact Yudkowsky, because part of what I'm trying to accomplish in telling this Whole Dumb Story is to deal reputational damage to Yudkowsky, which I claim is deserved. (We want reputations to track reality. If you see Erin exhibiting a pattern of intellectual dishonesty, and she keeps doing it even after you talk to her about it privately, you might want to write a blog post describing the pattern in detail—not to hurt Erin, particularly, but so that everyone else can make higher-quality decisions about whether they should believe the things that Erin says.) Given that motivation of mine, it seems important that I only try to hang Yudkowsky with the rope of what he said in public, where you can click the links and read the context for yourself: I'm attacking him, but not betraying him.

"A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning," UUT, 15 Jul 2023

Few polemics announce their aim so plainly, and the announcement is an instance of the norm of accounting: a reader who knows the author means to damage a reputation can discount accordingly. The method has a second consequence that Davis did not state in the memoir but conceded in its comment section. When a reader complained that the post attributed motives without considering alternatives, Davis offered as one explanation that the privacy norms let me report on my psychological speculations about named individuals, but not all of the evidence that supports them, which might have come from private conversations (Davis, reply to Max H, LW, 18 Jul 2023). The rule that protects Yudkowsky's private replies from publication also protects Davis's inferences about him from correction by readers who cannot see what prompted them. The last installment ends on a sentence of that kind: of the man who wrote the Sequences, Davis says that he is dead now, Without disclosing any specific content from private conversations that may or may not have happened, I think he knows it. The reader is given a conclusion and told that its grounds are withheld.

Davis also saw that the rule could be gamed from his own side. Norms of privacy, he wrote in the fourth installment, can regulate only what a text says outright, so someone who wants to reveal private information while obeying them has an incentive to leak everything they possibly can as subtext—to imply it, and hope to escape punishment on grounds of not having "really said it." He applied the point to himself: being furious at Yudkowsky for prevaricating without lying, he should not be so foolish as to think myself innocent and beyond reproach for not having "really said it." The passage that follows is a section framed as hypothetical, which this study engages only as argument and does not read for its subtext.

The method changed between drafts. The 2020 draft declared that it would name no one, in order to try to keep the rhetorical emphasis on "true tale of personal heartbreak, coupled with sober analysis of the sociopolitical factors leading thereto", and called Yudkowsky by invented names (unpublished draft, "'I Tell Myself to Let the Story End'", dated 2020). By 2023 the names were real, including Davis's own, since the pseudonym under which the gender blog had been written was dropped the same month: it would be dishonorable and absurd to keep up the charade of hiding my own True Name while speaking so frankly about other people ("Blanchard's Dangerous Idea and the Plight of the Lucid Crossdreamer," UUT, 8 Jul 2023). The friends who appear in the narrative under pseudonyms, marked by quotation marks, are private people, and this chapter keeps their pseudonyms.

The evidential limits are those of any memoir and some peculiar to this one. It is one person's account, reconstructed from emails and chat logs that he says make its detail possible. The other principals have published little in reply: Yudkowsky's views of the dispute survive in tweets and Facebook posts, most of them behind a login wall that this study could not pass, and in Discord messages that Davis transcribes in an unpublished draft; Scott Alexander's survive in comments and a few emails that Davis quotes. Where the memoir's transcriptions can be checked, they hold. The Twitter thread of 28 November 2018 that the third installment treats as the casus belli is preserved in Internet Archive snapshots from 2019 and 2020, and the tweets Davis quotes match the archived texts (Yudkowsky, Twitter, 28–29 Nov 2018, via Wayback Machine). The archive also bears out a smaller claim that a reader might have doubted, that the obvious rejoinder had been put to Yudkowsky in the replies: one respondent wrote, I am also a Yudkowskian, Eliezer; "female human" is a cluster in thingspace :) (reply to Yudkowsky, Twitter, 1 Dec 2018, via Wayback Machine). Nothing found in this study suggests that Davis misquotes his opponents, and his habit is to quote them at length; whether he characterizes them fairly is the question of the next section. Where posts written at the time cover the same events, they are used here to check the memoir's retrospective account.

The genre has known hazards. The literature on apostates' accounts of the movements they left (David Bromley's, recalled here from memory rather than consulted) warns that such narratives are shaped by the needs of the teller's new position. Davis did not leave for anywhere. He continued to write for the community's website, to cite its founding texts as authority, and to describe his project, in a phrase from 2024, as a heresy blog, not an apostate blog ("Prediction Markets Are Not a Drop-In Replacement for Concepts," UUT, 21 Dec 2024). His new position is inside the community, as its heretic, and a narrative can serve that position as an apostate's serves his; the self-description is evidence about the position and no exemption from the warning, and the memoir's claims are examined here on their merits rather than discounted for their genre.

2. The charge

The story the memoir tells can be put briefly. In March 2016 Yudkowsky posted on Facebook that he was over 50% probability at this point that at least 20% of the ones with penises are actually women, which Davis read as a reversal of the 2009 Sequences essay "Changing Emotions," a sustained account of why a man's fantasy of becoming a woman could not be fulfilled by any foreseeable technology and might not be coherent. In November 2018, defending the use of trans people's chosen pronouns, Yudkowsky tweeted that you're not standing in defense of truth if you insist on a word, brought explicitly into question, being used with some particular meaning, which Davis read as a reversal of the Sequences' account of how words can be wrong (Yudkowsky, Twitter, 28 Nov 2018, via Wayback Machine). Between November 2018 and April 2019 Davis, with a group of allies, sent Yudkowsky four emails and a cheerful-price offer (money offered for his time, at a price he would be glad to accept) asking for a public clarification that categories are not arbitrary, and published "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" (examined in Chapter 4). After a final email in September 2020 Yudkowsky published the clarification on Facebook, and Davis declared himself no longer at war. In February 2021 Yudkowsky published a proposal for pronoun reform that Davis answered in "Challenges to Yudkowsky's Pronoun Reform Proposal" (March 2022) and, at length, in the last installment of the memoir, which concludes that If Eliezer Yudkowsky can't unambiguously choose Truth over Feelings, then Eliezer Yudkowsky is a fraud.

The Introduction distinguished in this conclusion a charge of role-relative false advertising from an inference from inconsistency to motive. The memoir mixes them, and a third claim lies beneath both: that the texts say what Davis says they say. The three are taken in order.

The textual claim

The textual claim concerns three moments. On the first, the 2016 Facebook post, Davis's case is weaker than his rhetoric. He concedes in the last installment that the two posts weren't necessarily inconsistent: "Changing Emotions" might address the fantasies of men with a male gender identity and the 2016 post a different population, if gender identity were a real property. Davis thought this reconciliation dubious because the populations seemed to him to be the same, and he noted that the 2016 claim concerned a complicated empirical question about which reasonable people might read new evidence differently and change their minds. Whether the populations are the same is the sexological question of Chapter 3, which found Davis's strong answer beyond the evidence. On the 2016 post, then, the memoir shows at most a change of view, and only if the reconciliation Davis himself considered fails; what it establishes is that Yudkowsky did not say how the two posts fit together, which is a complaint about accounting.

The second moment carries the weight, and here the memoir is right. The thread of November 2018 says that asking to be called by a pronoun is not lying and that a dispute over a word's meaning is a policy dispute, not a stand for truth. Its last clause is the one Davis fixed on. The Sequences had argued at length that definitions can be wrong, that You cannot hide behind a comforting shield of correct-by-definition, and the memoir assembles nine passages from them making the point. A reader could take the 2018 thread narrowly, as a rebuke only to people who called pronouns lies. Yudkowsky offered that reading himself, according to a tweet of February 2021 that Davis copied into notes for an unpublished thread: Some people afaict were doing the literal ontologically confused thing; seemed like a simple thing to make progress on. Some people wanted to read it as a coded statement despite all my attempts to narrow it, but what can you do. (hidden page, "draft Twitter thread"). The archived thread supports the narrow reading in one respect. Yudkowsky asked in it, Is there a page I can read to find a non-confused explanation of how there's scientific truth at stake?, and when sent Davis's essay of February 2018 replied that he agreed that it is not ontologically confused (Yudkowsky, Twitter, 29 Nov and 1 Dec 2018, via Wayback Machine). Davis reports both tweets and judges the acknowledgment too quiet: one like against a hundred and forty on the thread's first tweet. The narrow reading has a problem that Davis identifies. The same thread says that sports leagues segregated around an Aristotelian binary raise a policy question that I personally find very humorous, which is a remark about the reasons people have for wanting to talk about sex, not about whether pronouns are assertions. And the clause about not standing in defense of truth is general in form. Yudkowsky's clarification of September 2020, as Davis quotes it and as Chapter 4 discusses, conceded the general point: one is being the bad guy if you try to shut down that conversation by saying that "I can define the word 'woman' any way I want". The reconciliation a defender would offer, that the thread was describing a legitimate negotiation over a word's meaning of the kind Chapter 4 accepts, does not reach the clause. On that account the negotiation has factual stakes, since it decides which inferences the word will serve, and the clause denies that insisting on a meaning is a stand for truth at all. The 2020 text withdrew that denial in general terms, which is why it reads as a correction of the clause and not only of its readers.

The third moment is the pronoun proposal of February 2021, which Davis quotes as calling the simplest and best protocol a convention under which he refers to those who have asked for it, with a default that goes by gamete size. Davis's reply, that the convention keeps sex in the default while claiming to remove it and that the reason people ask for pronouns is the meaning those pronouns already carry, was judged sound in Chapter 4, and it found its strongest public support from an unexpected quarter. LessWrong's lead administrator, Oliver Habryka, wrote under the cross-post that basically everything in this post strikes me as "obviously true", and that Yudkowsky's post had changed his own assessment of the community's leadership, so that he should expect that depending on the circumstances community leaders might make up sophisticated stories for why pretty obviously true things are false in order to not have to deal with complicated political issues (Habryka, comment on "Challenges," LW, 13 Mar 2022). That is close to Davis's own diagnosis, from a reader outside his circle. Not every reader agreed. Veedrac took Yudkowsky's point to be abstention, nor is it to deny that there is any preexisting connotation to the pronouns, and AprilSR wrote, I agree with a lot of the points you made, but they feel unrelated to the ones Eliezer made. (Veedrac and AprilSR, comments on "Challenges," LW, 14 Mar 2022).

The textual claim, so restricted, is established, on texts that are known, apart from the 2018 thread, as Davis quotes them, and that where they could be checked he quotes accurately: from 2018 to 2021 Yudkowsky made public statements on the meaning of gender words that departed in emphasis, and in the 2018 general clause in substance, from the philosophy he had published, did not acknowledge the departure when it was pointed out, and corrected it in 2020. The 2021 proposal is open to a different objection, the one Davis's reply made and Chapter 4 accepted: that it understated the costs of its convention and misdescribed the meaning pronouns already carry. Proposing a convention, with its reasons, is not a departure from the philosophy of categories, which allows announced conventions. A proposal of that kind can still misdescribe the usage it means to change, and that is a factual error inside a legitimate kind of proposal, not a claim that meaning is settled by announcement. It belongs with the understated costs, and the fault lies there. Davis says as much himself when pressed. Asked what more serious charge he was making, he replied: I'm not conjecturing anything worse than Kolmogorov complicity. (Davis, reply to Wei Dai, LW, 28 Jul 2023). The phrase, from an essay of Alexander's, names a strategy whose players, in Davis's gloss, typically seem to think that it's cheap to just avoid a few sensitive topics, and his complaint in the same reply was that the distortion had not stayed on the sensitive topic and had reached the cognitive function of categorization, a "dry" philosophy topic which you wouldn't expect to be politically sensitive.

The role-relative charge

The word fraud comes at the end of a passage that defines it. Davis means it in the dictionary sense of a person who is not what he or she pretends to be, and he states the pretense: (The "epistemic hero" posturing isn't compatible with the "sometimes personally prudent and not community-harmful" prevarication; he needs to choose one or the other.) The second phrase is Yudkowsky's, from the comments on the 2021 post as Davis quotes them: it is sometimes personally prudent and not community-harmful to post your agreement with Stalin about things you actually agree with Stalin about, in ways that exhibit generally rationalist principles. The standard is role-relative. Alexander, who wrote the 2014 essay Davis spent years rebutting and whom Davis still considered dishonest on the subject, is exempted from it: Scott Alexander chose Feelings, but I can't hold that against him, because Scott is explicit about only speaking in the capacity of some guy with a blog. Yudkowsky is not: I think Yudkowsky is opting in to being held to higher standards than other mortals. The charge is the norm of accounting intensified by role. One who claims a standard owes his audience notice of where he departs from it.

The standard has a source, and Davis cites it. In 2007 Yudkowsky wrote of Stephen Jay Gould, whom he accused of misrepresenting the state of evolutionary biology to the public, that Consistently self-serving scientific "error", in the face of repeated correction and without informing others of the criticism, blends over into scientific fraud. (Yudkowsky 2007b). The sentence holds that intent is not required for the charge, a position Davis's theory of deception shares (Chapter 5), and it names three features of the case it condemns. Applied to the case the memoir makes, they come out unevenly. That the errors were self-serving is the motive claim, taken up below. That they came in the face of repeated correction is established for 2018 and 2019 and for the 2021 proposal, though the correction of September 2020 counts against a pattern of refusing correction, and Davis acknowledged it at the time as success. His account of it then was that it would be petty to dwell on quibbles when the core problem had been addressed, though a footnote added, of the clarification's emphasis, I'm suspicious that the change in emphasis reflects the need to not be seen as criticizing the "pro-trans" coalition. What he later held against it was what followed: within a matter of months, he turned around and came back with the 2021 proposal (unpublished draft, "Guess I'll Die"). On his own account, then, the correction stood until a post that is open to a different objection. The third condition, that the criticism was concealed, is where the case is weakest, and Davis's own theory says why.

Yudkowsky's 2021 post carried a disclaimer, which Davis quotes in full and which is known to this study only in his quotation. It warned that people might be reluctant to voice good counterarguments in public, that in the counterfactual where he knew of strong ones he would have stayed silent and only Zack M. Davis would have said anything where you could hear it, and that This is a filter affecting your evidence, for which readers should adjust. It also claimed some confidence in my own ability to independently invent everything important that would be on the other side of the filter and check it myself before speaking. On the account Davis gave in "Heads I Win, Tails?—Never Heard of Her" (Chapter 5), a disclosed filter can turn distortion into missing information, but only when the disclosure tells the reader what is being selected against and nothing else in the message undoes it. Yudkowsky's disclaimer told readers the direction of the filter and named its most persistent source; it covered the author's own silence as well as other people's, since he would have kept quiet himself had he known a strong counterargument, and it said that the filter has not to my own knowledge filtered out a giant valid counterargument that invalidates this whole post. Its claim of confidence that he had reconstructed what lay on the other side told them, in effect, that no correction was needed. Naming Davis can be read two ways, as pointing readers to the dissent or as marking it as one man's, and the text as quoted does not settle which. Davis praises the disclaimer's explanation of filtered evidence as great, and states the standard he would have accepted: I don't have a problem with selective argumentation that's clearly labeled as such: there's no shame in being an honest specialist who says, "I've mostly thought about these issues though the lens of ideology X, and therefore can't claim to be comprehensive or even-handed; if you want other perspectives, you'll have to read other authors and think it through for yourself." His objection is to that confidence, which a post that omitted the obvious costs of its own proposal could not sustain, and to Yudkowsky's statement that the prudence was not community-harmful, against which Habryka's comment is one piece of evidence. Both objections are sound. The first is stronger than a simple account of disclosure would allow, because the reassurance partly withdraws the warning, and what the two show together is an accounting given and then partly taken back, which is still a lesser thing than concealment. By the Gould criterion, which requires that others not be informed of the criticism, the case is mixed: the post informed readers that criticism existed, named its most persistent source, and told them to adjust.

Whether Yudkowsky claimed the role that would make the standard apply is a further question. The memoir's evidence is a handful of remarks: that he aspires to make sure [his] departures from perfection aren't noticeable to others, that too many people think it's unvirtuous to shut up and listen to [him], and the dath ilan fiction in which a saner world looks down on Earth. The two tweets are known only as Davis quotes them, without their context, and the most famous remark, that rationalism is the belief that Yudkowsky is the rightful caliph, is Alexander's, made half in jest. The stronger evidence for the role is the Sequences themselves, written as instruction in how to think, and the Gould post, in which their author applied this very standard to a scientist who informed the public. Davis's own account complicates it, for of the earlier Yudkowsky he writes that Part of what made him so trustworthy back then was that he wasn't asking for trust. Davis anticipated the objection that everybody knew not to take the posture seriously, and conceded that such readers may have a point: If you already knew that Yudkowsky's pose of epistemic superiority was phony (because everyone knows), then you are wiser than I was. A commenter on the installment asked why anyone should be so invested in one man's mind, and Davis answered with an analogy of his own making, someone who spent eight years in the prime of their life prosecuting a false advertising lawsuit against a "World's Best" brand ice-cream for not actually being the best in the world (Davis, reply to RamblinDash, LW, 4 Mar 2024). The charge of false advertising survives the concession only in a form Davis himself states: there were readers, he among them, who believed the advertising, and the memoir is written for them. That some readers believed it does not by itself show that the teacher made the claim, and the remarks the memoir collects establish an aspiration and a public persona more securely than a promise. Alexander's modesty, for its part, removes the higher standard and leaves the ordinary one: an author who claims no authority still owes his readers accurate arguments, and Davis's judgment of Alexander's arguments did not change when he exempted Alexander from the charge of fraud. A standard that binds only those who claim authority can be escaped by disclaiming the authority while keeping the influence, and the ordinary standard is the one that still reaches an essay Davis spent years rebutting.

Davis keeps description apart from evaluation. The conclusion of the last installment says that whether being a fraud is wrong is a further question: If being a fraud were necessary for saving the world, maybe being a fraud would be the right thing to do? The installment had already allowed that, facing a choice between deceiving readers and incurring the wrath of Power, maybe deceiving your readers is the right thing to do, and asked only that the choice be real: if you cared about not deceiving your readers, you would want to be sure that those really were the only two options. The unpublished sequel takes up the case for the defense under the heading "Perhaps, if the World Were at Stake," asks isn't there a story here where I'm the villain, and offers two reasons why Yudkowsky's own decision theory might not condemn yielding to political pressure, prefaced by a disclaimer that Davis writes without any pretense that I'm any sort of neutral expert witness on decision theory (unpublished draft, "Guess I'll Die", dated 2023). This is the coordination argument's opposite. Davis allows that prudence might justify a departure from truthfulness and insists only that the departure be priced and that the alternatives be searched. His answer to the defense is that prudence did not require pronouncements: if that's your story, I think you need to actually not comment. He notices the awkwardness of arguing for silence (It's a little uncomfortable that I seem to be arguing for a duty to self-censorship here.) and resolves it by locating the wrong in the role: (If some garden-variety trans ally had made the same dumb arguments, I would make the same counterarguments, but I wouldn't feel betrayed.) The role-relative charge, so stated, is coherent and fair. Its weakness is the weight it gives the disclaimer: Davis quotes it, praises part of it and disputes the rest, but his verdict counts its overstatement and not the warning it did give.

The motive inferences

The memoir's vocabulary goes further than its argument. Of the 2018 thread it says that it is hard to read the Tweets Yudkowsky published as anything other than an attempt to intimidate and delegitimize people who want to use language to reason about sex rather than gender identity, and that the remark about sports is not something any scientifically literate person would write if they had actually thought about the issue at all, as opposed to having first decided (consciously or not) to bolster their reputation among progressives by dunking on transphobes on Twitter. Of the 2021 post it says that the point is to pander to biological sex denialists without technically saying anything unambiguously false. A footnote to the fourth installment says flatly that the issue did arise from outright bad faith. Of Alexander, the unpublished sequel says that he pulled it out of his ass because it was politically convenient. These are claims about what behavior was optimized for, and Chapter 5 argued that such claims need counterfactual evidence: how the same person behaves when the political incentive points the other way.

The memoir supplies more of that evidence than its critics allowed, in comparisons that hold the author fixed while the political stakes change. Alexander's essay on the Glasgow Coma Scale, published three months before his essay on categories, defends a category by its predictive use, and Davis counts sixteen uses of predict and its cognates; the categories essay defends one by its effect on happiness ("If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them," UUT, 30 Dec 2023). Yudkowsky in 2019 called the claim that Alexander's blog was alt-right a lie, a use of the word that the 2018 thread would have disallowed to his opponents, and Davis asked that the same standard be applied when the category was racist and the person Alexander. Yudkowsky's disavowal of neoreaction in 2013 disclaimed dismissing ideas for fear of unpopularity, and his disavowal of 2019 declared himself actively hostile. Each shows different treatment, and none is a controlled comparison. The Glasgow Coma essay and the categories essay concern classifications with different purposes, the two uses of lie concern an assertion about a blog and a choice of pronoun, and the two disavowals of neoreaction are six years apart, an interval in which beliefs and associations could change for reasons other than pressure. Davis states his own reading of the neoreaction pair as a wager, that moral pressure from the left had intensified in the interval, and says: My bet was on the latter. As Chapter 5 concluded of such patterns, they show selection more readily than they show which motive did the selecting.

The alternatives were put to Davis, and the memoir records them. In the Discord exchanges of 2022 transcribed in "Standing Under the Same Sky," on what Davis describes as a publicly-linked Discord server with hundreds of members, Yudkowsky's coauthor on Planecrash, Lintamande, suggested that the pronoun verdict might have been the same if someone had posed the question to him as a hypothetical in 2004, and that a person prompted by the surrounding society to think about pronouns, who thinks about them and gives an answer, is not thereby being disingenuous. Davis's reply, in the draft's unfinished notes, is that The input-output behavior of consciously trying to agree with Stalin and "getting prompted by the surrounding Society" are similar (unpublished draft, "Standing Under the Same Sky", dated 2023). That is true, and on Davis's functional account the process does not matter if the output misrepresents; whether it does is the textual question, settled above for the 2018 clause and not for the rest. The similar behavior shows nothing about what produced it, and so it is not enough for pander, bad faith, or intimidate, each of which, in ordinary use, names an aim. Davis's consciously or not gives the words a functional reading, but on that reading they still say that the behavior was selected for its political effect, which is the claim the comparisons support without establishing. In 2025 Davis told a journalist that he could not settle the question of consciousness: To this day, I am not sure to what extent he is being consciously dishonest versus has fallen into self-delusion ("Conversations With Cade Metz on the Rationalists," LW, 1 Jul 2026).

When readers of the third installment objected, Davis gave ground. Dean Valentine wrote that the motivated reasoning and dishonesty attributed to Yudkowsky seems comically exaggerated (Valentine, comment on "A Hill of Validity," LW, 16 Jul 2023). Max H, offered forty dollars by Davis to state his objections (he accepted, noting that he answered because of the social pressure and not the money), wrote that Basically everywhere you speculate about someone's internal motivations or thoughts, or claim that someone is doing something for political reasons or status reasons or energy reasons, I think you're failing to consider alternate hypotheses adequately (Max H, comment, LW, 17 Jul 2023). Davis withdrew one word, On reflection, the word "transparently" definitely isn't right here (thanks!), but I'm comfortable standing by "political reasons"., said he felt guilty about having mixed a cheerful price with social pressure, and rewrote the closing paragraphs after Wei Dai asked how he had ruled out the hypothesis that Yudkowsky was protecting genuine aims, calling the original egregiously terrible writing on my part (Davis, reply to Wei Dai, LW, 28 Jul 2023). The pattern matches the one Chapter 3 found in Davis's handling of evidence: specific concessions are frequent and followed by edits, while the core claim stays.

The last installment states a standard for its own inferences that would have answered most of these objections if the earlier ones had kept to it. Anticipating the charge that he was reading minds, Davis wrote:

But my complaint is about the work the algorithm is doing in Stalin's service, not about how it feels; I'm talking about a pattern of publicly visible behavior stretching over years, not claiming to be a mind-reader. (Thus, "take actions" in favor of/against, rather than "be"; "exert optimization pressure in the direction of", rather than "try".) I agree that everyone has a story in which they don't look terrible, and that people mostly believe their own stories, but it does not therefore follow that no one ever does anything terrible.

"Agreeing With Stalin in Ways That Exhibit Generally Rationalist Principles," UUT, 2 Mar 2024

Held to this, the memoir documents a pattern of public behavior, supports it with comparisons that are suggestive though not controlled, and persuaded a reader outside his circle, Habryka, which is evidence of the argument's force more than of Yudkowsky's motives. The words that go beyond it (pander, intimidate, bad faith, and of Alexander's dolphins a judgment about what he would have said in 2012 that nothing in the record tests) are the part the critics were right to reject. The fourth installment had already conceded the principle: of his own claim that Yudkowsky seemed to have given up on public reason, Davis wrote that that's a probabilistic inference, not a certain observation, and invited readers to say in the comments where it went wrong.

Where the coordination argument operates

One passage in the unpublished sequel shows the coordination argument of the study's thesis in its plainest form. Answering a list of possible cruxes from a Twitter interlocutor, David Xu, whom Yudkowsky endorsed, Davis wrote that the dispute was a conflict. I want to facilitate people making inferences (full stop). He saw what he was conceding: It's not like my side of the conflict isn't biting any bullets, either. I'm saying that I'm fine with my inferences causing more harm than benefit. Isn't that monstrous of me? The last published installment says the same in the language of a battle between Feelings and Truth, in which one must be able to say what sex trans women are even if it probabilistically contributes to that person's suicide, and If you don't want to say those things because hurting people is wrong, then you have chosen Feelings. The modality of the first sentence is capacity, but the second classes declining to say such things for the sake of the person hurt as the choice the installment condemns. Here the norm admits no price, and the same installment that allowed that deceiving readers might be right treats sparing them as choosing Feelings.

Davis knew the stance was a simplification. In "Standing Under the Same Sky" he writes that Technically, as a matter of decision theory, "sacred values" are crazy, and explains why he spoke as if Truth were one anyway: those who weighed it against happiness seemed to him wildly recklessly casual about harms from covering things up, so that I felt I had to appeal to the lessons for children about how Lying Is Bad. The exceptionless form is offered, by his own account, as a correction to an opponent's error in pricing, a deliberate overstatement in the other direction. That reason is close to the slippery-slope form of the coordination argument (Chapter 4): a rule held without exception resists erosion better than one held with stated prices. It is also the place where the memoir's dispute could not be settled. Davis's opponents weighed costs he would not weigh, and he answered them with a sacred value he did not hold.

3. The scope of the standard

The exemption of Alexander is consistent with the standard. Alexander wrote in July 2019 that his blog made no claim to special authority, it's a rationalist picnic and not a rationalist monastery (Alexander 2019), and the memoir, assuming that the post was prompted by Hoffman's request that Alexander stop drawing readers in under false pretenses, says: I appreciated it. Davis kept his judgment of Alexander's arguments, telling the journalist in 2025 that he thought Alexander pretty dishonest for political reasons and less self-aware about it than Yudkowsky, and explained the difference in his anger by the difference in what each claimed: I feel much less personally aggrieved at Scott, precisely because Scott is so humble. The same distinction governs the memoir's privacy practice, and there it works less well. Davis quotes his correspondence with Alexander because I'm not particularly trying to deal reputational damage to Alexander. The rule's stated purpose was to avoid betraying private trust, and that purpose does not depend on whether the author intends harm. One reader, Ben Pace, said he wouldn't personally have shared it without permission when Davis quoted an Alexander email in the comments, adding that he did not mean that Davis necessarily should not have (Pace, comment, LW, 9 Aug 2023).

The standard's scope extended to Nate Soares, then MIRI's executive director, in the dolphin dispute of June 2021 that Chapter 4 examines for its philosophy. The memoir's later judgment of that episode is a model of the attribution discipline Davis elsewhere neglects. Of Soares he writes that I believe his self-report that he wasn't trying to make a coded statement about gender; my initial impression otherwise was miscalibrated. Of himself: my social behavior during this entire episode was histrionic, and I probably could have gotten an equal-or-better outcome if I had kept my cool. Of Alexander, in the same paragraph sequence, he asserts the motive he has just declined to assert of Soares. The difference he gives is that Soares's argument came from Alexander's essay, so the question of its origin passed to Alexander, and the hidden draft reply to Soares supplied, alongside an admission that we have a terrible track record in guessing Scott's motives, the comparison with the Glasgow Coma post as evidence (hidden page, "Dolphin War!!"). The comparison is evidence of a difference between two essays. It is weaker evidence of what Alexander would have said about dolphins in 2012.

4. The posse

Davis did not campaign alone, and the memoir's account of his allies is the fullest testimony about the group from inside it. Michael Vassar, Ben Hoffman, Jessica Taylor, and two others joined the emails of 2019 and the conversations that followed. The memoir records their positions and, in places, Davis's distance from them. The most extreme are Vassar's, some quoted from his messages and some reported in Davis's words. During the breakdown of February 2017, before the posse formed, Vassar wrote to Davis, in a message the memoir reproduces, that his critics on Facebook should be treated as violent criminals armed with technology we created. In 2019, as Davis reports the conversation, he said that Yudkowsky and Anna Salamon, Davis's friend of ten years, should be regarded as criminals or enemy combatants who could claim no rights in regard to me or him, and proposed that the group develop skill in using social-justicey blame language, as was used against us, harder, a suggestion one member called a terrifying you-have-become-the-abyss suggestion and Hoffman, by Davis's account, thought obviously good. When Davis worried about attacking named people, Vassar answered that less precise targeting is more violent.

Davis's recorded responses are those of someone who borrowed the group's courage while recoiling from its methods. Of the war framing: I don't think I got the framing at this time. War metaphors sounded scary and mean: I didn't want to shoot my friends! To Salamon he wrote that if he had become an unusually useful pawn in Michael Vassar's deranged four-dimensional hyperchess political scheming, that was arguably not his fault. When Vassar changed his mind about gender in 2020 and argued that, under present conditions, the "protected class" account of trans women had to be chosen so that people would not be silenced, Davis called it a garbage bullshit appeal to consequences and held his ally to the rule he held his opponents to. He also records his dependence: One thing I regret about my behavior during this period was the extent to which I was emotionally dependent on my posse, and in some ways particularly Michael, for validation. The unpublished sequel puts the relation more bluntly, as Chapter 9 notes: Was Michael using me, at various times? I mean, probably. But just as much, I was using him. It also records a falling-out with the group in December 2020, after he was charged with the care of a person in psychiatric crisis, failed, and said something callous; the draft reports what members of the group said to him, which this chapter does not repeat.

The question this raises is the one Yudkowsky raised, in the Discord exchange of November 2022 that Davis transcribes. Yudkowsky attributed Davis's grievances to the group's influence: the most harm they did you was to teach you to see malice where you should have seen mortality, and added the distinction that noninnocent error is meaningfully different from innocent error; and noninnocent error is meaningfully different from malice. The distinction is the one Chapter 5 built from Davis's own work, and Davis said so, pointing to "Algorithmic Intent" as his attempt at the same terminology. Where they disagreed was over what follows when a noninnocent error is pointed out and not corrected. Davis's answer, in a parable about a senior scientist who refuses to retract a paper that pleased his funder, is that an innocent mistake becomes fraud if you refuse to acknowledge it after it's been pointed out. That is the Gould criterion again, and Yudkowsky's correction of 2020 is again the fact it has to reckon with. On the causal question, whether Vassar's circle made Davis what he became, the memoir argues the counterfactual seriously. One draft counts the emails Davis sent Vassar between August 2016 and January 2017 (fourteen) to show that Vassar was one influence among many; the other concludes that, had Vassar vanished, I think I would have been just as upset about the same things for the same reasons, but with fewer allies and fewer ideas to make sense of what was going on in my social environment. Habryka's independent reading of the 2021 post, which Davis put in front of Yudkowsky in the same exchange, is evidence that the grievance did not require Vassar to be seen.

5. The author in his own record

Davis's account of himself is unusually unsparing. The memoir records, in the order of their occurrence, conduct that a more careful self-presenter would have omitted. He confronted an acquaintance in private messages about a contradiction between her public comments and what she had reported elsewhere online (Was it rude of me to confront her on the contradiction in her PMs? Yes, it was extremely rude.). He asked a trans woman staying in his apartment whether he could touch her breasts, and explains why he felt entitled to ask without excusing it (I'm not saying I was right to say it; I'm admitting that I did say it). He published a chat in which a friend said she had been getting to the point of hating trans women and he answered, in part, I hate them, too!, before she added that his campaign was changing that for her (But you're actually changing that for me). He described his 2017 Facebook campaign as a suicidally aggressive one-person culture war campaign and its arrogance as a really fun part of the æsthetic. He conceded to Salamon that his litmus test was psychologically coercive and was even willing to go up to "violent". He reported that his math in "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" was meant as an appeal to principle—and as intimidation, and that he came to describe the outcome of 2020 as successfully bullying Scott and Eliezer into clarifying. In a post of February 2020 that opens by saying the war is no excuse for being a jerk, he wrote that a soldier in his kind of war has only tactical regrets, wishing to do it over Not to inflict less pain or incur less guilt ("If in Some Smothering Dreams You Too Could Pace," UUT, 23 Feb 2020).

He applies his theory of deception to himself in the same terms he applies it to others. On sending distressed emails at four in the morning: There's some sort of game-theory algorithm in the brain that feels subjectively genuine distress (like crying or sending people too many hysterical emails) but only when it can predict that it will be rewarded with sympathy or at least tolerated. On the Newtonmas party of 2019, after which he felt better: as if by credibly threatening to ragequit, the elephant in my brain had managed to extort more bandwidth from our leadership. On a promise in the Discord server that he would probably stop posting there: It turned out that I was lying about probably not talking in the server anymore. (Hedging with the word "probably" didn't make the claim true, and of course I wasn't consciously lying, but that hardly seems exculpatory.) These are the functional attributions he makes of Yudkowsky, made of himself, and he offers them with no more hedging than he gave Yudkowsky. They answer yes, for his own case, to a question Chapter 5 left to this one, whether his advocacy discloses its filter. He writes in the draft that he was motivated to look for criticisms of Yudkowsky and dath ilan, for personal reasons outside the scope of the server, and I thought it was great for people to notice this and take it into account, and when tested he admitted that I had totally been running a biased search for criticisms. What the privacy rule removes, the private side of the exchanges, is not disclosed and cannot be, and Yudkowsky's complaint that Davis was also not reporting any facts/arguments on the other side is one the memoir reports against itself.

The record includes criticisms Davis accepted. When Yudkowsky argued, in a passage Davis reproduces at length, that someone setting out to break a rationalist would turn him toward truth-telling and away from truth-seeking and teach him anger at others' imperfect conformance to principles, Davis's response was: And you know, that's a fair criticism of me. It is unhealthy to focus on other people's errors rather than perfecting oneself! The record bears the criticism out in part. The campaign of 2018 to 2020 produced one correction, the clarification of 2020, and after it the demands for acknowledgment continued, in the memoir itself. When Yudkowsky set him a test, to say how Planecrash valorizes truth-telling as Davis understood that virtue, and the answer met silence and then a friend's criticism, Davis concluded that he had failed it and wrote that Other participants in the chatroom, and readers of this memoir, should correspondingly update their beliefs about my competence. When Lintamande told him he was impossible to talk to on his subject, because of what Davis summarizes as the bizarre hostile performative thing I did in place of truthseeking inquiry, he called it good feedback on his social behavior, and noted, with a candor that cuts both ways, that he almost wondered whether this might be a good thing from a realpolitik perspective, since the threat of thirty thousand words might deter people from spreading bad ideas. The draft of 2020 had stated the principle on which all of this rests: If you were to approve of me on the basis of factually inaccurate beliefs, then the thing of which you approve, wouldn't be me.

The costs are recorded too, scattered across eight years. A short story of January 2017 shows a whiteboard reading FRIENDS LOST OVER THIS changed from one to two ("The Counter," UUT, 2 Jan 2017); an unpublished story dated April 2021 shows it at seven (unpublished draft, "I Wish You Well"). The posts of the period speak of tearing apart ten-year friendships and having me plotting to flee my hometown ("Cloud Vision," UUT, 24 Feb 2020), of five months in constant emotional pain waging a desperate and ultimately unsuccessful behind-the-scenes email campaign ("May Is Math and Wellness Month," AAL, 1 May 2019), of a hiatus to recover from a broken heart and a shattered faith in humanity ("Hiatus," UUT, 1 May 2019), and of a laziness born out of resignation and despair, a sense that I've outlived myself ("Backlog Metablogging, April 2022," UUT, 25 Apr 2022). The memoir adds a year's sabbatical from work, a quarter of degraded performance at the job that followed, code written in a sleepless night that the company has been paying for it ever since, crying in meetings, and money spent on cheerful prices. The last installment calls itself an 85,000-word sob story about how I didn't know, and I'm not better off. The tone is self-mocking. The causes Davis assigns to these losses are his own attributions, and the whiteboard counter is a fiction's way of keeping score, but the posts written at the time report the same losses before the memoir gave them a story.

What the record collects less carefully is the cost of the campaign to others. The confrontation over private messages, the request in his apartment, the litmus test he himself called coercive, the emails sent at four in the morning, and the paid offers that mixed money with social pressure each imposed something on a person who had not asked for it. The memoir records them as episodes in its author's story and does not add them up. Disclosure does not repair them, and a commitment to truth-seeking gave Davis reasons to want his interlocutors' attention without giving him a claim on it.

6. Outrage and truth

Chapter 1 left a question for this one. In 2012 Davis renounced moral outrage and political activism, because activism was painful and did not work, and compared the outrage to an addiction he could not break; in 2016 the writing turned outward again, under the banner of truth. Was the commitment to truth the deeper thing and the anger its expression, or the anger the deeper thing and truth its acceptable form?

The memoir supplies evidence on both sides, and much of it is Davis's own. He describes the repetitiveness of his writing in 2020 as grief in another form: the rage and grief must be decomposed into obsessive and repetitive pedantry, like a tic ("Oceans Rise, Empires Fall," UUT, 30 Jun 2020). He accepts Yudkowsky's term "hate-warp" for what distorted his reading of fiction. His notes of 2019 for an unpublished post contain the line most of the ill will is on my side, and a sentence of unusual detachment about the culture war he was fighting: But from the dominant culture's perspective, it was just being itself, and the "pushback" is the aggression, and the minority started it. (unpublished draft, "Social Strategy Notes, March 2019").

The evidence on the other side is the behavior of the commitment when the anger was absent or pointed the wrong way. In December 2019 Davis published a refutation of an argument by Yudkowsky that supported his own position on the existence of distinct types of gender dysphoria, against the intuition that My childhood hero was trying to do me a favor; it would be a betrayal to reject the gift. The post itself put the principle: the entire mindset in which arguments can constitute a political favor is a confusion ("On the Argumentative Form 'Super-Proton Things Tend to Come In Varieties,'" UUT, 13 Dec 2019). The same year he published "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point," an argument for the side he opposed (Chapter 4). He rebuked Vassar's appeal to consequences when it favored a view he might have welcomed. And when the war ended in September 2020 the work went on, as he had predicted it would: not being at war actually made it emotionally easier to do clarity-creation writing. A commenter observed that this sits awkwardly with a comment Davis had linked with praise, saying that commenters should not have to consider each other's feelings (FoxLisk, comment on "If Clarity," Isso, 2 Jan 2024), and the observation is fair. The writing did go on after the war ended, though the evidence is thin, since the grievance was reopened five months later.

The evidence supports a mixed answer in which the commitment has priority without having been the only motive. The commitments of the early blog predate the dispute (Chapter 1); they operated against Davis's interest and his allies'; and the writing went on in the months after the grievance was, by his own account, settled, though they were few. What the anger explains is the method: the escalations, the four-in-the-morning emails, the paid offers that pressed busy people for attention, the vocabulary of fraud and pandering. And the two can coincide in a single act: refuting a favorable argument of Yudkowsky's in 2019 was consistent with the commitment and also served a campaign against his authority. Davis wrote in 2024 that he felt bad about how much of his writing criticized the movement, because it was boring, and that he kept telling himself that one of these years I'm going to have healed enough trauma to forget about these losers already and just read ArXiv papers ("Comment on 'Death and the Gorgon,'" AAL, 31 Dec 2024). The sentence holds both readings at once.

7. The press and the religion

The press enters the story in June 2020, when Cade Metz of the New York Times wrote to Davis about a story on the rationalists and Alexander took his blog down rather than have his surname printed. The memoir's judgment of the article that appeared in February 2021 is that it was pretty lame? and guilty of a guilt-by-association attempt, and also that its implicit charge against Alexander was, in Davis's phrase, a "Gettier attack", essentially correct on bogus evidence; Chapter 10 examines that claim. What belongs here is Davis's conduct toward the journalist his community shunned. From 2024 he met Metz repeatedly, recorded the conversations, and published transcripts, telling him at the first meeting why: I am so serious about the free speech thing that I'm willing to take the risk that if you have an honest conversation with someone, they might quote your words out of context on their blog. ("My Interview With Cade Metz on His Reporting About Slate Star Codex," LW, 26 Mar 2024). In a transcript of October 2025, asked why he went on talking to someone he did not expect to write a good book, he answered: I have this faith in Speech. Faith that if I honestly try to describe the world as I see it, then maybe someone somewhere will be able to incorporate that information into their map and use that map to steer the world and make good things happen. I'm doing the quokka policy because I don't know what else I can do. ("Conversation Among Cade Metz, Michael Vassar, Jessica Taylor, and Zack M. Davis," LW, 2 Jul 2026). The quokka is the animal of his 2020 fable of the scorpion and the fox, who carries the scorpion across the river on its promise and is stung, and might after all have been a quokka ("The Parable of the Scorpion and the Fox," AAL, 26 Jul 2020).

The community's objections to the interviews concerned loyalty and something more, and Davis met them directly. Jiro wrote that The first rule in talking to Cade Metz is do not talk to Cade Metz. Thane Ruthenis argued that participation legitimized the book and supplied quotations without changing its narrative. That objection is about journalism as much as loyalty: someone committed to public truthfulness can still believe that an interview will leave the eventual audience less informed, because the journalist selects. Davis's publication of the transcripts is his answer, and it reaches only the readers who find them. Pressed, Davis conceded the point in his own terms and declined to change course: to some quantitative extent, I'm a hazard to the community's reputation, and I don't care! I just don't care very much about covering for the community's reputation! I care about telling the truth in public (Davis, reply to Jiro, LW, 6 Jul 2026). He applied the norm of accounting against group loyalty: he acknowledged a cost that others in the community would share, judged it small, and chose to impose it. It is also, in the same comment, an admission that he would engage in some strategic concealment if "destruction" were actually at hand, a priced exception of exactly the kind the coordination argument forbids. Toward Metz himself Davis practiced the restraint he did not always practice toward Yudkowsky. When a commenter wrote that Metz was disingenuous and wanted only to deny the rationalists status, Davis replied that this Seems too self-centered to be the real explanation. and gave the journalist's interest in the story its due (Davis, reply to orthonormal, LW, 18 Aug 2025). Davis would explain the difference by the role-relative standard, since Metz did not claim to be a rationalist, but roles justify different expectations of conduct and not different standards of evidence for inferring motive. The restraint shows that Davis could apply the discipline of attribution. The cases also differ in evidence, since he had years of Yudkowsky's public statements and little of Metz's, and the comparison adds little to the argument of §2 that his confidence about Yudkowsky outran even the larger record. This section assesses Davis's interviews and his account of the controversy, not the controversy itself: Metz's articles were not read for this study, and Alexander's public account of the dispute over his name is taken up in Chapter 10.

The conversations with Metz also contain Davis's plainest assessment of the movement. I do think the rationality movement has failed. We did not build, we did not achieve the grand methods of Bayesian reasoning that we thought. He took the failure as a contingent fact about flaws in people and flaws in human nature rather than a sweeping philosophical conclusion about the futility of reason. When Metz asked whether rationalists defer to their leaders on subjects they know nothing about, he answered, Yes. It's a horrible problem., and when Metz pressed the irony, No, that is a completely fair point. Absolutely, touché. He explained his own investment in the same terms: I actually believed that Eliezer Yudkowsky was the rightful caliph. And he insisted, against the journalist's framing, on what the movement had given him: I am doing so, so much better in life than if it hadn't been there.

The religious vocabulary is Davis's own key to the story, and he used it before the journalist did. The memoir calls the community his "robot cult," Yudkowsky a religious leader, and himself a devout adherent of the religion he made up, adding that My only defense is that my religion is actually true. When Metz's 2025 article called the community's concerns about AI "near-religious," Davis objected, and Metz answered that you and so many others in the community, who have been in the community for decades in some cases, years, use the same language. They use stronger language. ("My Interview With Cade Metz on His Reporting About Lighthaven," LW, 17 Aug 2025). Davis's distinction, that the community fills the sociological niche of a religion while its beliefs are to be judged on the merits, is sound in principle, and he drew it on the record: psychologically and sociologically, it seems very comparable to a religion, even though I would say that a lot of the beliefs are true. The frame explains the memoir's intensity better than it justifies its charge. A follower who trusts a teacher the way a Catholic trusts the Pope experiences a prudent silence as apostasy, and the memoir says that from anyone else the 2018 thread would have been a solidly above-average philosophy performance. The frame also carries the critique Davis made of himself in conceding the ice-cream analogy, and the sentence in which he took leave of the movement's founder is closer to the Sequences than to apostasy: what he learned from Yudkowsky and from Greg Egan, he wrote, was an ethos that doesn't endorse blind loyalty to every author or group you learned something from, but a discerning loyalty to whatever was good in what the author or group saw in our shared universe.

8. Conclusion

The Whole Dumb Story is the corpus's most extended application of its own standards to particular people, its author included. Its method is disclosed, its quotations are accurate where they can be checked, and its record of its author's conduct is unusually unsparing, though it weighs the costs he bore more carefully than those he imposed. Its central charge is in part established. The general clause of Yudkowsky's 2018 thread departed from his published philosophy and was not withdrawn until the clarification of 2020, which, as Davis quotes it, presented the earlier statement as aimed only at a naive essentialism; the 2016 post shows at most an unexplained change of view, and the 2021 proposal understated the costs of its convention. Davis's demand that a teacher who claims a standard should say where he departs from it is the norm of accounting fairly applied, by a criterion Yudkowsky himself had stated. Davis's theory of deception should also have counted Yudkowsky's 2021 disclaimer as a partial accounting. It named the direction of the filter and its most persistent source, and then undercut its own warning with a claim of confidence; Davis praised the warning's explanation and objected to the claim, but his verdict gave the warning less weight than his own theory required. The comparisons he assembled show differential treatment consistent with political selection without excluding other explanations, and they give no support to the stronger words he used. When readers pressed him he withdrew a word and rewrote a passage, and stood by the charge of political motive.

The memoir also shows where the coordination argument operated and where it did not. It operated in the refusal to weigh harm against truth, which Davis held as a stance he knew to be a simplification. It did not operate in his treatment of the case for the defense, which he stated, priced, and rejected on its merits, or in his treatment of his own conduct, which he recorded at length. In 2020 Davis wrote that a soldier in his kind of war regrets only his tactics. The memoir he wrote afterward regrets more than that, and reports it.

Note on method

Every published installment of the memoir, both unpublished sequels, the 2020 draft, and the other posts and drafts quoted or characterized were read in full; the drafts are linked at a Gitweb commit at which each was confirmed, in October 2026, to match the copy read. The LessWrong and blog comment threads cited were read in full as retrieved in October 2026. Yudkowsky's Twitter thread of November 2018 and its replies were read in Internet Archive snapshots; his Facebook posts of 2016, 2020 and 2021 are login-walled and are cited as Davis quotes them, as are his and others' messages in the Discord exchanges that the unpublished drafts transcribe. Statements by Michael Vassar and other members of Davis's circle are identified in the text as quoted messages or as Davis's reports. Alexander's "Some Clarifications on Rationalist Blogging" (2019) was read in an Internet Archive copy. Cade Metz's articles were not read; Davis's conversations with Metz are cited from the transcripts Davis published. Bromley's work on apostates' narratives is cited from memory. Yudkowsky did not comment in the LessWrong threads of the published installments, and no public response by him to the memoir was located; his one located comment on the blog (Isso, 22 Sep 2024) concerns a scene from Planecrash and prediction markets. Jessica Taylor's 2021 LessWrong post on her time around MIRI and CFAR, and the press accounts of the community by Tom Chivers and Gideon Lewis-Kraus, were not read for this chapter. Private individuals who appear in the memoir under pseudonyms are referred to by those pseudonyms. An earlier draft of this chapter was reviewed by GPT-6-Astra (OpenAI), and a later one by Claude Fable 5.1; the chapter was revised in response to their criticisms, several of its corrections are the reviewers', and some passages adapt wording they proposed.
