Chapter 4Where to Draw the Boundaries: The Philosophy of Categorization

From 2018 to 2025 Davis argued that a category is a piece of inferential machinery and can therefore be wrong: values may decide which features a category must help predict, but the distribution of things in the world then constrains where the boundary falls. The distinction is sound, and the calculations that illustrate it are correct under their stated assumptions. The stronger claims attached to it, that other categories are "optimized for deception" and that appeals to consequences are inadmissible in arguments about definitions, do not follow, and the program's capstone essay does not say they do. What the work supports is a norm of accounting, which Davis's own coordination argument gives reason to hold strictly, provided the accounting actually reaches the people who use the word. Applied to woman, the theory leaves at least two real categories in contention, and by its own lights the dispute between them is legitimate.

1. Two sentences, seven years apart

In August 2012, filling out a workbook for a summer course on sexuality, Davis answered a question about how to define virginity this way: It's pointless to argue about the definitions of words; once you know what behavior someone has engaged in, then calling it 'virginity' or 'non-virginity' doesn't give you more information ("Contemporary," AAL, 7 Aug 2012). It is a fair statement of what Eliezer Yudkowsky had taught in his 2008 sequence "A Human's Guide to Words." Philosophers know the move as what David Chalmers calls the method of elimination, in which one eliminates use of the key term, and one attempts to determine whether any substantive dispute remains (Chalmers 2011, §3). In April 2019 the same writer ended a long essay on the philosophy of language with an instruction that seems to point the other way:

Do not ask whether there's a rule of rationality saying that you shouldn't call dolphins fish. Ask whether dolphins are fish.

"Where to Draw the Boundaries?," AAL and LW, 13 Apr 2019

The first sentence treats a dispute about category membership as a confusion to be dissolved; the second treats it as a question with an answer. (The conclusion argues that they are one position.) Between them came what the memoirs call the Category War. From 2016, Davis's arguments about transsexualism in the Bay Area rationalist community were met again and again with some version of an argument from Scott Alexander's 2014 essay "The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories": category boundaries are not facts, so nothing is wrong with drawing woman to include trans women if doing so helps people. A 2016 dialogue registers the shock: I wasn't expecting people to redefine a top-20 noun out from under my feet. ("Prescription," AAL, 28 Aug 2016). The answer occupied him for most of the next decade.

The program defends one distinction, stated more precisely each time. Values may legitimately decide which features a category must help predict; given that choice, where the boundary falls is constrained by the distribution of things in the world. Around it Davis made two stronger claims: that a category drawn for reasons other than prediction is functionally a deception, and, most plainly in 2019, that appeals to consequences are inadmissible in arguments about definitions for anyone who aspires to rationality. The second is part (c) of his position, the coordination argument (in the terms of the Introduction), applied to concepts. Neither follows from the mathematics, and the 2021 essay that completes the mathematics holds the weaker position, part (b), the norm of accounting: a departure from the predictive optimum may be justified, but it must be named. That norm is what the coordination argument actually supports (§8). The sexology belongs to Chapter 3, the general theory of deception to Chapter 5, and the dispute with Yudkowsky to Chapter 7.

2. The inheritance

Yudkowsky, 2008

"A Human's Guide to Words," some thirty posts, reduces words to inferential machinery. A concept is a compression: a short word for a conjunction of properties pays only when those properties co-occur more often than chance predicts. Of a made-up word, "wiggin," for green-eyed black-haired people, Yudkowsky concludes more bluntly than his later readers remembered:

If green-eyes and black hair have no greater than default probability to be found together, nor does any other property occur at greater than default probability along with them, then the word “wiggin” is a lie: The word claims that certain people are worth distinguishing as a group, but they’re not.

Eliezer Yudkowsky, "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace" (2008)

The source already calls a bad category a lie, so Davis's "optimized for deception" extends the sequence rather than departing from it. The sequence's most quoted example, though, is compressed to the point of assertion. In "Where to Draw the Boundary?" (singular), a speaker who insists that his list of "fish" cannot be wrong is told: Or you could stop playing nitwit games and admit that dolphins don’t belong on the fish list. The post supplies the principle, to find which things are clustered together—and sometimes, which things have a common cause, but not the argument that for dolphins the relevant clustering is by descent rather than habitat. Davis supplied one in 2019 (§4) and a deeper one in 2021 (§7).

A later 2008 post cuts the other way. Yudkowsky called a category whose boundary you draw in a way that sensitively depends on the exact values built into your utility function an "unnatural category." His examples are evaluative (lethal poison, person, murder), and he did not condemn them: That’s what moral arguments are for—for drawing and redrawing the boundaries. The post's purpose anticipates Davis's later demand: to recognize when someone tries to pull a fast one during a moral argument, by insisting that no moral argument exists, that is, when a choice is presented as a discovery. Davis's 2021 essay accepts value-laden categories of this kind and narrows the role values play in them (§6).

Alexander, 2014

Alexander's essay makes two arguments and deserves a fairer summary than its opponents gave it. Its first three sections argue that classification is relative to purpose. King Solomon, who cares whether a creature is hunted by boat or on horseback, is not wrong to file whales with the finned sea-creatures, and boundaries, like national borders, can be badly drawn for one's goals without being false. Alexander does not say that anything goes: A category “fish” containing herring, dragonflies, and asteroids is going to be stupid. The fourth section turns to transgender people. Alexander had just admitted to hospital a suicidal trans man, and concludes:

If I’m willing to accept an unexpected chunk of Turkey deep inside Syrian territory to honor some random dead guy – and I better, or else a platoon of Turkish special forces will want to have a word with me – then I ought to accept an unexpected man or two deep inside the conceptual boundaries of what would normally be considered female if it’ll save someone’s life. There’s no rule of rationality saying that I shouldn’t, and there are plenty of rules of human decency saying that I should.

Scott Alexander, "The Categories Were Made For Man, Not Man For The Categories," Slate Star Codex, 21 Nov 2014

Much of what Davis wrote on the subject can be read as an attempt to show that the fourth section does not follow from the first three. Solomon's purpose is a choice about which features to predict. Saving a patient's life is a purpose of another kind: it concerns the effect of a classification on the person classified. The border metaphor hides the shift, because real borders are drawn for their effects on the people inside them. Alexander's conclusion needs a premise about what kind of category woman is: closer to a border, partly made by agreement, than to fish. He asserted the premise through the metaphor, and Davis was right to demand an argument for it (§8 asks whether it is true). (His sixth section does gesture at the kind of empirical argument Davis says is needed, the biological correlates of transgender.) In December 2019, after what Davis's memoir describes as a long argument between them at Christmas, Alexander appended a note:

While nothing is stopping you from defining a word any way you want, you should definitely worry about it. I had hoped that the Israel/Palestine example above made it clear that you have to deal with the consequences of your definitions, which can include confusion, muddling communication, and leaving openings for deceptive rhetorical strategies. In most cases plausible definitions will be limited to a few possibilities suggested by the territory, with worsening penalties as you deviate from these further.

Alexander, editorial note added December 2019 (read in the archived copy listed in the works cited)

That is closer to Davis's position than the essay's phrasing was, though it can also be read as making explicit a qualification the essay already contained, that badly drawn boundaries have costs. Davis judged that the note still betrayed confusion, because it kept the border metaphor ("If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them," UUT, 30 Dec 2023). He was right about the metaphor, and the note concedes at least part of what his first reply was written to establish: that the territory limits which definitions are plausible.

3. The first statement (2018)

The program begins with a parable. "Blegg Mode" adds to Yudkowsky's factory, which sorts blue egg-shaped "bleggs" from red cubical "rubes," some red cubes that have been sanded and dressed in blue fur. The narrator works out that a new category is worth having when it involves cheap-to-detect features that correlate with more-expensive-to-detect features that are decision-relevant with respect to the agent's goals ("Blegg Mode," UUT, 1 Feb 2018). Three weeks later the long reply to Alexander granted his relativity point and denied that his conclusion follows:

But this is just giving up way too easily. The map is not the territory, and many very different kinds of maps can correspond to the territory in different ways—we have geographical maps, political maps, road maps, globes, &c.—but that doesn't mean no map is in error. Rationalists can't insist on using the one true categorization system, because it turns out that—in all philosophical strictness—no such thing exists. But that doesn't release us from our sacred duty to describe what's actually true. It just leaves us faced with the slightly more complicated task of describing the costs and benefits of different categorization systems with respect to different criteria.

"The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions," UUT, 23 Feb 2018

The device that makes this possible, and that remains Davis's signature, is the subspace. Whales cluster with mammals in the dimensions along which mammals resemble each other, and with fish in the dimensions of life in water, with no contradiction. Choosing a subspace reflects interests; once it is chosen, the clustering is not up to us. Hence a modest program for disputes over words: Before shrugging and saying, "Well, this is a difference in values; nothing more to be said about it," we can talk about the detailed consequences of what is gained or lost by paying attention to some differences and ignoring others.

The application to gender is more careful than the essay's reputation. Davis grants that a natal female who presents to the world as a man, and whom other people model as a man on a System 1 level with no apparent incongruities, might be said to be a man in the sense of social gender (but not in the sense of "biologically male adult human"), because that's the mental category that people are actually using for him: It works because and to the extent that transitioning actually works. His further claim, explicitly conditional on the two-type taxonomy that Chapter 3 assesses, is that most trans women in Western countries aren't just not part of the female cluster in configuration space; they're specifically part of male cluster along most dimensions. The essay's scatterplot of fictitious data states the value-dependence in its caption: People who care more about predicting x₁ and x₂ have reason to prefer categories and corresponding language that group by natal sex (blue category boundary); people who care more about predicting x₃ have reason to prefer categories and language that group by gender identity (red boundary). That caption is the whole later dispute in miniature (§8).

The companion "Reply to The Unit of Caring on Adult Human Females" argues that a self-identification definition has no base case, and asks for something modest: Even if many usages of words like woman can and should be interpreted in a trans-inclusive sense, it's important that it also be possible to sometimes use the words in a trans-exclusive sense in those cases where the distributions of trans people and cis people of a given ‘gender’ differ significantly for the variables of interest. Asked whether, given perfect physical transition in a two-type world, such people should be called women, Davis answers: Honestly, I lean towards Yes, but I can at least see the argument of someone who preferred not to use the word that way. ("Reply to The Unit of Caring…," UUT, 19 Apr 2018). Yet the February essay that granted the social-gender sense also called the self-identification convention a route to universal socially-mandatory lies. The careful position and the polemical one were present from the start.

The first critics

When "Blegg Mode" was cross-posted to LessWrong in March 2019, the commenter gjm gave it its most searching reading. The "cheap-to-detect" heuristic, he wrote, has the look, to me, of a principle that's constructed in order to reach a particular conclusion, and he offered counterexamples (isotopes, enantiomers, types of supernova) in which categories were refined because a difference mattered, cheap to detect or not (gjm, comment on "Blegg Mode," 13 Mar 2019). Jessica Taylor made the point from the theory of inference: a Bayesian inference method isn't going to assign special importance to observed features, as it treats the observations as causally downstream of the ontological reality rather than causally upstream of it (Jessica Taylor, 13 Mar 2019). Davis conceded (Yeah, I might have been on the wrong track there.; 14 Mar 2019), and observational cost drops out of the later theory, returning only as a reason to coordinate on simple tests (§5).

gjm also stated the opposing view more precisely than Alexander had: Some categorizations are better than others, but there are often multiple roughly-equally-good categorizations and picking one of those rather than another is not an epistemological error. (13 Mar 2019). Davis replied that, given an agent's goals, such ties would be a shocking coincidence (14 Mar 2019). The question of ties was not settled (§8).

4. Two kinds of value-dependence (2019)

"Where to Draw the Boundaries?" is the hinge of the corpus; its plural title answers Yudkowsky's singular. It is a dialogue whose interlocutor speaks, Davis later disclosed, in unattributed sentences from Alexander, Yudkowsky, and Rob Bensinger (§9). It reconciles Yudkowsky's verdict on dolphins with Alexander's by way of subspaces, and states what rationalists mean when they say a definition can be wrong:

When rationalists say that definitions can be wrong, we don't mean that there's a unique category boundary that is the True floating essence of a word, and that all other possible boundaries are wrong. We mean that in order for a proposed category boundary to not be wrong, it needs to capture some statistical structure in reality, even if reality is surprisingly detailed and there can be more than one such structure.

To the interlocutor's claim that a joint is a joint only because we care about it, Davis replies: Everything we identify as a joint is a joint not "because we care about it", but because it helps us think about the things we care about. He spells it out:

Which dimensions of Thingspace you bother paying attention to might depend on your values, and the clusters returned by your brain's similarity-detection algorithms might "split" or "collapse" according to which subspace you're looking at. But in order for your map to be useful in the service of your values, it needs to reflect the statistical structure of things in the territory—which depends on the territory, not your values.

An unpublished draft of this period names the distinction in its title, "Two Kinds of Value-Dependence". The first kind of value-dependence is a choice of subspace, of which features to predict. The second would be a choice of boundary within it for reasons other than predicting those features. Davis holds that the first is legitimate and the second is not, and characterizes the second precisely: There is an important difference between having a utility function defined over a statistical model's performance against specific real-world data (even if another mind with different values would be interested in different data), and having a utility function defined over features of the model itself.

Two arguments carry the weight. The first is a dilemma about redefinition. Let "Foos" cluster near [1, 2, 3] and "Quuxes" near [2, 4, 6]. Someone who redefines "Foo" to cover both, but on seeing a first coordinate of 2 still predicts 4 and 6, is not using the new concept; someone who was would predict 3 and 4.5, the merged means. The argument assumes, as the post says, that within a category the coordinates are conditionally independent, so that the category alone carries the prediction; a broader category that kept the internal structure would be the post's other case, in which the knowledge is still represented, under another description. The cluster-structure knowledge that "entities for which x₁≈2, also tend to have x₂≈4 and x₃≈6" needs to be represented somewhere in the one's mind in order to get the right answer. The post grants that there isn't going to be a unique way to encode the knowledge into natural language; what a redefinition cannot do is make the knowledge unnecessary. The argument thus places a burden on redefinition, that the useful distinctions remain recoverable somewhere, without requiring that they keep their old names. It also says what the 2008 dolphin verdict was for: to maximize the probability a world-model assigns to observations of dolphins, you need to take into consideration the many aspects of reality in which the grouping {monkeys, squirrels, dolphins, horses ...} makes more sense. The second argument is a calculation about title inflation. If half of the low-paid jobs are given the title "Vice President," the mutual information between title and pay falls from about 0.918 bits to about 0.252, and all that's actually been accomplished is making language less useful. Both figures are correct (see the note on method), for a distribution in which the title predicts only pay; a real change of titles might serve duties, authority or standing that the calculation leaves out.

The post also gives the argument from arbitrariness to indifference its canonical form: If the choice were really arbitrary, you would be satisfied with the choice being made arbitrarily: by flipping a coin, or calling a random number generator. (It doesn't matter which.) Whatever criterion your brain is using to decide which word or belief you want, is your non-arbitrary reason. That defeats appeals to arbitrariness made to block an objection; against someone who says openly that the data underdetermine the choice and gives a reason, it has no force. Whether the reason is legitimate is what the distinction between the two kinds of value-dependence is supposed to settle.

Wei Dai pressed the hardest constructive question. Grouping by a few dimensions is sometimes exactly right, as when a biologist hypothesizes that grey skin and large ears let prey escape a new predator. So: Since it doesn't seem to make sense to never use a word to point to a cluster in a "thin" subspace, what is your advice for when it's ok to do this or accept others doing this? (Wei Dai, comment on "Where to Draw the Boundaries?," 21 Apr 2019). Davis was candid about the limit of his account: I wish I had a rigorous reduction of what "relevant in the context" means, but I don't. (29 Apr 2019). Much depends on that admission. The distinction is clean in the abstract. In application, deciding which dimensions are relevant to a context is where the substantive disagreements live, and that decision belongs to the first kind of value-dependence, the legitimate kind. §8 draws the consequence.

5. Coordination: the self-imposed qualification

Davis's next essay complicated his position to his opponents' advantage, which counts against gjm's suspicion of reverse-engineering. "Schelling Categories, and Simple Membership Tests" starts from the "univariate fallacy": two distributions can overlap heavily on each of forty variables taken alone and still be almost perfectly separable when all forty are taken together ("The Univariate Fallacy," AAL and LW, 15 Jun 2019). Add a forty-first binary variable that is individually more diagnostic than any of the others but irrelevant to the decisions at stake. If agents must coordinate and each can observe only one variable, they may do best to define their shared category by the forty-first:

the right categories to use given constraints on communication and observation, might be different from the category boundaries you would draw from a "God's eye view", in part because consideration of which categories are easy for different agents to coordinate on is relevant, not just raw information-theoretic expressive power.

"Schelling Categories, and Simple Membership Tests," AAL and LW, 25 Aug 2019

So an age cutoff that everyone can compute stands in for the illegible category of maturity, at the cost that a simple test is subject to adversarial Goodhart. Davis then turned the apparatus against his own side, in a post that announces an apparently novel argument in favor of the self-identity criterion. When observers disagree about someone's sex and social life needs one answer, the obvious focal point is to ask the person, and then there's no ambiguity about how to classify anyone. Pure natal sex and pure self-report are both stable conventions; compromises are not, because any line not drawn on a sufficiently sticky Schelling point will face constant attempts to push it up or down the slippery slope ("Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point," UUT, 6 Oct 2019). "More Schelling" explains why the fight is over words rather than policies: changing what a common word picks out means imposing on all the downstream predictions and decisions people were already using that category/word for ("More Schelling," UUT, 22 Dec 2019).

These essays concede the most. They concede that coordination can legitimately shape a boundary, and they supply a reason to prefer self-identification that has nothing to do with sparing feelings; when Raymond Arnold, a LessWrong moderator, later complained of a hole in the arguments concerning "but, like, coordination tho" (11 Jan 2021), Davis could rightly point to them. A third criterion comes from model selection: we should not posit more explanatory entities (such as categories) unless they can pay rent in better predictions ("On the Argumentative Form 'Super-Proton Things Tend to Come in Varieties,'" UUT, 13 Dec 2019; worked through in code in "Message Length," AAL and LW, 19 Oct 2020). By 2019 the program thus had three criteria for a good category (fit in a chosen subspace, economy, coordinability), of which the polemics seldom mention the last two.

6. The deception thesis (2020–2021)

"Where to Draw the Boundaries?" ends its argument with a parenthesis: (Oh, and if you're actually trying to optimize other people's models of the world, rather than the world itself—you could just lie, rather than playing clever category-gerrymandering mind games. It would be a lot simpler!) Over the next eighteen months Davis built a theory of meaning and deception, on the sender–receiver games of David Lewis and Brian Skyrms (Chapter 5), meant to show that this was more than a quip. Its consequence here is that meaning lives in a convention, so that redefining a word is an attempt to change a convention others rely on, and whether it misleads depends on whether listeners still decode the word the old way.

"Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception"

The capstone essay (AAL and LW, 8 January 2021) is Davis's most important work on the subject. It begins by refusing the border metaphor (Countries aren't Bayesian networks. They just aren't.). A category is a probabilistic model, the central node of a naïve Bayes network, and the drawn "boundary" is a picture of the model, so you can't "redraw the boundaries" associated with a communication signal without messing with the model that generates them, which means messing with your predictions about the real world.

The main technical advance answers a question the 2019 framing could not: what is wrong with a squiggly boundary, given that a region splitting the probability mass in half carries one bit whatever its shape? When answers can be nearly right, a compact category leaves less expected error. Learning whether a number from 1 to 8 is odd or even cuts the expected squared error only from 5.25 to 5; learning whether it is in 1–4 or 5–8 cuts it to 1.25. The essay's factory example makes the same point with a gerrymandered "blegg*" scheme. Values enter through the choice of space and metric: But given a metric on the variables that you care about predicting and using to inform predictions, which categories are cognitively useful depends on the the distribution of data in the world. You can't define a word any way you want. The dependence on values is a specific kind of lawful dependence between your values and the distribution of data in the world, not an atomic preference for using a particular encoding for its own sake. Yudkowsky's unnatural categories are absorbed and narrowed: poison is squiggly in chemical space but compact in a space of effects on human health, so values may choose value-laden dimensions, but not the boundary within the space they have chosen.

Then the title thesis. Davis imagines a factory engineer with a stake in a supplier that is paid more for each object labeled a blegg. Under a score of "squared error minus supplier revenue" the gerrymandered categories win (−151.57 against −142.62), and such a score lies on a continuum whose far end is deception pure and simple. Hence: there's no functional difference between saying "I reserve the right to lie p% of the time about whether something belongs to category C", and adopting a new, less-accurate category system that misclassifies p% of instances with respect to the old system. The opening generalizes: Agents that communicate in ways that systematically depart from efficient encodings are better modeled as trying to deceive each other or wirehead themselves.

What the essay does with this classification matters more than its title suggests. It does not forbid the departure (Depending on your goals, deception can be rational!); it asks that the departure be recognized: But in order to help you decide whether deceiving people is the right thing to do, it helps to notice that what you're doing is deceiving people. Its most original section concerns mimicry: a Batesian mimic matches its model in a subspace (wing markings) and differs elsewhere (toxicity), so it deceives where the unmatched features matter and is harmless where they do not. The essay ends on meat substitutes. Where the advocate wants substitutes called meat to reduce animal suffering, Davis writes: I can't prove that that's not the right the answer to the decision problem of what verbal behavior to perform. The stakes are genuinely high. Departing from the structure of language extracts a price, but If, empirically, being generous about what counts as "meat" can prevent massive suffering (by altering the social defaults around consumption behavior), then maybe that's the right thing to do. The essay thus separates the laws governing maps from decisions about what to say, which may weigh consequences provided they are seen for what they are; its theoretical conclusion is that the departure be recognized by the one who makes it. Telling the listener, which the norm of accounting adds, is a further step the essay points toward without taking. Its title, and a closing parable about a "Great Teacher" who would not egregiously mislead people about the philosophy of language when being egregiously misleading happens to be politically convenient, carry a stronger charge than its argument.

The rule against appeals to consequences, and its critics

The stronger claim had been stated fifteen months earlier, as a rule about admissible arguments. Against Alexander's "Against Lie Inflation," which had argued that stretching "lie" to cover motivated reasoning would make everyone angrier, Davis replied that Appeals to consequences are invalid because they represent a map–territory confusion, an attempt to optimize our description of reality at the expense of our ability to describe reality accurately (which we need in order to actually optimize reality). The same post makes the rule a condition of membership: as aspiring epistemic rationalists, we're not allowed to care about which descriptions sound nice; we're only allowed to care about which descriptions match reality. ("Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist," AAL and LW, 14 Oct 2019). Even here consequences keep a place on the side of speech: if calling someone a liar would make him angry, that's an argument that I have an incentive to lie about whether I think Elijah is lying! The memoir records the intuition behind the rule in 2020: for squiggly boundaries, the only reason you would realistically want to use them would be to commit fraud.

LessWrong's 2019 Review, in the winter of 2020–21, tested the rule hardest. The commenter TAG argued that socially constructed categories (money, marriage) are useful without reflecting pre-existing structure. Davis listed coordination, deception, and self-fulfilling prophecy as complications (of the last, I'm not sure I understand the theory of this yet) and held that otherwise it's not the map's job to provide compensatory usefulness; the map's job is to reflect the territory. (13 Dec 2020). TAG asked why coordination and self-fulfilling prophecy were not already exceptions (31 Dec 2020). The MIRI researcher Abram Demski, Davis's ablest defender, argued that a norm against appeals to consequences is a good Schelling fence for a community of truth-seekers, and was exact about the kind of claim that was: My argument in support of Zack's argument appeals to consequences, but does so in service of the normative question of whether a community of truth-seekers should adopt norms against appeals to consequences. (28 Dec 2020). Arnold asked why Davis did not simply call the prohibition rule-consequentialist (21 Jan 2021).

In private correspondence Davis went further. In January 2021, the month "Unnatural Categories" appeared, he wrote to Demski, as his memoir records, that Demski had been right to ask whether boundaries should ideally be drawn with an eye to consequences: In that light, it made sense to regard "have accurate beliefs" as merely a convergent instrumental subgoal, rather than what rationality is about—as sacrilegious as that felt to type. The memoir adds a reservation: And yet, somehow, "have accurate beliefs" seemed more fundamental than other convergent instrumental subgoals like "seek power and resources". This is reported private correspondence, and the assessment in §8 does not rest on it.

Other responses to "Unnatural Categories" were few. In 2025 the commenter tslarm asked: If I'm honest about the boundaries of my new category system, how is this deceptive? (14 Jun 2025). Davis granted that whether it's "lying" to use a category label depends on what the label is construed to "canonically" mean (8 Jul 2025). A counterexample came from Wei Dai in 2024: a "neural fireplace" that induces the experience of a fire. "Trans women are women" might work similarly, as a signal of local values, trying to imply something like "people around here do not distinguish between trans women and cis women in their values, at least in most circumstances" (Wei Dai, 18 Jan 2024). Davis answered with the norm, not the theorem: The thesis of this post is that people who think neural fireplaces are fireplaces should be arguing for that on the merits (19 Jan 2024). Wei Dai's rejoinder, that some goals, like being treated with care by friends, are achieved by the friends' agreement itself, went unanswered (20 Jan 2024).

7. From clusters to causes (2021–2025)

The third phase begins with a retraction. When the blogger tailcalled objected to Davis's 2016 claim that summarizing correlations is the entire point of a taxonomy, Davis replied under the title "You Are Right and I Was Wrong": Different causal theories can generate the same correlations in a particular set of observations, while still making meaningfully and drastically different claims about the world. (UUT, 1 Feb 2021). A category, on the revised view, earns its keep by tracking causal structure that generalizes.

The occasion came four months later, when Nate Soares, MIRI's executive director, tweeted that excluding dolphins from "fish" required staggering definitional gymnastics, and Yudkowsky, who had called the opposite view nitwit games in 2008, retweeted it. Davis's "Reply to Nate Soares on Dolphins" (AAL and LW, 9 Jun 2021) and its sequel "Blood Is Thicker Than Water" give the argument Yudkowsky had compressed. Phylogeny is not an aesthetic preference, because evolution happened. On Davis's model the genome sits at the root of the causal graph of an organism's traits, so that conditioning on ancestry renders many downstream traits approximately independent, which is what makes a category node worth having; how good the approximation is, and at which taxonomic rank, the argument leaves to the biology. The cluster picture becomes an illustration: "There's conditional independence structure in the causal graph" is a meaningfully deeper explanation than "There's a cluster in configuration space", because conditional independence is what what makes it possible to construct a "space" such that there are clusters. ("Blood Is Thicker Than Water 🐬," AAL and LW, 27 Sep 2021). The first reply grants Soares more than its tone suggests: a short word for swimmy creatures is reasonable, and so is using fish for it if everyone knows what you mean. Its objection is to abolishing the narrower sense. It ends on an insinuation: Could there, perhaps, be some additional context to this conversation that Soares neglected to make explicit? That seems unlikely, however.

The reception did not bear the insinuation out. Soares answered that he had been self-consciously shitposting, that the fragment he endorsed was be wary of those who say they are here to refine your concepts, for sometimes they are here to steal them (Nate Soares, 10 Jun 2021), and gave object-level reasons for his change of mind (11 Jun 2021). Vaniver checked divergence dates and found guppies more closely related to dolphins than to sharks: The trouble with defending the 2008 classification is not that it's phylogenetics, it's that, as far as I can tell, it's bad phylogenetics. (16 Jun 2021). And cata suggested that the political mapping Davis assumed might run the other way: For me, "dolphins are fish because they swim and look fishy" is associated with a "common sense clusters" classification strategy, which also gives a traditional common-sense idea of who is a man and who is a woman. (16 Jun 2021). If so, the classification by appearance that makes dolphins fish is the one that makes a passing trans woman a woman, and Davis's dolphin stance and his gender stance pull in opposite directions (§8).

On "Blood Is Thicker," Alexander asked whether, during a long stretch of convergent evolution, you're allowed to categorize them however you want based on which is more useful for you? If the latter, what makes you think we're not in that period right now? (Scott Alexander, 8 Oct 2021). Davis conceded the principle: You should categorize them whichever way is more useful for making predictions and decisions in whatever context you're operating in. (12 Oct 2021). The pattern recurs throughout the program. A critic says that several categories are legitimate and use picks among them, and Davis replies that this was already in the post. It usually was, which locates the real disagreement elsewhere: in costs left unacknowledged, in what a word already means, or in which category gets the default.

The late essays state the mature theory most clearly. "Beyond the Binary" explains why discrete markers make good definitions though they do little everyday inferential work: Discrete features make for better word definitions than high-dimensional statistical regularities, even if most of the everyday inferential utility of using the word comes from the high-dimensional statistical correlates. The essay admits its cost, biting the bullet on masculine women and feminine men being in some sense less "real" women and men, respectively, and of how the map might describe such outliers without bulldozing them, ends and I still can't. Let me know if you figure it out. ("Beyond the Binary," UUT, 12 Dec 2023). "A Generic Ontological Finickiness," answering Yudkowsky's suggestion that concern for accurate description is usually a rationalization, concedes the selectivity critics had complained of, which gjm had put most sharply in 2022 as Davis's own theory of selective reporting turned on him (it's possible for one's thinking to be made worse overall by a process of correcting errors, if the choice of which errors has enough bias in it; gjm, 14 Mar 2022). Davis owns the selection: I selfishly focus on the topics that are important to me. The essay ends: Maybe human beings don't always, inherently, consistently care about exact descriptive accuracy—but rationalists should. (UUT, 14 Sep 2025).

The academic neighbors

Davis rarely situates this work in academic philosophy, but it has close neighbors there. The causal turn puts him near Richard Boyd's "homeostatic property clusters," kinds that are, in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's summary, families of such properties that are contingently clustered in nature (Bird and Tobin 2022, §1.2.2); "the root of the causal graph" resembles what Boyd calls homeostasis, though Boyd's account allows several mechanisms maintaining a cluster and does not require one root cause. The dolphin argument meets the debate over biological pluralism, and Davis's thicker-subspace argument is a reason to prefer phylogeny for the widest range of inferences, not a refutation of pluralism, and his concessions to Alexander and to aphyer (who defended both the cook's and the botanist's "fruit") show that he did not claim otherwise.

On the gender question, the nearest academic statement of Davis's conclusion is Alex Byrne's thesis that S is a woman iff S is an adult human female (Byrne 2021, §1, restating Byrne 2020); Davis names Byrne as a fellow philosopher in the 2025 essay. Their methods differ. Byrne argues about what woman means; Davis argues about which category does the most inferential work, joined to an empirical claim about present usage (§8). On the other side, Sally Haslanger's "ameliorative" analysis states the position Davis attacks more candidly than any of his interlocutors did: the world by itself can’t tell us what gender is, or what race is; it is up to us to decide what in the world, if anything, they are. (Haslanger 2000, 34). And Chalmers, discussing such projects under Peirce's name "the ethics of terminology," gives the reason "More Schelling" gives for why disputes over words matter: for nonideal agents such as ourselves, the accepted meaning for a key term will make a difference to which concepts are highlighted, which questions can easily be raised, and which associations and inferences are naturally made (Chalmers 2011, §8). Davis reached these positions without citing that literature, which had already met objections he first encountered in comment threads.

8. Assessment

What holds

The distinction itself. That values pick the space and the metric while the data constrain the clusters is standard in statistics and machine learning. "Constrain" is the right word. Even given a space and a metric, clustering is not unique: Jon Kleinberg proved that for a set of three simple properties — essentially scale-invariance, a richness requirement that all partitions be achievable, and a consistency condition on the shrinking and stretching of individual distances — we show that there is no clustering function satisfying all three (Kleinberg 2002). Kleinberg's theorem concerns clustering procedures in general; gjm's "multiple roughly-equally-good categorizations" makes the related point about particular data, and Davis's model-selection criterion (§5) answers the residual freedom only in part. Within these limits the distinction stands, and it exposes the gap in Alexander's essay, which moves from a premise about the first kind of value-dependence to a conclusion that needs an unargued premise about what kind of category woman is. Both Alexander and Yudkowsky eventually conceded the narrow point. Yudkowsky's September 2020 Facebook clarification (login-walled; quoted as Davis quotes it) said that you are being the bad guy if you try to shut down that conversation by saying that "I can define the word 'woman' any way I want".

The piggyback diagnosis. Since words have no intrinsic meanings, Davis argues, there is no reason to redefine an existing word except to piggyback off the meaning people are already using that signal for, which is not in itself deceptive. Critics heard mind-reading, but practitioners avow the strategy. Haslanger: I think there are rhetorical advantages to using the terms ‘gender’, ‘man’ and ‘woman,’ and ‘race’ for the concepts I’ve defined, but if someone else is determined to have those terms, I’ll use different ones. (Haslanger 2000, 52). Applied to pronouns in 2022, the diagnosis has real force: if she and he were mere alternatives with no difference in meaning, then there would be no reason to care which one someone used, as long as the referent was clear ("Challenges to Yudkowsky's Pronoun Reform Proposal," UUT, 13 Mar 2022). The argument shows that the pronoun carries something people care about. It does not show that what they care about is the descriptive content in dispute, since a pronoun can be valued as recognition or courtesy, which Davis's own remark that pronouns can be meaningful without being denotative allows. LessWrong's administrator, Oliver Habryka, while doubting that LessWrong was the right venue for the discussion, found basically everything in this post obviously true (Oliver Habryka, 13 Mar 2022).

The stronger claims

The stronger claims are not stated in one place. They appear as a rule in 2019 ("Appeals to consequences are invalid"; "we're not allowed to care"), as an intuition recorded for 2020 (a squiggly boundary's only realistic use is fraud), as a classification in 2021 (systematic departure from efficient encoding is better modeled as deception), and in polemics that apply the word "lies" to conventions and rationales: "universal socially-mandatory lies" (2018); the Great Teacher (2021); You don't have to invent absurd lies to make it look like the convention that happens to be dominant in your local social environment has no costs. (2022, in an essay its author describes as written with as much vitriolic rhetoric as the target audience is likely to tolerate). Two claims are left to assess: that a departure is a deception, and that consequences are inadmissible in arguments about definitions.

Departure as deception. Lying p% of the time and misclassifying p% of instances behave identically when the listener decodes the word under the old convention and the two produce the same expectations conditional on the signal, which is how Davis's example of a fraudulent receipt sets them up; equal error rates alone would not make them equivalent. That condition is part of the claim, as the reply to tslarm grants, and it marks where the claim has real application: a convention requiring speakers to apply a word against what they and their listeners perceive makes the word misleading as decoded. The 2018 complaint that a sticker can only incentivize them to lie has this case in view. Two kinds of case fall outside it. An announced departure, like fish for swimmy creatures if everyone knows what you mean, misleads no one who hears the announcement. And pronouns, as Davis insists against Yudkowsky, can have meaningful semantics without being denotative statements that can be straightforwardly "false"; refusing one does not constitute a philosophical commitment that pronouns can be "lies" as such. The 2022 formulation is more careful than the 2018 one, and it is the one the theory supports.

The rule against appeals to consequences. As a rule for belief it is sound: what is true does not depend on what it would be good to believe. As a rule for definitions it is exactly as sharp as the distinction between the two kinds of value-dependence, since choosing which features to predict is not, on Davis's account, an appeal to consequences at all. Davis admitted he had no rigorous reduction of contextual relevance (§4), and TAG's question was fair: coordination and self-fulfilling categories are cases where what a category does to its users bears on how it should be drawn, and Davis had granted both.

The rule can still be defended as a rule. Demski, Arnold, and the January 2021 concession all treat it as a norm justified by its effects on a community of inquirers. Rule-consequentialists hold that such a norm may properly be followed even where breaking it would do better, because, in Brad Hooker's summary, a rule hedged with exceptions will provide too much opportunity for temptation to convince agents that one of the exception clauses applies, when in fact stealing would be advantageous to the agent (Hooker 2023, §8). Davis's coordination argument has this structure. Modeling a coalition choosing how much map accuracy to trade for grant money, he concluded that In the absence of distinguished salient intermediate points along the uniformly continuous trade-off between maximally accurate world-models and sucking up to the Emperor, the only Schelling points are x = ∞ (tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth) and x = 0 (do everything short of outright lying to win grants). ("Speaking Truth to Power Is a Schelling Point," LW, 30 Dec 2019). If every departure from accuracy is a step onto that slope, the rule must be exceptionless.

The question is which rule the argument supports. The slope runs along one dimension, the accuracy of the shared map, and a departure moves a community down it only if the map loses what the departure hides. A disclosed departure hides nothing at the moment of disclosure. A community that says "we use fish for water animals in this regulation, and dolphins are mammals," or "we use woman here for social gender, and developmental sex is a different matter," keeps both facts on the map; it has reallocated a short word, at a cost in message length that Davis's own apparatus can measure. Whether it stays disclosed is a question about uptake: the qualification must reach the listeners, travel with the word, and remain usable in ordinary speech, which is the kind of question "More Schelling" raises about all the downstream uses of a word. The argument's force falls on undisclosed departures, which degrade the map while seeming not to, and on disclosures that do not survive circulation. The rule it supports is a rule against concealment, and it is easier to enforce than Davis's: whether a speaker has stated a reason is observable, though whether he stated all of it is not, while whether his reason was predictive is a fact about motives, which Chapter 5 shows to be hard to establish. (Nor does the slope argument show that a changed convention must be unstable: "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point" found self-report to be one of the two stable conventions.) The September 2020 email to Yudkowsky that closed the first phase of the Category War says that the impossibility of defining woman any way one likes is totally compatible with the claim that trans women are women, and trans men are men, and nonbinary people are nonbinary! It's just that you have to argue for why those categorizations make sense in the context you're using the word, rather than merely asserting it with an appeal to arbitrariness. An unpublished outline puts it in one line: It helps to be explicit about when you're being normative, and when you think you're describing the territory. (unpublished draft, "The View From Nowhere"). The coordination argument licenses the norm of accounting, held as strictly as Davis wished to hold his rule, with the condition that accounting counts only when it succeeds in communicating.

Conventional and interactive kinds

The objection with the widest reach came early, from Ozy Brennan in 2018. Ozy granted Davis's main point (There are two different senses of the word “useful.”) but argued that social gender is like money, which is money because everyone agrees that it is. "You're a woman if you identify as a woman" is then a criterion for membership, not a definition, so the decision of who should be socially gendered male or female, and how many social genders we should have is not an epistemic decision. This decision can and should be made on purely utilitarian grounds. (Brennan, "Man Should Allocate Some More Categories," Thing of Things, 18 Jun 2018).

Davis's reply is better than his critics usually allow. Not everything can serve as money, and by Gresham's law, where custom requires that defaced Canadian dollars pass as American, people will privately discount them: They will be lying. His conclusion is carefully limited: I happily agree that fully consensual gender is a coherent position. That doesn't make it feasible. ("Reply to Ozymandias on Fully Consensual Gender," UUT, 20 Dec 2019). That is an argument about feasibility, and fiat paper's journey from absurdity to universality shows the analogy cuts both ways. The academic form of Ozy's proposal is Ásta's conferralism, on which gender is a status conferred by others. Her schema includes a slot for the base property, what the subjects are attempting to track (consciously or not), if anything (Mikkola 2022, §2.3, summarizing Ásta 2018). Davis's reply, which finds Ozy's predictions from social gender largely tied to other people's perceptions of sex, amounts to the claim that for social gender the slot is filled, by perceived sex, and that a convention severing the conferral from its base will be privately discounted.

The deeper version of Ozy's point concerns what Ian Hacking called interactive kinds, where, as the encyclopedia summarizes him, in social contexts the object of classification itself changes in being classified. Social gender is close to a paradigm case, and the self-fulfilling categories Davis twice said he did not yet understand are the program's largest unfinished business. The best reply, which Demski made against TAG, has an academic version: The difference between natural kinds and social kinds is a difference of the degree of control we have over the object of classification. (Khalidi 2010, as summarized in Bird and Tobin 2022). Constructs are part of the territory and can be mapped well or badly, which answers TAG. It does not restore the rule against appeals to consequences. Where we have control, a proposed category is partly a proposal about what to make true, and arguing for it is legitimately arguing about consequences.

Two categories, and the negotiation between them

At least two real categories stand near the word woman. One is developmental sex, which "Blood Is Thicker" and "Beyond the Binary" argue buys conditional independence across many downstream traits and which no current intervention changes. The other is social gender, the class a person is treated as belonging to, which supports the predictions Ozy lists (pronouns, harassment, expectations about childcare) and includes successfully transitioned people. Davis grants it in 2018 (a man in the sense of social gender), again in an unpublished draft (she is obviously the correct pronoun for people who look like women!; hidden page, "Dolphin War!! 🐬"), and in the pronoun essay, which rests on speakers' assigning pronouns by perceived sex. Perceived sex is not, in Davis's framework, a third category: as Taylor put it in 2019, observations are downstream of the latent variable, so perception estimates developmental sex, and social gender is the interactive kind built on the estimate. Perception is still worth studying in its own right where treatment follows it, and "social gender" gathers appearance, treatment, pronouns and expectations without showing that they form one category; the classification each side proposes has to be specified before the dispute between them can be judged.

By the program's own lights, then, which category gets the short word is a question of the first kind of value-dependence: which inferences the speech community most needs the word to carry. The 2018 scatterplot pictures exactly this, and its caption says that people who care about different variables have reason to prefer different boundaries. The framework therefore classifies the dispute over woman as a disagreement about relevance, the legitimate kind, not as gerrymandering. That conclusion is favorable to Davis's framework and unfavorable to his polemic. In Plunkett and Sundell's terms the dispute is a metalinguistic negotiation, one that employs competing metalinguistic usages of an expression, and that reflects a disagreement about the proper deployment of linguistic representations (Plunkett and Sundell 2013, 3). Such disputes are genuine, because metalinguistic negotiations influence and advance more general processes of collective decision-making and action (17).

Davis's strongest reply is that the categories are not symmetric. Social gender is conferred on the basis of perceived sex, which estimates developmental sex, so where they come apart the estimate has misfired, and the uncatalogued decisions people make with a top-20 noun include some where the misfire matters. The reply is why the program is not refuted. But it rests on an empirical claim about what speakers use the word for: that their downstream decisions track the developmental category more than the social one. For questions about reproduction, and medical questions that turn on developmental history, that is plainly true. For much of ordinary social life, on Davis's own 2018 account of social gender and his 2022 account of pronouns, the social category does the work. This is cata's point. The classification by appearance that Davis rejects for dolphins is the one he accepts for pronouns, and the difference between the cases has to be argued from what each word is used for.

Ozy's reductio shows where the sexology enters. Ozy aimed it at Davis's appeal to psychological differences: if a group whose traits differ on average from those of women as a whole is not women, then By extension, lesbians are not women. Davis's long unpublished reply to Ozy, which concedes much of the principle (In all philosophical strictness, I think I agree.), answers with the univariate point: a tall woman might be more male-typical than female-typical in the one particular aspect of their height and female-typical in the conjunction of everything else (unpublished draft, "I Mean, Yes, I Agree That Man Should Allocate Some More Categories, But"). (The draft leaves placeholders where it meant to say more about lesbians.) For developmental and bodily dimensions the answer needs no sexology: lesbians are developmentally female and trans women are not. For the psychological dimensions, where Ozy aimed, it needs evidence about psychological distributions, which the two-type hypothesis supplies in one form, since the claim that most trans women are male-typical in the conjunction of psychological traits is the 2018 conditional. Even that evidence would not settle the normative half of Ozy's objection, which properties govern membership. The categorization argument depends on the contested science there and only there, and that is the question Chapter 3 inherits.

Davis's arguments for his preferred default (the thicker subspace, the better definition, present usage, the tax on uncatalogued decisions) are the right kinds of argument for a metalinguistic negotiation, and some are good. They show that woman has a strong claim to the developmental category where downstream decisions depend on it. They do not show that a community choosing otherwise for other contexts makes an error about the laws of probability, or that its members must be lying. Wei Dai's unanswered rejoinder belongs here: if part of what people want from the word is to be treated as members of a group, the word achieves some of that in the territory. Davis's cost, borne by everyone who needs the narrower sense, is also real. Weighing the two is a decision about consequences, and on Davis's own account it must be made openly.

That is where Davis is strongest, and his critics tended to miss it. His most constant demand was that the trade be stated. Of Yudkowsky's pronoun policy, which Davis follows himself (And indeed, I don't misgender people! (In public. Only rarely in private, when someone's transition doesn't seem legitimate or serious to me, and the person I'm talking to doesn't seem liable to object.)), he wrote: I'm not claiming that Yudkowsky should have a different pronoun usage policy. His objection is to presenting a choice with costs on both sides as a neutral fact of language design. An advocate of self-identified pronouns, he says, should be able to tell the speaker who bears the cost, Sorry, I'm participating in a political coalition that believes that trans people's feelings are more important than yours with respect to this policy question; sucks to be you. The demand that the trade be stated is sound. Davis's sample statement of it is not fair to the other side, which might give reasons other than feelings: equal standing, predictable interaction, the burden of contesting someone's status in public.

9. The argument as conduct

Davis made the deployment of the argument part of its subject. The history belongs to Chapter 7, but two matters bear on the philosophy.

The stealth reply comes first. In a 2020 "containment thread" Davis disclosed that "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" was intended as a "stealth reply" (quoting/paraphrasing without attribution in order to reply to the philosophical substance while eliding the political context) to Alexander and Yudkowsky (LW, 10 Dec 2020). The aim was to let reputable people endorse the philosophy without taking a side on gender, which is reasonable, since the point is, as the memoir says, a general point about how language itself works that has nothing to do with gender. But the method withheld from readers the context that explained why the questions were being asked. Davis acknowledged as much (I could see a case that it was unfair of me to include political subtext and then only expect people to engage with the politically clean text; "A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning," UUT, 15 Jul 2023) and disclosed the context himself within two years. Readers who did not know the context could not see why the questions mattered (Arnold called the closing paragraphs a meandering vagueblog; 20 Jan 2021), while David Hornbein called the post a crushingly well-argued piece of philosophy of language and its relation to reasoning (17 Dec 2020).

The second is motive, and the dolphin episode supports more than one reading. The published reply insinuated, through irony, that Soares had a reason he was not stating. A draft written a week later, preserved as a hidden page, accepts Soares's account of his own motives (I believe that you weren't trying to play political games with me!), yet treats the key sentences of Alexander's essay as an "algorithmic" lie and demands that Soares condemn them publicly: And if you can't do it, then you're a fraud. Davis did not publish it. He published instead a protest at Soares's tone (18 Jun 2021), to which Soares replied, My take on this conversation is that you're dramatically mismodelling me, and then running really far -- and getting quite distressed -- based on bad models. (Nate Soares, 18 Jun 2021), and Vaniver, as moderator, warned against imputing positions stronger than people are holding (18 Jun 2021). Three days later Davis wrote, Thanks. I regret letting my emotions get the better of me. I apologize. (21 Jun 2021). Withholding the draft was restraint, and the apology was prompt, but the suspicion reached print, and Soares's account of his reasons, which no one has shown to be false, weighs against it. Whether such diagnoses are held with the uncertainty Davis's own theory of evidence would assign them is the attribution problem, which Chapter 5 states in general and Chapter 7 applies to the case against Yudkowsky.

10. Conclusion

Davis began by holding that arguments about definitions were pointless, because the facts are what matter, and ended by holding that they are often arguments about facts in disguise: which features cluster, which causes generate which traits, which inferences a word licenses. The two positions are one, since both refuse to let a verbal choice stand in for an empirical claim. The work, from the subspace move through the squared-error calculation to the causal graph, is an unusually sustained piece of independent philosophy, and its central distinction deserves a place in any serious discussion of how to argue about words.

The stronger claims attached to it do not hold as stated. The mathematics measures the price of a category, not whether it is worth paying, as the essay that completes the mathematics says. A departure from the predictive optimum misleads where it is concealed and listeners still decode the word the old way; disclosed departures and non-propositional signals fall outside the charge. The rule against appeals to consequences is only as sharp as a distinction Davis could not make rigorous. A norm survives. If you want a word to pick out something other than the cluster it now names, say so. Give the reason, and say whether it is a claim about the territory or a choice about what to do. Acknowledge what is lost, and by whom. Do not present a choice as a discovery or a discovery as a choice. Davis's coordination argument, offered for holding accuracy without exception, is a good argument for holding this norm strictly, since concealment is what moves a community down the slope he described, provided that disclosure is judged by whether it reaches its listeners. That is what the Category War established, and Davis's two principal opponents conceded its narrow point.

Whether the norm favors one side of the gender dispute is a separate question, and Davis sometimes answered it in the negative: the norm is totally compatible with trans-inclusive usage, provided the usage is argued for. By his framework's own classification, the dispute over woman is a disagreement about which inferences the word should serve. Settling it means weighing the costs and benefits of at least two real categories for a speech community's purposes, a question for social science and ethics, and at one point for the sexology of Chapter 3. Davis's achievement was to show, more precisely than anyone in his community had, why the weighing cannot be avoided and why it has to be done in the open.
