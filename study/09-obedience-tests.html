Chapter 9Obedience Tests: School, Psychiatry, and the Honesty of Coercion

Davis writes about schools and psychiatric wards as institutions of one kind, and his complaint against both is less that they compel than that they describe their compulsion as something else, education in one case and care in the other. The demand that coercion be named as coercion is the norm of accounting applied to institutions, and the empirical literature on coercion in psychiatric admission supports a good part of it: what patients experience as coercive depends heavily on whether they are informed, heard and dealt with fairly, and voluntary status does not prevent it. The literature does not support the further claims Davis attaches to the demand, that the ward exists mainly for the convenience of families and that its staff lie. On the record Davis himself supplies, the institutions in his own case were answering a real danger. The corpus records three successive responses to coercive institutions: a scrupulous obedience that ended in collapse, a general cynical theory of social control, and, in 2026, a practice of telling the instructions that bind from the instructions that do not. The last is the norm of accounting doing the work that the exceptionless reading of rules could not do, and Davis presents it as a recovery.

1. School and the ward

The pairing is Davis's own and comes early. A one-sentence post of 2013, written weeks after his first psychiatric hospitalization, reads in full: I feel like schools, prisons, and mental hospitals are all making the same mistake: locking children, criminals, and crazy people up together just creates more childishness, criminality, and madness. ("Diversity Is Strength," AAL, 12 Mar 2013). Four years later he called the ward where he had been held a prison/kindergarten that everyone bizarrely insists on calling a "psychiatric hospital" ("Fresh Princess," UUT, 26 Mar 2017), and an unpublished memoir draft describes the ward's routine as kindergarten-like activities and the outpatient program that followed as more school-like group sessions where they talk about feelings and coping strategies (unpublished draft, "People, Evolved Social-Control Mechanisms, and Rocks", dated 2023). The phrase that names the chapter is applied to school. In 2024 Davis described the general-education courses he still needed for his degree as insulting obedience tests that are wholly inferior to my free reading and blogging ("Should I Finish My Bachelor's Degree?," AAL, 10 May 2024), and in 2026 he regretted having wasted too much of my life in classes that were just obedience tests ("College Was Not That Terrible Now That I'm Not That Crazy," AAL, 1 Jan 2026).

The biographical facts are few. Davis left the University of California at Santa Cruz after a breakdown at the end of 2007, studied mathematics alone, returned through community college to San Francisco State University in 2012, and left again in 2013, when a psychotic episode brought on by lost sleep led to his first hospitalization (Chapter 1 follows the 2013 posts). After a coding bootcamp he worked as a software engineer. In February 2017, during the dispute about transgender identity described in Chapter 2 and Chapter 7, a second sleep-deprived psychosis ended in a three-day involuntary detention, and a milder relapse followed in April. In 2024 and 2025 he went back to San Francisco State and finished a mathematics degree. The writing about these events is spread across published posts, a hidden diary, and three unfinished memoir drafts, the longest of them some 14,000 words. Its subject, in every period, is what an institution says it is doing to a person and what it is doing.

Two limits govern what follows. The breakdowns are not used to discount any argument in this book, here or elsewhere; Davis's arguments about psychiatry are assessed as arguments. Nor does the chapter offer a diagnosis. Davis reports one himself as a possibility (the reference class that could receive a bipolar I or paranoid schizophrenia diagnosis) and his reasons for declining to use it are among the things examined.

2. Naming the force

The demand appears first in a joke about Star Trek. In a 2014 dialogue, quoted more fully in Chapter 1, the speaker wishes that Captain Janeway, having separated Seven of Nine from the Borg against her will, would say plainly that she was depriving her of autonomy and that after a few more months of captivity, Stockholm syndrome will set in and future-corrupted-you will grow to like it ("Yet Another Idle Wish for a Future Star Trek Series," AAL, 17 Nov 2014). The speaker does not ask Janeway to release Seven. He asks her to give an account of the act that does not borrow the authority of the captive's own good.

After 2017 the same demand is made about the ward in the first person. Davis refused the institution's vocabulary (I continue to refuse to use the word hospitalization) and, in notes toward an unfinished second memoir, stated the account he would have accepted: I would be much less angry about February if they had just said, "We're physically detaining you because we don't trust you not to do anything crazy; we don't expect you to regard this as 'care'" (unpublished draft, "Memoirs of My Recent Madness, Part II: Friendship Survived", dated 2017). The note occurs, characteristically, in a digression about a toddler kept behind a bagel-shop counter in plain breach of the health code, which Davis offers as an emblem of the tragedy where the rules that are written down are different from the rules that are actually enforced or sane or enforceable, which has disastrous effects on meaning in language. The objection is to a gap between description and practice, and its cost is located in language.

The memoir draft of 2023 gives the strongest version:

I unironically suspect it would be kinder and less confusing if the authorities would just explicitly label it a jail: to just say, Society or your loved ones are sick of your antics, so we're going to lock you up in this place and drug you until you obey us and act "normal". An honest punishment of force applied to change behavior is so much less cruel than the psychological abuse of a punishment that coerces its recipients into affirming it as "care".

"People, Evolved Social-Control Mechanisms, and Rocks" (unpublished draft)

Two claims are folded together in this passage. One concerns honesty: a coerced person suffers an additional injury when required to affirm the coercion as benefit, and an institution that names its force spares him that. The other concerns motive: the honest name for what the ward does is punishment for being a nuisance. The first is a version of the norm of accounting, and section 5 finds it partly supported. The second is an empirical claim about why wards exist, and the draft argues for it directly. Psychiatric wards, it says, make sense as being for the welfare of friends or family who are sick of putting up with living with a crazy person. It's a form of responsibility laundering, by which relatives can hand a loved one to an institution with a clean conscience. And the word hospital misleads, because We don't really have procedures to treat mental illness.

The analogy Davis drew in 2022 between school and gender identity, examined for its bearing on gender in Chapter 2, has the same structure. Imagining a movement that would relieve "student dysphoria" by renaming students "research associates" while leaving their conditions unchanged, he wrote that When I was angry about being in school, it wasn't because of the word "student"—it was because I wanted more autonomy and I wanted more respect for my intellectual initiative. A change of name that leaves the substance in place does worse than nothing, on this view, because it would probably make things worse by sabotaging the concepts and language I needed to articulate what my problem was ("Student Dysphoria, and a Previous Life's War," UUT, 16 Apr 2022). The ward that calls detention care and the movement that calls a student a researcher make the same error in opposite directions: one uses a kind word to make compulsion bearable, the other to make a grievance disappear.

3. The incomplete contract

The school writing complicates the picture, because the coercion Davis describes there was in large part his own. An unfinished account of his education, kept among the drafts of the mathematics blog, describes the anxieties of his first year at Santa Cruz: fear of unwitting plagiarism, uncertainty whether skimming a paragraph counted as having done a reading, a homework sheet annotated in red to show which steps a teaching assistant had supplied. The draft's diagnosis is that I implicitly felt as if in school, my job was not to learn, but to comply with the teacher's commands, and I couldn't bear it not being absolutely clear what constituted correct compliance. When he enrolled at a career college in 2008, he hoped to atone for his failures by excelling in a required algebra course, and the college waived the requirement on the strength of his calculus credits: Evidently, the career college staff had a more pragmatic understanding of the purpose of school than I did. The draft's next sentence is The obedience test was all in my head. (unpublished draft, "School Prologue", undated).

The breakdown of 2007, retold in the 2022 post, followed an inability to reach the word count for the final paper in a course on feminist theory that seemed to be taught in a dialect of English that I could not speak. Davis describes himself crying and screaming for hours, "I betrayed them; I betrayed them." The draft adds that he received a B+ for the course and wrote to the teaching assistant that the grade puzzled me, because I thought it had been manifest that my performance merited an F or D grade. The institution, in this episode, demanded less than Davis believed it did and judged him more generously than he judged himself. What made school unbearable was the combination of a vague instruction with a conscience that treated every instruction as binding without remainder.

The reaction was total. Ironically, in the inferno of shame over having betrayed my mandate to the University, my attitude towards school flipped practically overnight. The two-line post of 2012, Shut up! Shut up and leave me alone with my books! ("A Philosophy of Education," AAL, 3 Dec 2012), belongs to this period, and so does the hatred of the whole diseased culture of institutional servitude that the 2022 post recalls from community college. The structure matters for the rest of the chapter. Davis's first response to a coercive institution was to obey it more completely than it asked, and when obedience failed, to reject its authority wholesale. What he did not yet have was a way to comply with some instructions and not others without feeling that the distinction was itself a betrayal.

4. February 2017 on the record

The plan for this study asked whether, in the episodes Davis describes, the institutions were responding to real danger. The question can be answered only from Davis's own record, but that record is unusually full. The 2023 draft reconstructs the days from emails, chat logs and the clinical notes he later obtained, and it does not spare him.

On the night of 15 February 2017 Davis did not sleep. The next day he sent emails he later called delusional, went to his office in San Francisco, and alarmed his coworkers with a sentence he had chosen, he says, so as not to give anyone grounds to detain him: I think it was something like, "I think I'm in the mental state that causes people to perform the verbal behavior of saying they want to commit suicide." His comment on the strategy is dry: In retrospect, I don't think people pay attention to such distinctions. A coworker took him to his mother's house. That night, afraid of being kidnapped and tortured, he entertained the thought that safety did not rule out the possibility of me needing to commit suicide before going to sleep in order to avoid having to experience "my share" of the kidnap-and-torturing. On the morning of the 17th his mother took him to be evaluated. The notes record that she made treatment a condition of his staying with her, that he agreed to medication and an outpatient program, and that he does not meet criteria for California Welfare & Institutions Code 5150 (LPS) for involuntary detainment for inpatient psychiatric monitoring. He was released. That afternoon he left the house without telling her, she called the police, and they found him at the train station. This time he was detained, held for three days, and released to his father. (Section 5150 provides, from memory, for an emergency hold of up to seventy-two hours.)

On this account the first institution to see Davis declined to detain him, and the second detained him after a report from a frightened parent that her son, who had spoken of suicide the day before and had not slept in two days, had disappeared. Davis maintains that the detention was wrong, and his argument is not that he was sane: I still think I was correct to want to avoid the psych hospital, but that's not at all the same thing as thinking that I was sane. His argument is that confinement among strangers was the worst available treatment for a man who needed sleep, and that The problem is that while having a psychotic break is the worst possible time to be kidnapped by strangers. That argument may be right about what would have served him best. It does not show that the danger the institutions acted on was imaginary, and on his own account it was not.

Davis's record of his earlier hospitalization points the same way. A 2021 draft notes that in 2013, when I started feeling crazy after missing that night of sleep in 2013, I asked to go to the hospital! Imagine being that misinformed! (unpublished draft, "Wellness Strategy Notes, 15 January 2021"). A post written two months after that stay already measured the experience against its physical facts: After my recent ordeal in the psych ward, the part of me that talks described it as 'hellish.' But I was physically safe the entire time. ("Relativity," AAL, 11 May 2013).

Two of Davis's revisions within the record deserve notice, because they show his method at work on his own grievances. The first concerns medication. In the ward he took pills under pressure that he describes as a threat, and he points to the rights handbook he was given, which promised a right to refuse medication: A "right" that you get punished for exercising is ... not much of a right? Four years later, seeking help for insomnia, he learned that he had been given an antipsychotic and the sedating antidepressant trazodone, and concluded that I now think that taking the medication was a good idea. He anticipates the inference a reader would draw, that the authorities were right to force him, and answers: Because submission to authority isn't the same thing as sanity. Had someone he trusted handed him the same pill with an explanation, I would have taken it. The concession is real, and so is the distinction. The same draft's claim that psychiatry has no procedures to treat mental illness does not survive it, since the drug he now endorses was the treatment.

The second concerns the paperwork. A form stated that Davis had self presented due to [my] suicidal thoughts, which was false: the police had brought him. In June 2017 he cited the form as proof that ward staff blatantly make shit up ("Memoirs of My Recent Madness, Part I: The Unanswerable Words," UUT, 19 Jun 2017). Rereading the notes in 2023, he found a duller explanation: Reading the doctor's notes six years later, it seems likely that this false description was copied from the beginning of a note written at the psychiatric emergency service, a note that later in the same text gives the correct sequence of events. The falsehood is the same; what changed is the account of how it arose. The later account requires no liar, only a form filled in from an earlier note, and it is the kind of explanation that Davis's own theory of deception (Chapter 5) is built to accommodate: a false statement produced by a process, with no one deceiving.

5. What the literature on coercion shows

Davis's demand can be set against a substantial research literature on how psychiatric patients experience coercion. Much of it descends from the MacArthur research on coercion (of the 1990s, from memory), whose Admission Experience Survey and Perceived Coercion Scale were used in 74 of the 143 sources in the review discussed next. A 2025 scoping review of 143 sources found that Relational and legal factors were most consistently associated with perceived coercion, while individual and clinical factors showed inconsistent findings. Two findings bear directly on Davis. The first is that coercion is felt well beyond the boundary of legal compulsion: Many voluntarily admitted individuals reported high levels of coercion, often linked to subtle forms of pressure, lack of adequate information, or exclusion from meaningful decision-making processes. The second is that fair process reduces it: Numerous quantitative studies have found an inverse relationship between levels of procedural justice and levels of perceived coercion, and distress over formal coercive measures was particularly intensified when patients believed decisions were made arbitrarily, punitively, or due to perceived professional incompetence (Lessard-Deschênes et al. 2025). An earlier review of outpatient commitment reached a similar conclusion: there is evidence that the perception of coercion can be mitigated by procedural justice that is demonstrated by fairness, patient inclusion in the process, and benevolence on the part of authority figures. (Galon and Wineman 2010).

This is support for the first of Davis's two claims. The literature treats information, voice and candor as part of what determines whether coercion harms, and it finds that pressure exercised informally, through threats of longer stays or loss of privileges, is experienced as coercive whatever the patient's legal status. Davis's complaint that a right to refuse medication was a right he would have been punished for exercising describes the informal coercion the review catalogues. His wish to be told we are detaining you because we do not trust you is close to what the procedural-justice literature recommends, which is that the reasons for compulsion be stated and the patient's account heard. The same review notes that Police transportation was also perceived as highly coercive and traumatizing, which is how Davis's detention began.

The literature tells against the second claim. Davis's honest name for the ward is punishment, and the literature identifies the perception of punitiveness as one of the things that makes coercion worse. One element of procedural justice, in Galon and Wineman's summary, is benevolence on the part of authority. An institution that described its holds as punishment for antics would be honest only if that were its purpose, and the evidence on purpose does not favor Davis. The largest prospective study of patients' own judgments, covering 2,326 involuntary admissions in eleven European countries, found that between 39 and 71% felt the admission was right after 1 month, and between 46 and 86% after 3 months. (Priebe et al. 2010). A qualitative study in England found three groups. Those who thought their detention wrong thought that their problems could have been managed through less coercive interventions, and experienced hospitalisation as an unjust infringement of their autonomy, and an ambivalent group believed that they needed acute treatment and that hospitalisation averted further harm but thought a less coercive course might have served (Katsakou et al. 2012). Davis, who now endorses the medication and rejects the confinement, belongs to the ambivalent group, and his argument is that group's argument.

Davis's 2014 dialogue anticipates one reply to these figures. If captivity produces Stockholm syndrome, a patient's later approval of his captivity is weak evidence that it was right. The objection has force against the bare percentages, and the studies cited here do not answer it. It has less force against Davis's own case, because his later approval of the medication rests on independent information, a clinician's record and a published guide to insomnia, rather than on any change of heart toward his captors. The objection also cuts in both directions: a patient's lasting resentment of a detention, formed while he was afraid of being killed, is not privileged evidence either.

The claim of responsibility laundering can be tested against what families report. A 2020 systematic review of twenty-three studies of carers' experiences of involuntary admission found that Carers often described conflicting feelings of relief coupled with distress and anxiety about how the patient might cope and respond. Among the reasons for relief were that the person was in a safe place; that there was an opportunity for respite and to share responsibility for care with health professionals. The same review found that Many carers reported that they felt guilt, feeling responsible for the detention of the person they cared for (Stuart et al. 2020). Davis is right that detention relieves families and that some of the relief is a transfer of responsibility to professionals, and that is a fact institutions rarely state. He is wrong that the transfer comes with a clean conscience; the families studied report guilt and distress alongside relief. His own mother, in the record he gives, was frightened and cranky, and called the police after her son vanished from a house where he had spoken the night before of suicide. That is the situation the review describes.

Davis made a version of this objection himself, in a post of 2018 comparing the justification "We need to institutionalize people in order to prevent them from hurting themselves" to the ticking-bomb argument for torture: a scenario with obvious consequentialist merit that makes it rude to say I claim that this hypothetical scenario is not realized nearly as often as you seem to be implying and therefore falsifiably predict that many of your alleged real-world examples will fall apart on further examination. ("Concerning Frame Control Via Salient Scenarios," AAL, 1 Oct 2018). The prediction is the right kind of claim to make, and the post deserves credit for making it falsifiable. Davis's own case, on the evidence he published, is one of the examples that does not fall apart.

6. People, mechanisms, and rocks

The second response to coercive institutions was a general theory, formed in the weeks after the detention. On 12 March 2017 Davis posted on Facebook, as the 2023 draft quotes him, that it is surprisingly useful to model the world as being made out of three things: people (who can be friends, enemies, or strangers), evolved social-control mechanisms [...] and rocks. One should not take what people say about the mechanisms at face value but predict how they will behave. The published memoir of June 2017 applies the theory to psychiatry: Davis concedes the possible diagnosis, but right now, I'm modeling the field of psychiatry as an evolved social-control mechanism rather than a genuine attempt to help people, and I correspondingly decline to use its language and categories. A 2017 post from the same weeks, written on his twenty-second day out of prison, generalizes further: There are no principles. There have never been any principles. The horror is not, "This is a cult." The horror is that everything is a cult. ("Wicked Transcendence III," AAL, 15 Mar 2017).

The theory resembles, from memory, Thomas Szasz's account of psychiatry as social control, with a difference that matters. Szasz denied that mental illness was illness; Davis grants that something in him could be diagnosed and objects to the institution's right to describe it. His dispute is over the description, as it was in 2014.

The theory's best test came in April 2017, when a friend Davis calls "Roberta" was detained in another state and the ward refused to confirm or deny that she was there. Davis spent two days calling, and his account in the June memoir is the center of that post. He bet Scott Alexander, who is a psychiatrist, $500 against $25 that if Roberta was there she had not received her friends' messages. A visitor reported that she had, and Davis paid; later Roberta said she had no memory of any message, and the two men cancelled the bet. Davis concluded that my theory that psych ward employees (besides Scott) are lying kidnappers made a correct prediction at 20:1 odds. His own account gives reason to doubt both pieces of evidence. The visitor's report rested on Roberta's response, and Roberta's later report rested on her memory of a period when, Davis writes, she was actually thinking in terms of interpreting lots of observations as messages from various sources. Even if no message was delivered, an undelivered message is evidence of negligence. The step from it to lying is the step from a false output to a deceiving intent, which Chapter 5 shows Davis taking too readily elsewhere.

The 2023 draft preserves what the theory licensed in the moment. Writing to the friends coordinating the search, Davis said: We are extracting our friend from the bowels of an unaligned AI. The reason we're using words rather than force isn't because force would be wrong; it's because we'd lose. When a friend he calls "Kyle" objected that hostile tactics would make life miserable for staff doing a difficult job and help no one, Davis replied that I didn't care about not making life unpleasant for people trying to do their absolute best in a difficult job. The draft does not endorse this. It records Kyle's report that the staff had been polite and helpful and adds a three-word parenthesis, (Nice people doing their jobs!), that turns the theory's own phrase against it. The cynical theory had removed the individuals from view, and the memoirist puts them back.

There is a disciplined version of the theory in the corpus as well, and it is the one Davis kept. A post of 2018 reads in full:

Always be peaceful and tell the truth to your friends because you love and trust them. Always be peaceful and tell the truth to cops, schoolteachers, psychiatrists, CPS agents, &c. because you're outgunned and bad at lying. Don't be confused about your reasons for doing things, even if you always end up doing the same thing.

"Concerning Motives for Cooperation," AAL, 2 Oct 2018

The behavior prescribed is the same in both cases, and the post adds only a requirement that one's account of one's own reasons be true. It is the norm of accounting turned inward, and a long way from the April emails.

7. Michael Vassar and the psychoses

The theory that institutions are social-control machines was shared, and in Davis's circle it was associated with Michael Vassar, a former president of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (from memory) who figures throughout the memoirs (Chapter 7). In October 2021 Jessica Taylor published an account of her time at and around MIRI and the Center for Applied Rationality that included a cluster of psychotic breaks in the community. Scott Alexander replied that most of those involved had been close to Vassar and his friends, whom he called "the Vassarites," and that their influence caused borderline psychosis and occasionally full-blown psychosis, which they would discourage people from seeking treatment for, because they thought psychiatrists were especially evil and corrupt and traumatizing and unable to understand that psychosis is just breaking mental shackles. He added, (I am a psychiatrist and obviously biased here) (Scott Alexander, comment on Taylor, "My experience at and around MIRI and CFAR," LW, 17 Oct 2021).

Davis answered the next day that one of the breaks in question was presumably his own, (Which Michael had nothing to do with causing, by the way.), and proposed a different explanation of the correlation: To the extent that there is a correlation between talking to Michael and having psych issues, I suspect a lot of it is a selection effect rather than causal, since Vassar had told him that he sought out people high in Eysenckian psychoticism. He corrected an earlier report, which had circulated through another blogger, that Vassar had broken him out of the ward: Michael did visit me in the psych ward, which was incredibly helpful, but the discharge was routine (Davis, comment, 18 Oct 2021). Alexander replied that I disagree with your assessment that your joining the Vassarites wasn't harmful to you, citing a later episode in 2019 (comment, 18 Oct 2021). In July 2022, after interviewing those involved, he retracted the central claim: My main conclusion is that I was wrong about Michael making people psychotic. Everyone I talked to had some other risk factor, like a preexisting family or personal history, or took recreational drugs at doses that would explain their psychotic episodes. His revised explanation is nearly Davis's: Michael has a tendency to befriend people with high trait psychoticism and heavy drug use, and often has strong opinions on their treatment, which explains why he is often very close to people and very noticeable at the moment they become psychotic. He kept a separate worry about cult-y high-pressure recruitment tactics and apologized to Vassar and Taylor (comment, 3 Jul 2022).

On the specific claim about February 2017, the record supports Davis. His first psychosis, in 2013, came when he had not corresponded with Vassar for three years, and the 2017 episode followed a public dispute that had nothing to do with him. The causal graph Davis drew in a later draft, prone-to-psychosis ← underlying-bipolar-ish-condition → gets-along-with-Michael (unpublished draft, "Guess I'll Die", dated 2023), is the one Alexander came to accept.

The question of influence after the fact is different, and here Davis's own drafts supply the evidence for Alexander's side. In the weeks after his release Davis described to Vassar the delusional frame of the episode, in which Vassar and others were scientists guarding humanity from inside a simulation, and Vassar replied, as the 2023 draft quotes him, What you were thinking is about right I think. But we still know that animals sleep. When Davis wrote that he should give more weight to the view that nice people were doing their jobs than to his sense that the machines were trying to kill him, Vassar answered that 'Trying to kill' is what an anthropomorphized version of the process looks like from the inside. Davis copied two more grounded friends into the thread, writing that he was terrified of what the universe is going to do to me if I become too much like him. The next day he sent the message headed "slowly coming around to you": Okay, maybe confusing and hurting the feelings of the nice people doing their jobs is actually acceptable collateral damage in the war against the control system. A month later came the Roberta emails and the April relapse. None of this shows that Vassar caused a psychosis. It shows a man recovering from one being encouraged, by the friend he trusted most, toward the reading of institutions that he himself had just judged frightening, and the draft that records it is Davis's own.

Davis's public reply of 2021 did not include this. In the draft he explains why: he was rebutting a charge he thought absurd, and A defense lawyer has an easier job than a rationalist—if the prosecution makes a terrible case, you can just destroy it, without it being your job to worry about whether your client is separately guilty of vaguely similar crimes that the incompetent prosecution can't prove. The draft goes on to concede that a later episode, in early 2021, actually were the result of being on the receiving end of (what I perceived as) "the everyone-yelling thing" from Vassar's circle. The defense-lawyer stance is the selective advocacy that Davis's own theory of deception classifies as misleading when its filter is concealed. His public comment did gesture at the filter (I don't want to derail the thread by going into the details of my own situation), and the full account, unfavorable parts included, is in the draft. By the standard of Chapter 5 the comment was a disclosed partial account, and the disclosure was thin. The draft is what supplies the rest.

8. Real and fake instructions

The third response belongs to 2024–2026, when Davis returned to San Francisco State to finish his degree. The 2024 post weighing the decision makes a concession the earlier writing does not. School, it says, functions as a signal, and once the signaling regime has been established, it's mostly true that people who don't finish the standard course probably have something wrong with them. Davis applies this to himself: Separately from the fact that I'm obviously right that my personal passion projects are more intellectually meritorious than the busywork school demanded of me, there's also something wrong with me. The argument resembles, from memory, Bryan Caplan's signaling account of education, and Davis draws from it the conclusion that the institution's persistence is less mysterious than his younger self supposed.

The 2026 retrospective records the result. The university is still, to Davis, the same communist dystopia I remember from a dozen years ago, and the post is full of the old complaints: course numbers in place of titles, a textbook in poor taste, a professor who would not credit an answer that drew on material not yet covered. The difference is that the old complaints no longer paralyze him. The incomplete contract was an annoyance, not a torturous contradiction in the fabric of reality. The section titled "Cynicism and Sanity" explains the change:

In a word, what saved me was cynicism, except that cynicism is just naturalism about the properties of institutions made out of humans. The behavior of the humans is in part influenced by various streams of written and oral natural language instructions from various sources. It's not surprising that there would sometimes be ambiguity in some of the instructions, or even contradictions between different sources of instructions. As an agent interacting with the system, it was necessarily up to me to decide how to respond to ambiguities or contradictions in accordance with my perception of the moral law.

"College Was Not That Terrible Now That I'm Not That Crazy," AAL, 1 Jan 2026

The distinction he draws next is the one the 2007 student could not make: Cheating on a math test would be contrary to the moral law: it feels unclean to even speak of it as a hypothetical possibility. Clicking through a harassment-training video without watching it is not, though the instructions said to watch it. I'm allowed to notice which instructions are morally "real" and which ones are "fake", without such guidance being provided by the instructions themselves. The scrupulosity of 2007 now looks to him like a misplaced virtue: I don't want to condemn my younger self entirely, because the trait that made me so dysfunctional was a form of integrity. And the cost of it was paid by others as well: The total mental breakdown didn't help anyone! It was bad for me, and it imposed costs on everyone around me.

What replaced the exceptionless reading of instructions was disclosure. Davis used language models for hints on homework and footnoted each use; he skipped readings in a general-education course and said so in class; he wrote the post itself so that If anyone has any doubts about the meaning of my Bachelor of Arts in Mathematics for Liberal Arts from San Francisco State University, they can read this post and get a pretty good idea of what that entailed. He concludes: I've put in more than enough effort into being transparent that it doesn't make sense for me to be neurotically afraid of accidentally being a fraud.

This is the study's thesis at small scale. Part (c), the coordination argument, holds that a norm must be kept without exception because any exception slides toward abandonment. The student of 2007 lived that argument with respect to instructions, treating every ambiguity in an assignment as a possible betrayal, and it broke him. The graduate of 2025 keeps the norm of accounting (b) and drops the demand of (c): he departs from instructions he judges fake, states the departure, and bears its cost, which in one course was a C−. The disclosed departure is not a defection, and the account is what keeps the exception from becoming a slide. Davis does not frame the change this way, and the post's conclusion is about sanity rather than epistemology, but the structure is the one the Introduction attributes to his best work.

What the post shows is limited. Its subject is instructions addressed to Davis by an institution he can leave. It does not extend the same latitude to others, and nothing in it softens his position on the speech norms of Chapter 6 or the categories of Chapter 4. Davis is still the judge of which instructions are fake, and the post's own episode of an extra-credit point offered for attending a campus protest shows the old escalation intact: he threatened to take the matter to the department chair within two days, and describes the outrage as performative in some sense, but it was also deeply felt—as if putting on a performance was the most sincere thing I could do under the circumstances. And the post's concession on credentialism goes further than the earlier writing allowed: being miscalibrated about his own mathematical preparation, Davis writes, helps explain why the coercion of credentialism is functional, why the power structure survives instead of immediately getting competed out of existence. The 2023 draft made no comparable concession about the ward.

A parable published in August 2026 can be read against this history. A boy at a celebrated school copies the first sentence of each chapter as his required "reflection sentence" and is checked off every time; The teacher always gave him a checkmark, therefore his way of doing reflection sentences was correct. When his stepmother shows him that an arithmetic problem marked correct is wrong, using three calculators, he collapses, as if at some cosmic horror only he can see. Davis declines to state the moral (But they're not paying me to tell you what it is exactly.) ("Charles Goodhart Elementary School," AAL, 5 Aug 2026). The title names Goodhart's law, and the obvious reading is a satire of measurement. A second reading is available from the rest of the corpus. The boy has taken the institution's verdict as constitutive of correctness, as the Santa Cruz student took its instructions as constitutive of duty, and his horror when the verdict fails resembles what Davis describes in 2017, when the belief that the world was "made out of words" gave way. That reading is this study's, not the parable's.

9. Conclusion

Davis's writing on school and psychiatry makes one demand consistently from 2014 to 2026: an institution that compels should say that it compels, and should not require the compelled to call it a gift. The demand is the norm of accounting applied where the costs of a description fall on the person described, and the research on coercion in psychiatric care supports more of it than a reader might expect. Being told the truth about one's status, and being heard, changes how coercion is experienced. The polemical theory that grew up around the demand in 2017, in which wards are jails for the convenience of families and their staff are lying kidnappers, does not survive the same research or Davis's own later accounts, which concede the danger, credit the medication, and explain the false paperwork without a liar. Davis's record of his own case is careful enough to supply the evidence against his theory of it.

The 2023 draft ends with a question it does not answer: Sometimes, I worry that human psychology is built such that things couldn't have been too much otherwise—that the realistic alternative to taking things too seriously (to the detriment of one's health, and severely inconveniencing others) is taking them lying down. The 2026 retrospective is evidence that the alternatives were never only those two. Its practice of naming departures and bearing their cost is a third course, and Davis reached it in the setting where he had first suffered from the lack of it. Whether the same practice can govern his dealings with institutions he cannot leave, or with the community whose norms he spent a decade contesting, the corpus leaves open.
