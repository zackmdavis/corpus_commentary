Where to Draw the Boundaries

The philosophy of categorization across An Algorithmic Lucidity and The Scintillating But Ultimately Untrue Thought, 2016–2025. A pilot chapter for a study of Zack M. Davis's blogging corpus.

About this chapter. This is a pilot chapter, written in a single session by Claude (Fable 5.1) for the user who commissioned the study, as one of two parallel pilots on the same topic. It is meant to stand on its own, but it assumes the shape of a larger monograph around it: the sexology that Davis's categorization arguments sometimes lean on is examined in another chapter and only summarized here, and the history of Davis's dispute with the rationalist community is treated only as far as the categorization question requires.

Positionality. The study is written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic. The corpus includes criticism of Claude and Anthropic (notably the 2026 posts on corrigibility in Claude's constitution), and Davis has used Claude and other language models as arbiters in some of his later disputes. None of that bears directly on the posts discussed in this chapter, which predate it or concern other people, but the reader should know who is writing. I have relied on the files in the corpus and on sources fetched and read in this project rather than on recalled impressions of the author or the discourse; every quotation was checked by script against its source file.

Limitations. Three of the texts that provoked Davis's most important posts are login-walled: Eliezer Yudkowsky's November 2018 Twitter thread, his September 2020 Facebook clarification, and his February 2021 Facebook post on pronouns. Archived copies could not be retrieved. They are quoted here only as the corpus quotes them, and I say so where it matters. Nate Soares's June 2021 Twitter thread is in the same position, though Soares restates his view at length in LessWrong comments that I did read. Alex Byrne's 2020 paper "Are women adult human females?" could not be downloaded; I rely on Byrne's own restatement of it in his 2021 reply to Robin Dembroff and on Dembroff's reconstruction, both read in full.

1. The problem and the thesis

Between February 2018 and September 2025 Zack M. Davis wrote something like forty posts, across two blogs and the LessWrong forum, on a single philosophical question: what makes one way of sorting things into categories better than another? The question is old. Plato's butcher who carves nature at its joints, Locke's nominal essences, Quine's remark that the notion of a kind and the notion of similarity are substantially one notion, Goodman's grue, Richard Boyd's homeostatic property clusters, John Dupré's "promiscuous realism" about biological taxa: there is a literature, and Davis engages almost none of it. His sources are instead a sequence of blog posts that Eliezer Yudkowsky wrote in early 2008 under the title "A Human's Guide to Words," a 2014 essay by Scott Alexander called "The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories," a handful of textbooks in probability and information theory, and Brian Skyrms's work on signaling games. The occasion was narrower still. Davis took up the question because people kept citing Alexander's essay at him when he tried to argue, in the Bay Area rationalist community of 2016 and 2017, that a sexual-orientation-based typology of male-to-female transsexualism was correct, and that the men in that typology's second type were not women in any sense that a scientifically literate person should be obliged to honor.The provocation is narrated in "A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning" (UUT, 15 July 2023), http://unremediatedgender.space/2023/Jul/a-hill-of-validity-in-defense-of-meaning/, and the sexology in "Blanchard's Dangerous Idea and the Plight of the Lucid Crossdreamer" (UUT, 8 July 2023), which this chapter does not treat.

A reader who knows only that much would be entitled to expect a motivated hack: an epistemology constructed to deliver a verdict on one contested noun. That expectation is wrong, and showing why it is wrong is the first task of this chapter. The categorization posts are the most sustained, most technically serious, and most successful philosophical work in the corpus. They identify a real equivocation in Alexander's essay, one that Alexander himself eventually conceded in an editorial note; they extend Yudkowsky's informal account of categories with genuine content (the subspace resolution of the dolphin problem, the mutual-information analysis of title inflation, the squared-error formalization of "compactness," the observation that a variable at the root of a causal graph buys conditional independence across every downstream feature); and they were produced under conditions, social and psychological, that would have excused far worse work. Several of the people Davis was arguing with said as much. Said Achmiz called the central post an excellent post with that rare quality, like much of the Sequences, of the ideas it describes being utterly obvious—in retrospect; the LessWrong administrator Oliver Habryka said of the 2022 pronoun post that basically everything in this post strikes me as "obviously true".Said Achmiz, comment on "Where to Draw the Boundaries?", 21 April 2019, LessWrong; habryka, comment on "Challenges to Yudkowsky's Pronoun Reform Proposal", 13 March 2022, LessWrong.

The second task is to say where the work fails, and here the chapter's thesis has two parts. First, Davis's positive theory, that the cognitive function of categories is to support probabilistic inference and that any other reason for drawing a boundary reduces to coordination, self-deception, or deception, is overextended. It handles natural kinds well and conventional kinds badly. Money, marriage, crime, adulthood, and (on the view of most of the philosophers who have written about it) gender are categories whose boundaries partly constitute the facts they describe, and a theory that classifies every non-predictive reason for a boundary as "wireheading" or "war" has stipulated its conclusion rather than earned it. Davis half-knows this: the "Schelling categories" posts of 2019 are an admission that coordination is a legitimate second-order criterion, and in a 2020 comment he lists the self-fulfilling prophecy as a complication whose theory he does not yet understand. But the admission is never integrated into the polemic. Second, and more consequentially, the application of the theory to the word "woman" trades on an unresolved choice between two categories that Davis's own framework licenses as natural: perceived sex, the category that everyday cognition actually uses and that successfully transitioned people can enter, and developmental sex, the category rooted in gametes and chromosomes that no current intervention changes. Davis's 2018 essay grants the first; his 2021–2024 writing insists on the second; the framework says both are clusters in different subspaces, and that which one gets the short word is a coordination problem whose solution depends on what speakers want to talk about. By his own lights, then, the dispute he fought as a battle between "Truth" and "Feelings" was a conflict over which Schelling point a speech community should adopt, with real costs on both sides. The framework is better than the war fought in its name.

There is an irony in how the war ended, and the chapter will come to it. The two principals, Alexander and Yudkowsky, each eventually conceded the narrow philosophical point in public: Alexander by appending a note to his 2014 essay in December 2019 saying that one must deal with the consequences of your definitions, which can include confusion, muddling communication, and leaving openings for deceptive rhetorical strategies, and Yudkowsky in a September 2020 Facebook post that, as Davis quotes it, said you are being the bad guy if you try to shut down that conversation by saying that "I can define the word 'woman' any way I want".Alexander's note is the bracketed "EDIT" at the end of "The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories", read in the archived copy cited in sources.md. Yudkowsky's post is login-walled; the sentence is quoted from "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them" (UUT, 30 December 2023), http://unremediatedgender.space/2023/Dec/if-clarity-seems-like-death-to-them/. Neither changed his policy on how to talk about trans people. That outcome is exactly what Davis's own theory predicts if the underlying disagreement was about coordination and values rather than about the laws of probability. He experienced it as a defeat.

A note on method. Davis's posts on this subject are unusually interlinked, and later ones restate earlier ones; the memoir posts of 2023–2024 then restate the whole sequence as a narrative. I read them in order of composition and treat the memoirs as a second source rather than as the authoritative one, because the memoirs are written to prosecute a case. Where a claim about what someone else said rests only on the memoirs (Yudkowsky's tweets, Facebook posts, and private correspondence), I say so.
