3. The first reply (2018)

Davis's prescriptivist instincts predate the controversy. In August 2016, in a short dialogue called "Prescription," one speaker complains that other people are really bizarrely cavalier about using words to mean whatever they feel like at the moment and that I wasn't expecting people to redefine a top-20 noun out from under my feet."Prescription" (AAL, 28 August 2016), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2016/Aug/prescription/. The noun is not named. Four months later a one-paragraph aside regrets having missed the chance to be for civil unions but against redefining existing words that already mean something else."Prescription II" (AAL, 2 December 2016), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2016/Dec/prescription-ii/. And a March 2017 note on the "Bayes-structural justification for free speech norms" already contains the key figure of the later work: a coalition that bans hate speech probably won't notice when members of the coalition begin to slowly gerrymander the hate speech category boundary in the service of their own values."An Intuition on the Bayes-Structural Justification for Free Speech Norms" (AAL, 8 March 2017), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2017/Mar/an-intuition-on-the-bayes-structural-justification-for-free-speech-norms/. "Gerrymandered" would become his standard adjective for a boundary drawn for its effects.

"Blegg Mode"

The first published piece of the program is "Blegg Mode" (1 February 2018), eight hundred words of what Davis's tag calls "deniably allegorical" fan fiction for Yudkowsky's factory. Hard, blue, furry objects start arriving; on inspection they are rubes with sanded corners and glued-on fur. The narrator, invoking Alexander, insists on calling them "adapted bleggs," but notices that they occupy a third category in his mind anyway, and extracts a criterion: a population earns a category when it has cheap-to-detect features that correlate with more-expensive-to-detect features that are decision-relevant with respect to the agent's goals; such a category is a distinction that makes a difference, something your brain wants to have an efficient representation so that you can attend to it."Blegg Mode" (UUT, 1 February 2018), http://unremediatedgender.space/2018/Feb/blegg-mode/. The accompanying hand-drawn two-by-two diagram puts "adapted rube-to-bleggs" in the cell where an easily observed feature and a decision-relevant hidden feature both differ, marked "worth a new category." The criterion is sound as far as it goes, and its weakness was identified on the day the piece reached LessWrong in March 2019: the commenter gjm called it a principle with the look, to me, of a principle that's constructed in order to reach a particular conclusion, and Jessica Taylor pointed out that a Bayesian classifier isn't going to assign special importance to observed features, as it treats the observations as causally downstream of the ontological reality rather than causally upstream of it, to which Davis replied that he might have been on the wrong track there.Comments by gjm and jessica.liu.taylor on the "Blegg Mode" linkpost, March 2019, LessWrong; Davis's reply at commentId rRW59uDjfQYmSJ7bP concedes that the "depoliticized" description was disingenuous. Taylor's point is the better one. Observability is a constraint on which category you can use, not on which categories exist; "Blegg Mode" runs the two together, and the later work separates them under the heading of Schelling categories.

"The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions"

The main reply, "The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions" (23 February 2018, about 6,500 words), is the essay Davis describes as the capstone of a year's sabbatical, and it is the hinge of the whole program."The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions" (UUT, 23 February 2018), http://unremediatedgender.space/2018/Feb/the-categories-were-made-for-man-to-make-predictions/. It opens by granting Alexander everything in Parts I–III and then turning the grant into an accusation: all of it is entirely correct—and thus, an excellent motte for the less honest sort of Slate Star Codex reader to appeal to when they want to obfuscate and disrupt discussions about empirical reality by insisting on gerrymandered redefinitions of everyday concepts. The central paragraph against "not an error" is worth quoting because it states the position Davis would spend six years elaborating:

But this is just giving up way too easily. The map is not the territory, and many very different kinds of maps can correspond to the territory in different ways—we have geographical maps, political maps, road maps, globes, &c.—but that doesn't mean no map is in error. Rationalists can't insist on using the one true categorization system, because it turns out that—in all philosophical strictness—no such thing exists. But that doesn't release us from our sacred duty to describe what's actually true. It just leaves us faced with the slightly more complicated task of describing the costs and benefits of different categorization systems with respect to different criteria.

Whales are mammals and whales are dagim; both categories do cognitive work, because each predicts unobserved features; and in configuration space the whale sits in two clusters at once, in two different subspaces. The essay's most valuable sentence is a methodological one. Where political factions fight over a common word, rationalists may not be able to say that one side is simply right and the other is simply wrong, but we can at least strive for objectivity in describing the conflict. And: That there exists an element of subjectivity in what you choose to pay attention to, doesn't negate the fact that there is a structured empirical reality to be described—and not all descriptions of it are equally compact. On Lincoln's riddle: you can call a tail a leg, but you can't stop people from noticing that out of a dog's five legs, one of them is different from the others.

Then comes the diagnosis of Alexander's Part IV. The claim that one may redraw the boundary to save a life is true in a tautological sense: if you deliberately gerrymander your category boundaries in order to get the answer you want, you can get the answer you want. The hair-dryer analogy fails because carrying a hair dryer affects one person, while collectively agreeing that gender should be defined on the basis of self-identity affects everyone who wants to use language to categorize humans by sex: The words man and woman are top-20 nouns! People need those nouns to describe their experiences! The cost of the redefinition may be small per speaker but needs to be aggregated across everyone subject to it, like so many dust specks in their eyes. Note what this argument concedes: that the question is one of costs and benefits, aggregated across parties. That is a utilitarian frame, not a "words can be wrong" frame. The essay never resolves which it is making, and the ambivalence persists through the whole program.

What the essay does next is more interesting than the diagnosis, and it is the part the later polemic tended to forget. Davis says that the right kind of argument for calling trans women women would be an empirical one: that successfully transitioned people are categorized as their target sex by the pre-verbal perceptual machinery everyone actually uses, so that they fit into existing concepts of gender, and It works because and to the extent that transitioning actually works. A natal female who presents as a man and is modeled as a man might be said to be a man in the sense of social gender (but not in the sense of "biologically male adult human"), because that's the mental category that people are actually using for him. This is a concession of real size. It says that there are at least two legitimate senses of the gender words, perceived and developmental, that the perceived sense is a natural category in Davis's own terms (it is what the classifier in people's heads computes), and that trans people can belong to it. The rest of the essay then argues that most trans women, conditional on the two-type taxonomy and the non-intersex etiology of late-onset dysphoria, do not pass well and are drawn from the male distribution along most psychological dimensions, so that most trans women aren't just not part of the female cluster in configuration space; they're specifically part of male cluster along most dimensions, which people already have a concept for. The scatterplot that accompanies this (fictitious data, three dimensions, a purple cluster that matches the blue one on two axes and the pink one on the third) is honest about its dependence: People who care more about predicting x₁ and x₂ have reason to prefer categories and corresponding language that group by natal sex; people who care more about predicting x₃ have reason to prefer categories and language that group by gender identity. The figure is a picture of two natural categories and a value-dependent choice between them. Davis drew it himself, in 2018, and the rest of this chapter will keep returning to it.The figure and its caption are in the post; the plotting source is published as an ancillary page, UUT_content/pages/ancillary/categories-scatterplot-source.md, live at http://unremediatedgender.space/ancillary/categories-scatterplot-source/.

The essay's last third leaves philosophy for policy and testimony: two anonymous women on the changing character of "all women" events in a community where, by the 2018 Slate Star Codex survey, trans women were a large share of the women; the author's refusal to say what the right norms are (People should get what they want. We should have social norms that help people get what they want. I don't know what the optimal social norms about transitioning would be.); a photograph of pronoun stickers at an American Philosophical Association meeting; and the lines that gave the essay its reputation: If you need a sticker to get people to gender you correctly, your transition has failed. All you can do is incentivize them to lie. This is not rationality. This isn't even kindness. We're smarter than this. The coda rewrites Alexander's Emperor Norton as a man who one day suspects he is not emperor and asks his advisor for the truth; the advisor, reaching for the categories-are-made-for-man line, finds that you don't believe your own bullshit, tells him, and offers to help him plan what to do now. Davis discloses here that he had himself been delusional the previous year and had been helped by friends who didn't automatically dismiss everything I said as wrong, but who also told me when I wasn't making sense. The parable is the essay's moral core and also its weakest inference: it assumes that the trans person's self-understanding stands to reality as Norton's did, which is the thing in dispute.

"Reply to The Unit of Caring on Adult Human Females"

Two months later Davis answered Kelsey Piper, then writing on Tumblr as The Unit of Caring, who had said she could find no one willing to say what good properties the definition "adult human female" has."Reply to The Unit of Caring on Adult Human Females" (UUT, 19 April 2018), http://unremediatedgender.space/2018/Apr/reply-to-the-unit-of-caring-on-adult-human-females/. Piper's post is login-walled on Tumblr and was not read; her three numbered objections are quoted in Davis's reply. The post is shorter and better organized than its predecessor, and its first point is the best single argument in the 2018 material: the definition has non-circularity. One can list membership tests that do not mention the category; a self-identification criterion cannot, because without a base case, the infinite recursion of (people who identify as (people who identify as (people who identify as ...))) never terminates. Davis then does something the memoirs do not advertise: he constructs a non-circular trans-inclusive sense of the word, adult human females, and also adult human males who have undergone interventions to resemble adult human females sufficiently closely so that they can be taken as such socially, and calls it a mere broadening of the "adult human female" definition that tacks on extra complexity. His complaint is not that the broad sense exists but that the narrow one is being made unsayable: the point is that it should be socially acceptable to describe reality using words. Three dialogues dramatize what is lost when a speaker cannot say That's not what I meant by woman in this context and you know it: a girls' track meet, a meetup whose women were all trans, the Dhejne cohort study's violent-crime rates. The epigraph is a maxim from Alicorn, Thou shalt not strike terms from others' expressive vocabulary without suitable replacement. On Piper's thought experiment of perfect transition technology, Davis gives a conditional answer and ends it with a sentence that should be set beside the APA stickers passage: if psychological sex differences exist and most trans women transition for reasons other than having female minds, then under perfect body-changing technology Honestly, I lean towards Yes, but I can at least see the argument of someone who preferred not to use the word that way.

Put the two 2018 essays together and the position is this. Words have narrower and broader senses, which can be distinguished from context. "Woman" in its sex sense picks out a robust cluster; "woman" in a perceived-sex sense picks out another; a trans-inclusive sense can be defined non-circularly as an extension of the first by the second. None of these is false. What is objectionable is (a) the argument that one may extend a category on grounds of kindness alone, because that is not a reason that bears on the category's function; and (b) the social norm that forbids the narrow sense. Both objections are correct. But notice how much of Alexander's practical conclusion survives them. A contextualist about "woman" who insists only that the sex sense remain available has not shown that "trans women are women" is false; he has shown that it is true in one sense, false in another, and that a speech community choosing which sense to make default is choosing, in Davis's word, a convention.
