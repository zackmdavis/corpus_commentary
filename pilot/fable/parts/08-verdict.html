8. Verdict

The philosophy of categorization in Davis's two blogs is a single sustained argument, begun in 2018 against an essay by Scott Alexander, developed on LessWrong between 2019 and 2021 as a sequel to Yudkowsky's "A Human's Guide to Words," and restated in mature form in 2023. Its core is correct. Alexander's 2014 essay equivocated between "useful for inference" and "useful for someone's welfare," and inferred from the first that a boundary may be drawn for the second; Davis saw this, said it, and was eventually conceded the point by both of the people whose words he was contesting. Along the way he added to Yudkowsky's framework several things that were not in it: the subspace reconciliation of the two dolphin verdicts; the mutual-information analysis of title inflation; squared-error "compactness" as the thing a good category buys; the Schelling-category theory of coordination under limited observation; the root-of-the-causal-graph account of why phylogeny is a thick subspace and why discrete markers make good definitions; the univariate fallacy; and the observation that a predictor which uses a concept cannot replace it. Any of these would be a respectable contribution to a philosophy-of-language seminar, and together they constitute the most rigorous thing anyone in the rationalist community has written on the subject.

The program's reach exceeds its grasp in two places, and both were visible to its author. The theory of categories as prediction devices, with every non-predictive boundary treated as deception or wireheading, cannot accommodate conventional kinds: money, marriage, adulthood, and the social sense of gender are categories whose function is partly to be agreed on, and the Schelling-category post of 2019 already says so. Davis's critics pressed this (Ozy, TAG, Raemon, Wei Dai, Demski, aphyer), he conceded it each time, and the concession never made its way back into the thesis. Second, applied to "woman," the framework licenses two natural categories, perceived sex and developmental sex, and Davis's own 2018 scatterplot is a picture of the value-dependent choice between them. His later insistence that the short word belongs to the second is a claim about which cluster speakers most need, which is a sociolinguistic fact rather than an information-theoretic one, and he does not argue it. The "Truth versus Feelings" framing of the memoirs is thus unavailable to him. On his own theory the Category War was a coordination conflict between two truths, and the fact that it ended with the narrow point conceded and no policy changed is what that theory predicts.

Where I land on the object-level dispute, since the brief asks: Davis is right that it must remain sayable, in plain words, that trans women are male in the developmental sense and that this sense is causally thick; the norm that forbids the sentence is a real cost, and the philosophical arguments offered for the norm in 2014 and 2018 were bad. He is wrong that the broad, socially-anchored sense of "woman" is a lie, a shell game, or a category error, and his best work (Schelling categories, "Reply to Ozymandias," "Challenges," "Beyond the Binary") says so in all but the summary sentences. The sentence he most wanted conceded, Alexander's "there's no rule of rationality saying that I shouldn't," is false as stated, since there are such rules and they have costs; the sentence Alexander should have written, that the costs are worth paying, is the one Davis never engaged, because his theory had defined it as deception.

The empirical premise on which the application rests, that most trans women are drawn from the male distribution along most psychological dimensions, is held with a confidence that the categorization work cannot supply and that Davis's own 2021–22 revisions of the taxonomy partly withdraw. That question is deferred to the chapter on the sexology, where it belongs; the reader should not let the elegance of the categorization mathematics stand in for an answer to it.

Finally, the method. The deniable-allegory posts are a literary achievement and a philosophical one: "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" is good on dolphins whether or not one knows what it is about, and the comment thread shows how much a reader can learn from arguing about dolphins. But the stealth was a strategy for extracting a concession, and it cost its author the thing the posts were supposed to buy: a reception on the merits. gjm's complaint that the error-correction is one-sided is answered by the 2025 post, honestly, as a confession of motive, and that is the right answer. What the memoirs do with the concession, converting a philosophical disagreement that was eventually resolved into a charge of fraud, is the part of the corpus that the philosophy least supports and that a future reader is most likely to remember. The posts deserve to be remembered for the other thing.
