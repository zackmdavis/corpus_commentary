2. The inheritance: Yudkowsky's "Human's Guide to Words" and Alexander's essay

Davis's position cannot be understood without the two texts it is built on and against, and both deserve to be read on their own terms rather than through his summaries of them.

Yudkowsky, 2008

"A Human's Guide to Words" is a run of about thirty posts that Yudkowsky published on Overcoming Bias in February and March of 2008 and later collected under that title. Its governing move is a reduction. A word is not a label with a meaning attached; it is a communication signal whose function is to let one mind cause another mind to reconstruct a concept, and a concept is a piece of inferential machinery. "Disguised Queries" tells the parable of a factory worker sorting blue egg-shaped "bleggs" from red cubical "rubes" and discovering that the question "Is this object a blegg?" has no content beyond the specific predictions it stands in for: which bin it goes in, whether it glows in the dark, whether it contains vanadium. The question "Is this object a blegg?" may stand in for different queries on different occasions. If it weren't standing in for some query, you'd have no reason to care.Eliezer Yudkowsky, "Disguised Queries", 9 February 2008, LessWrong. "How An Algorithm Feels From Inside" explains why the question nevertheless feels as though it has leftover content: the brain's categorizer is a central node with an activation of its own, so that even after you know every characteristic of the object, you still find yourself wondering: "But is it a blegg, or not?"Yudkowsky, "How An Algorithm Feels From Inside", 11 February 2008, LessWrong. "The Cluster Structure of Thingspace" supplies the geometry: objects are points in a very high-dimensional configuration space, categories are clusters, and because the empirical clustered structure of the real universe is not so crystalline, a natural category may have no set of necessary and sufficient properties—no set of characteristics that all group members have, and no non-members have.Yudkowsky, "The Cluster Structure of Thingspace", 8 February 2008, LessWrong.

So far this is anti-essentialism of a familiar kind, and a reader who stopped here could take the sequence as licensing conventionalism: if categories are human inferential conveniences with fuzzy edges, then surely one draws them as one likes. The sequence's second strand exists to block that reading. "Where to Draw the Boundary?" (the singular title matters later) says that the challenge worthy of a rationalist is figuring out which things are similar to each other—which things are clustered together—and sometimes, which things have a common cause, calls a boundary that loops around and swerves through the clusters a wrong word, and delivers the line that Davis would later quote against its author more than once: Or you could stop playing nitwit games and admit that dolphins don't belong on the fish list.Yudkowsky, "Where to Draw the Boundary?", 21 February 2008, LessWrong. "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace" gives the strand its only piece of mathematics: a word for a conjunction of properties earns a shorter code than the conjunction itself only when the properties co-occur more often than their marginal probabilities predict, so that the way to carve reality at its joints, is to draw your boundaries around concentrations of unusually high probability density in Thingspace, and One may even consider the act of defining a word as a promise to this effect.Yudkowsky, "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace", 23 February 2008, LessWrong. "Conditional Independence, and Naive Bayes" explains why a category node is worth having at all: when observing a central variable screens the surrounding features off from one another, the inferential mess becomes tractable. "Categorizing Has Consequences" adds the psychological point that drawing a boundary in thingspace is not a neutral act, because a human brain, once given a label, begins harvesting similarities whether or not they exist.Yudkowsky, "Categorizing Has Consequences", 19 February 2008, LessWrong. The summary post, "37 Ways That Words Can Be Wrong," opens by saying that saying "There's no way my choice of X can be 'wrong'" is nearly always an error in practice, whatever the theory and closes on item 37: You think that definitions can't be "wrong", or that "I can define a word any way I like!" This kind of attitude teaches you to indignantly defend your past actions, instead of paying attention to their consequences, or fessing up to your mistakes.Yudkowsky, "37 Ways That Words Can Be Wrong", 6 March 2008, LessWrong.

Two further posts from the same year complicate the picture in ways Davis would have to deal with. In "Unnatural Categories" Yudkowsky coins that phrase for a category whose boundary you draw in a way that sensitively depends on the exact values built into your utility function, gives "poison" and "person" and "true happiness" as examples, and adds: I don't mean to imply that unnatural categories are worthless or relative or whatever. That's what moral arguments are for—for drawing and redrawing the boundaries.Yudkowsky, "Unnatural Categories", 24 August 2008, LessWrong. That is a concession that some category boundaries are legitimately fixed by what we care about and not by the statistics of the territory; Davis's 2021 post with the near-identical title would have to narrow it. And in "Changing Emotions" (January 2009) Yudkowsky took sex change as the archetype of a transformation that is easy to name and enormously hard to do: it's not like people have a binary attribute reading "M" or "F" that can be flipped as a primitive action; remapping body and pleasure circuitry would produce a normal, masculine man wearing a female body like a suit of clothing; and If I fell asleep and woke up as a true woman—not in body, but in brain—I don't think I'd call her "me".Yudkowsky, "Changing Emotions", 5 January 2009, LessWrong. Davis reports encountering the 2004 mailing-list post from which it was revised in June 2008 and describes its effect on him in "Sexual Dimorphism in Yudkowsky's Sequences, in Relation to My Gender Problems" (UUT, 2 May 2021), http://unremediatedgender.space/2021/May/sexual-dimorphism-in-the-sequences-in-relation-to-my-gender-problems/. Davis would read this post as a statement that sex is a high-dimensional cluster that cannot be entered by fiat, and he is not wrong to; but the post's own subject is the difficulty of mind-editing, and it does not say anything about what the word "woman" should mean.

Where does this sit in the philosophical literature that Yudkowsky does not cite? Closest to Quine's "Natural Kinds" (1969), which ties kinds to similarity and similarity to what confirms an induction, and to the cluster-kind realism that grew out of it. The Stanford Encyclopedia's summary of the latter could serve as a gloss on "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace": kinds are families of such properties that are contingently clustered in nature, and A natural kind is any such family of co-occurring properties that may be employed in inductive inferences or for the purposes of scientific explanation.Alexander Bird and Emma Tobin, "Natural Kinds", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 1.2.2, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/natural-kinds/. Read in the version fetched 16 October 2026. Boyd's version adds that the clustering is sustained by a homeostatic mechanism, which will matter when Davis arrives, in 2021, at the idea that a variable at the root of a causal graph is what makes a cluster robust. What Yudkowsky adds to that tradition is not metaphysics but engineering: the claim that the same criterion governs a Bayesian classifier and a human vocabulary, and the pedagogical insistence that it be stated in the objectivist register ("words can be wrong") rather than the relativist one. Davis later reports Alexander wondering, on a train in 2019, why the moral had not been phrased as "you can define a word any way you want, but then you have to deal with the consequences," and records his own answer: If you write your summary slogan in relativist language, people predictably take that as license to believe whatever they want without having to defend it."A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning". The exchange is reported from memory and from Davis's side only. The whole of the subsequent dispute can be read as a quarrel over that pedagogical choice.

Alexander, 2014

"The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories" (21 November 2014) is a six-part essay, and its argument has a distinct shape in each part that the later controversy tended to blur.Scott Alexander, "The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories", Slate Star Codex, 21 November 2014, read in the Wayback Machine copy linked from the corpus, archived. Part I is about King Solomon and whales. The Hebrew dag picks out finned sea creatures; a modern speaker's "fish" picks out a phylogenetic group; the two words are different words, and there is no fact of the matter on whether a whale is a fish. The argument is entirely semantic. Part II takes over Yudkowsky's own account of Pluto and the planets (it cites "How An Algorithm Feels From Inside") and concludes that when an object satisfies half the criteria for one category and half for another, it's a judgment call which you have to decide by fiat. Part III shifts to the metaphor of national borders: borders can be stupid, but in no case can an agreed-upon set of borders or a category boundary be factually incorrect. Part IV applies this to gender. The anti-transgender argument Alexander says he takes seriously is that rationalists must call a man who thinks he is a woman a man and take the consequences; his reply is An alternative categorization system is not an error, and borders are not objectively true or false. He then describes the transgender movement's project as a switch from using chromosomes as a tiebreaker to using self-identification as a tiebreaker, and ends the part with the sentence everything turned on:

If I'm willing to accept an unexpected chunk of Turkey deep inside Syrian territory to honor some random dead guy – and I better, or else a platoon of Turkish special forces will want to have a word with me – then I ought to accept an unexpected man or two deep inside the conceptual boundaries of what would normally be considered female if it'll save someone's life. There's no rule of rationality saying that I shouldn't, and there are plenty of rules of human decency saying that I should.

Part V is the hair-dryer story: a patient whose obsessive fear of having left her hair dryer on was cured by carrying the hair dryer in her car, and the moral that the primary thing in psychiatry is to help the patient, whatever the means. Part VI answers the "trans-Napoleon" objection, partly by gesturing at the various heavily researched apparent biological correlates of transgender, and ends with Emperor Norton of San Francisco, humored by his fellow citizens, as one of the most heartwarming episodes in the history of one of my favorite places in the world.

The essay is doing two things that it does not distinguish. The first is a thesis about which dimensions matter: whether to sort sea creatures by habitat or ancestry depends on whether you run a fishing fleet or a genetics lab, and no fact decides it. That thesis is true, is Yudkowsky's, and is the content of Parts I through III. The second is a thesis about where to draw the line once the dimensions are fixed: that one may accept "an unexpected man or two" inside the boundary of "female" because doing so will save a life. That thesis is not supported by the first, because the reason offered for it is not a claim about similarity along any dimension at all. The Turkish exclave is a weird border, but it is a border on the same map; the question is whether the analogy carries over to a category whose job is inference. Part II, which Alexander took from Yudkowsky, already says what categories are for: to sweep all common correlations into one big category in the middle. A boundary that does not track the correlations fails at that job however decent the motive. Ozy Brennan, no ally of Davis on the object level, saw the equivocation at once in 2018: Taylor points out that there are totally shenanigans going on here. There are two different senses of the word "useful."Ozy Brennan, "Man Should Allocate Some More Categories", Thing of Things, 18 June 2018, https://thingofthings.wordpress.com/2018/06/18/man-should-allocate-some-more-categories/. "Taylor" is Davis, then writing under the pen name M. Taylor Saotome-Westlake. So, eventually, did Alexander. The note he appended in December 2019 reads in part: While nothing is stopping you from defining a word any way you want, you should definitely worry about it. He added that plausible definitions will be limited to a few possibilities suggested by the territory, with worsening penalties as you deviate from these further. That is a long way from "not an error," and the distance was travelled under pressure from Davis.

Two things should be said in Alexander's defense before Davis's reply is examined. The first is that Part VI does gesture at an empirical argument, the biological correlates, which is the argument Davis himself says a non-question-begging case would have to make. The second is that Alexander's conclusion is a claim about a policy, what to call people, and the essay's confusion lies in dressing a policy argument as a point about the metaphysics of categories. Strip the dressing and the position is: there is no uniquely correct boundary; this boundary, which costs little, helps someone a lot; so adopt it. That is not a philosophical error. It is a utilitarian bid in a coordination problem. Whether it is a good bid depends on how little it costs, and the cost is exactly what Davis set out to compute.
