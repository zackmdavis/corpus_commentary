5. Objections

I take five objections in turn, in roughly increasing order of how much of Davis's program they touch. The first two were pressed by his critics and he answered them, partly. The third and fourth are my own, built from his concessions. The fifth concerns the empirical premise and is only sketched, because the full treatment belongs to the sexology chapter.

(a) Conventional kinds

Ozy Brennan's 2018 reply made the point with money. Bitcoin, gold, dollar bills, and prison cigarettes are all money, and what makes them money is that everyone agrees; this is not circular, since the definition is "any item or verifiable record that is generally accepted as payment for goods and services and repayment of debts in a particular country or socio-economic context." Social gender is like money. It predicts pronouns, gendered terms, holidays, who gets to hold the baby, who gets harassed on the street, and so forth; and "you're a woman if you identify as a woman" is not a definition but a criterion for who should be put in the category, so that the decision of who should be socially gendered male or female, and how many social genders we should have is not an epistemic decision. This decision can and should be made on purely utilitarian grounds. On the women's-events problem Ozy had a sharper reply still: The actual category they should be using is not "cis women." The actual category they should be using is "people who would be contribute to the atmosphere you made this a woman-only event for." Allocate more categories; do not fight over the old one.

Davis's answer came eighteen months later in "Reply to Ozymandias on Fully Consensual Gender," and it is better than his critics usually allow. He accepts the analogy and turns it: not any form of money will serve, because perishable goods cannot store value and non-fungible ones cannot be a unit of account; by Gresham's law, in a market that is required to treat defaced Canadian five-dollar notes as American, smart buyers will prefer to buy with defaced Canadian dollars, and smart sellers will try to find plausibly-deniable excuses to not accept them; and so with gender: even if everyone says the words, people following their own self-interest are likely to systematically make decisions that treat non-well-passing trans women as if they were something more like men, even if no one would dream of being so rude as to admit out loud that that's what they're doing. "Consent" is the wrong word, because it names an asymmetry of interests where there is none, and resolving a conflict between a person's wish to be modeled one way and another's perception in the direction of "consent of the modeled" would amount to mind control, or at least compelled speech. Fully consensual gender, he concludes, is a coherent position. That doesn't make it feasible."Reply to Ozymandias on Fully Consensual Gender" (UUT, 20 December 2019), http://unremediatedgender.space/2019/Dec/reply-to-ozymandias-on-fully-consensual-gender/.

Notice what this argument is and is not. It is an argument that a convention must be backstopped by enforcement and that a convention which contradicts what people perceive will leak. It is not an argument that conventional kinds are illegitimate or that their boundaries are "deceptive." On the contrary, Davis grants that money is a convention whose feasibility depends on many facts of economics and sociology, and that fiat currency, which would once have provoked "how dumb do you think I am," now circulates. The money analogy therefore cuts both ways. If a convention can carry fiat paper from absurdity to universality, the question about a gender convention is a question about feasibility, cost, and enforcement, which is a utilitarian question of just the kind Ozy said it was. Davis's 2020 LessWrong comment on this point concedes the structure while refusing the conclusion: coordination, deception, and self-fulfilling prophecy are three "complications" to the prediction-only theory, and of the third he writes, I'm not sure I understand the theory of this yet. His conclusion in the same comment, that it's not the map's job to provide compensatory usefulness; the map's job is to reflect the territory, is true of maps and false of money. The commenter TAG put it in one line: You can make predictions about money, but that is not the central purpose of money.Davis's comment of 13 December 2020, LessWrong; TAG's reply of 31 December 2020. Raemon seconded TAG, finding a pretty gaping hole in your arguments that have something-or-other-to-do-with "but, like, coordination tho". So the first objection stands, with a qualification. Davis is right that a conventional category whose convention outruns perception will be unstable and will be privately defected from; "fully consensual gender" is not a free lunch. He is wrong that this makes the convention a kind of lie rather than a kind of institution with costs.

There is a philosophical literature exactly here, and Davis does not engage it. Sally Haslanger's "ameliorative" project defines gender terms by what we want them to do for us, and defines "woman" as a position of systematic subordination marked by presumed female reproductive role; the Stanford Encyclopedia's summary notes that on this definition the Queen of England may not count as a woman, and that Haslanger accepts the revisionary result because the definitions are not meant to capture our intuitive gender terms.Mari Mikkola, "Feminist Perspectives on Sex and Gender", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 4.2.1, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/feminism-gender/. That is the position Davis is attacking, held explicitly and with its reasons given; it is also, as Robin Dembroff points out, a project that ties prescription to description rather than abandoning the latter.Robin Dembroff, "Escaping the natural attitude about gender", Philosophical Studies 178 (2021), 983–1003, at 994. Dembroff's own suggestion, that in political contexts "woman" and "man" function as floating signifiers that are doing normative, political work more so than descriptive work, is the same observation as Wei Dai's 2024 comment that "trans women are women" may be a signal of local values, trying to imply something like "people around here do not distinguish between trans women and cis women in their values, at least in most circumstances".Dembroff, 992–93; Wei_Dai, comment of 18 January 2024, LessWrong. Davis's reply to Wei Dai was that the value should then be argued for on the merits rather than smuggled into a noun. That is fair. But a theory on which every non-predictive use of a category is deception has no room for the ordinary fact that calling someone a woman can be a way of saying how one intends to treat her, and that fact does not go away because one disapproves of it.

(b) Deception overreach

The second objection is to the title of the 2021 post. Several commenters noticed that an openly declared category system is not functionally a lie. tslarm: If I'm honest about the boundaries of my new category system, how is this deceptive? Daniel Filan: redefining "book" to include vegan chocolate bars and redefining it to include a small number of random other things are not equivalent, and only the second resembles reserving the right to lie.tslarm, comment of 14 June 2025; DanielFilan, comment of 9 January 2021; both on "Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception", LessWrong. Davis's answer, in both cases, is to point to "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!": deception is defined relative to the cooperative subgame, and a usage that is priced in has not thereby stopped being optimized against the receiver's interest. But that reply proves too much. It makes "deceptive" a property of a usage's history (what it was optimized for) rather than of its current effect on anyone's beliefs, which is the sense in which the firefly mimic remains "deceptive" after the prey have adjusted. Under that definition every convention that was once contested is deceptive forever, including the ones Davis likes.

Davis himself has the materials for a more modest claim. In "Comment on 'Deception as Cooperation'" he bites the bullet and retreats to "strive to notice the difference." In the containment thread and in the long correspondence with Abram Demski that he narrates in the memoir, he goes further. Demski had asked whether, ideally, all conceptual boundaries should not be drawn with appeal to consequences, since the reason Bayesianism is an attractor is that probabilistic reasoning is useful; a design with a separate epistemic module answerable to nothing but accuracy is, in Demski's words as Davis reports them, a split-brain in which an instrumental task-monkey does all the important stuff while an epistemic overseer supervises. Davis's reply, fourteen months later: on further thought, he was right, and it made sense to regard "have accurate beliefs" as merely a convergent instrumental subgoal, rather than what rationality is about—as sacrilegious as that felt to type."If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them", sections "A Worthy Critic At Last (November 2019)" and "A False Dénouement (January 2021)". Demski's own LessWrong comment of February 2021 defends "pure epistemics" as a coherent flag to rally around while admitting that I don't think we can always ground pure epistemic talk in consequences, LessWrong. If accuracy is instrumental, then Alexander's "nothing to make those decisions on except consequences" is not a fallacy but the correct meta-level description, and the real disagreement is about which consequences count and over what horizon. Davis's practical answer, that consequences for the model's accuracy count and consequences for third parties' feelings do not, is a defensible rule for a truth-seeking institution. It is not a theorem about intelligent systems, and the 2021 post presents it as one.

(c) Polysemy, and the dilemma that goes both ways

The third objection is that Davis is a contextualist about "woman" who argues as if he were not. The evidence is in his own texts: "it's not uncommon for words to have both narrower and broader definitions which can be distinguished from context" (2018); water(1) includes ice and water(2) does not (2021); fish(1) and fish(2); berry(1) and berry(2); and in the containment thread, when TekhneMakre proposed that a word for "the cluster of people who want to be treated the way women are treated" is legitimate, Davis agreed that it is fine where unambiguous and said the problem is only that a dominant faction in my Society really doesn't want it to be socially permissible to disambiguate.Davis, comment of 27 February 2022 on the containment thread, LessWrong. This is the view that Jennifer Saul, Talia Bettcher, Elizabeth Barnes, and Dembroff defend in the philosophical literature under the names contextualism and polysemy, and that Alex Byrne, the philosopher whose conclusion ("women are adult human females") Davis's most resembles, treats as the main obstacle to his thesis. Byrne's "polysemy puzzle" is Davis's dilemma from the other side: if "woman" has several meanings, shouldn't one of these meanings correspond to the category adult human female? Why wouldn't it?Alex Byrne, "Gender Muddle: Reply to Dembroff", Journal of Controversial Ideas 1(1) (2021), section 4. Davis would say yes, obviously, and that is all he has ever asked.

But once the view is stated that way, the thing Davis spent years calling a philosophy-of-language error shrinks to a dispute about which sense should be default and whether the other sense may be spoken. Those are questions about social norms, not about whether categories can be wrong. Davis's genuine dilemma for the other side is this: either "woman" is polysemous, in which case "trans women are women" is true in the broad sense, uninformative about the narrow one, and no reason to forbid the narrow one; or it is univocal, in which case the sentence is a substantive claim that needs an argument. That dilemma is sound and it is his best weapon. The matching dilemma for Davis is that if "woman" is polysemous, then insisting that the narrow sense is "the" natural category, the one that "carves reality at the joints," is itself a choice among joints, and the choice is governed, by his own theory, by what speakers want to talk about and can coordinate on. He concedes as much whenever a critic says it. What he does not concede is that the concession dissolves the frame of "Truth versus Feelings."

(d) Two natural categories

Which brings me to the objection I think is decisive against the polemic, if not against the philosophy. Davis's framework licenses at least two natural categories in the neighborhood of "woman," and his writing moves between them without ever settling which one the short word belongs to.

The first is perceived sex: the output of the pre-verbal, subconscious, System 1 process that notices breasts, facial structure, voice, gait, and so on, which, as he says, is not directly focused on genitalia or chromosomes. This category is natural by every criterion in the program: it is what the classifier in people's heads computes, it supports a vast range of inferences, and passing trans people are inside it. Davis affirms this in 2018, affirms in the dolphin-war draft that she is obviously the correct pronoun for people who look like women, and in "Challenges" affirms that he does not misgender people in public. The second is developmental sex: the cluster rooted at the SRY gene, which, as "Blood Is Thicker Than Water" and "Beyond the Binary" argue, buys conditional independence across every downstream feature including ones not yet discovered, and which no current intervention changes. This category is natural too, and more robust. Davis's later writing treats it as the referent: in 2024 he says the thing one must be able to say in public is that trans women are male and trans men are female with respect to a concept of binary sex that encompasses the many traits that aren't affected by contemporary surgical and hormonal interventions."Agreeing With Stalin in Ways That Exhibit Generally Rationalist Principles" (UUT, 2 March 2024), http://unremediatedgender.space/2024/Mar/agreeing-with-stalin-in-ways-that-exhibit-generally-rationalist-principles/, section "The Battle That Matters".

Both claims are true "with respect to" their categories, as Davis's phrasing acknowledges. The question that his framework poses and that he never squarely answers is: which of these should the top-20 noun be reserved for, and why? The program's own answer, from "Blood Is Thicker Than Water," is that the short word tends to go to the thicker subspace and to the category one needs most often (You'll probably end up talking about {u, w} more often than {u, v} because the former cluster is in a "thicker", higher-dimensional subspace). But that is a claim about usage frequency and decision relevance, and in everyday social life the decisions people make with gender words (pronouns, forms of address, who to flirt with, which bathroom) are overwhelmingly driven by perceived sex, not karyotype. Davis knows this; it is why he grants that passing works and why "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point" treats the self-ID convention as a solution to disagreements about perceived sex. The cases where developmental sex is decision-relevant (medicine, reproduction, sport, sex-segregated spaces) are real and he lists them, but they are the cases for which, as Ozy said and he conceded, more specific vocabulary is available and in use. So the dispute over the default sense of "woman" is, on Davis's own analysis, a conflict between two natural categories, in which one side wants the word's default to track perception and self-report (a Schelling point with costs) and the other wants it to track development (a Schelling point with costs). The memoir's framing, a battle between Feelings and Truth, between Politics and Truth, is unavailable to someone who holds the theory of the 2019 posts. The battle is between two truths and the policy question of which to make default. That is what Alexander said in 2014, badly.

I do not think Davis would accept this restatement, and I can state his best reply. It is that the two categories are not symmetric: perceived sex is a proxy for developmental sex, an evolved detector whose whole function is to estimate the thing that matters for mating, so that when the proxy and the target come apart the proxy has misfired, and a convention that enshrines the misfire enshrines an error. That is the point of the mimicry section of "Unnatural Categories" and of the sentence about the trans woman who looks like a male celebrity: passing as a woman isn't the same thing as actually being female. The reply has force, and it is why the program is not simply refuted. But it depends on a further premise: that the thing people mostly want gender words for is the target rather than the proxy. For a sexologist, a physician, or a man deciding whom to date, that is true. For most of the rest of social life it is the proxy that does the work, as Davis's own account of "social gender" in 2018 and of Julia Serano's transition in "Crossing the Line" (2020) describes."Crossing the Line" (UUT, 17 December 2020), http://unremediatedgender.space/2020/Dec/crossing-the-line/, reads Serano's account of being treated differently after transition as evidence that perceived sex triggers mental models trained against the distribution of adult human females. Which premise is right is an empirical question about how a speech community uses its words, and it is not settled by information theory.

(e) The empirical premise

Finally, the empirical premise that most trans women are, along most psychological dimensions, drawn from the male distribution. The chapter on sexology examines the two-type taxonomy; here I note only how the categorization argument depends on it and what the mathematics does and does not establish.

The univariate fallacy is a fallacy, and Davis is right to deploy it against the inference from overlap-per-trait to indistinguishability. The brain-mosaic dispute is the cleanest test case. Daphna Joel and colleagues reported in 2015 that across more than 1,400 MRI scans most brains are comprised of unique "mosaics" of features, some more common in females compared with males, some more common in males compared with females, and some common in both females and males, and concluded that human brains do not belong to one of two distinct categories: male brain/female brain.Daphna Joel et al., "Sex beyond the genitalia: The human brain mosaic", PNAS 112(50) (2015), abstract; retrieved via the Europe PMC API (see sources.md). The full text sits behind a CAPTCHA at PMC and was not read. Marco Del Giudice and colleagues replied that the method systematically fails to detect large, consistent sex differences.Marco Del Giudice, Richard A. Lippa, David A. Puts, Drew H. Bailey, J. Michael Bailey, and David P. Schmitt, "Joel et al.'s method systematically fails to detect large, consistent sex differences", PNAS 113(14) (2016); title and metadata only via Europe PMC. Davis cites the cogprints preprint of this critique in "Sexual Dimorphism"; it was blocked by an anti-bot page when I tried to fetch it. Joel's own online response concedes the point that matters for Davis: predicting sex from brain structure can clearly be done, and I am sure with much better accuracy than Del Guidice and colleagues achieved; what her group denies is the reverse inference, from sex category to an individual's mosaic.Daphna Joel, "Response to 'Mosaic Brains?…'", online document, 23 December 2015, read in full (see sources.md). Davis's summary in 2021, that lack of "internal consistency" is exactly the behavior we expect from multivariate normal-ish distributions with different-but-not-vastly-different means, is therefore a fair reading of a dispute in which both sides agree on the classification accuracy and disagree about which direction of inference the word "dimorphic" should track. Which is to say: the mosaic dispute is itself a dispute about what the categories are for, and Davis's side of it is the side that cares about classifying individuals by sex.

What the mathematics does not establish is where trans women fall. The 2018 scatterplot is fictitious by its own caption. The claim that the purple cluster sits with the blue one on most axes rests on the etiological thesis that late-onset gender dysphoria in males is mostly not an intersex condition, and that thesis is contested in the literature and, in Davis's own later work, softened: by 2022 he describes the two-type taxonomy as a useful approximation for this causal model, a model in which transition is a threshold on the sum of femininity, autogynephilia, and cultural factors, and in which one can buy decent predictive accuracy by casually speaking as if there were two discrete "types", and in 2021 he published a post titled "You Are Right and I Was Wrong" conceding to tailcalled that Different causal theories can generate the same correlations in a particular set of observations, while still making meaningfully and drastically different claims about the world."The Two-Type Taxonomy Is a Useful Approximation for a More Detailed Causal Model" (UUT, 8 July 2022), http://unremediatedgender.space/2022/Jul/the-two-type-taxonomy-is-a-useful-approximation-for-a-more-detailed-causal-model/; "You Are Right and I Was Wrong: Reply to Tailcalled on Causality" (UUT, 1 February 2021), http://unremediatedgender.space/2021/Feb/you-are-right-and-i-was-wrong-reply-to-tailcalled-on-causality/. The categorization argument is explicitly conditional on the sexology, and Davis has always said so. The reader should hold the two apart: the laws of categorization that the LessWrong posts articulate do not depend on any claim about trans women; the application to trans women depends on a claim that the laws cannot supply.
