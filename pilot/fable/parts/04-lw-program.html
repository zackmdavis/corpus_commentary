4. The Less Wrong program (2019–2021)

Between April 2019 and November 2021 Davis moved the argument from his pseudonymous gender blog to LessWrong, the forum built around Yudkowsky's Sequences, and recast it without its gender content. He later described the first of these posts as a "stealth reply" to Alexander and to a November 2018 Twitter thread of Yudkowsky's, quoting/paraphrasing without attribution in order to reply to the philosophical substance while eliding the political context, and in December 2020 he put up a "containment thread" acknowledging that the reason I've been doing so much philosophy-of-language blogging lately is because I was frustrated because I thought a lot of people were motivatedly getting the philosophy of language wrong for political convenience, specifically around transgender issues."Containment Thread on the Motivation and Political Context for My Philosophy of Language Agenda", LessWrong, 10 December 2020, https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5aqumaym7Jd2qhDcy/containment-thread-on-the-motivation-and-political-context. The post exists only on LessWrong; it is in LW_content/. The stealth was not very stealthy, and I return to its cost in section 7. Here I take the posts as the philosophy they claim to be, because they are also that.

"Where to Draw the Boundaries?"

The plural in the title answers Yudkowsky's singular. "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" (13 April 2019) is a dialogue with "the one," an interlocutor whose lines are unattributed quotations from Alexander and Yudkowsky, and its first move is the reconciliation of their two dolphin verdicts. Yudkowsky in 2008 had said dolphins don't belong on the fish list; Alexander in 2014 had said that was a judgment call. Davis's answer: both are right, in different subspaces."Where to Draw the Boundaries?" (AAL, 13 April 2019; originally on LessWrong), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Apr/where-to-draw-the-boundaries/; LessWrong copy at https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/esRZaPXSHgWzyB2NL/where-to-draw-the-boundaries.

When rationalists say that definitions can be wrong, we don't mean that there's a unique category boundary that is the True floating essence of a word, and that all other possible boundaries are wrong. We mean that in order for a proposed category boundary to not be wrong, it needs to capture some statistical structure in reality, even if reality is surprisingly detailed and there can be more than one such structure.

Thingspace is not well defined, he concedes, but one can form a space from whatever properties one cares about, and Different choices of properties correspond to different cross sections of the grander Thingspace. Dolphins cluster with fish in the subspace of swimming-related dimensions and with mammals in another; the sailors' word was not wrong within its domain, and a modern biologist's word is better because the biologist's model has more dimensions in it. Then the interlocutor makes Alexander's move in its purest form: Everything we identify as a joint is only a joint because we care about it. Davis's reply is the thesis of the whole program in one sentence: No. Everything we identify as a joint is a joint not "because we care about it", but because it helps us think about the things we care about. Values choose the subspace; the territory supplies the clusters within it. The distinction he wants is between having a utility function defined over a statistical model's performance against specific real-world data (even if another mind with different values would be interested in different data), and having a utility function defined over features of the model itself.

Two numerical examples carry the weight. In the first, "Foo" names a cluster centered at [1, 2, 3] in three dimensions and "Quux" one at [2, 4, 6]; someone who redefines Foo to cover both and then, on seeing a first coordinate of 2, still predicts 4 and 6 for the others is not using the new concept but only mouthing it, because a genuine Foo-with-the-new-definition would predict 3 and 4.5. The point is that You can't change the categories your mind actually uses and still perform as well on prediction tasks—although you can change your verbally reported categories. In the second, a worker is "promoted" to Vice President of Sorting with no change in pay, tasks, or authority. Davis models a four-property Jobspace, computes that the mutual information between title and pay falls from about 0.918 bits to about 0.2516 bits once half the low-pay jobs get high titles, and concludes that all that's actually been accomplished is making language less useful. The post ends by proposing a thirty-eighth way that words can be wrong, 38. Your definition draws a boundary around a cluster in an inappropriately 'thin' subspace of Thingspace that excludes relevant variables, resulting in fallacies of compression., and with a pastiche of Yudkowsky's Musashi: Do not ask whether there's a rule of rationality saying that you shouldn't call dolphins fish. Ask whether dolphins are fish. One parenthesis deserves notice because the 2021 sequel grows out of it: if you're actually trying to optimize other people's models of the world, rather than the world itself—you could just lie, rather than playing clever category-gerrymandering mind games. It would be a lot simpler!

The post is good, and the reception on LessWrong was substantive on both sides. Said Achmiz's praise has been quoted. David Hornbein, in the 2019 Review, called it a crushingly well-argued piece of philosophy of language and its relation to reasoning and observed, double-edgedly, that "write a nigh-irrefutable argument about philosophy of language, in order to strike at the heart of the substantive disagreement which provoked the social conflict" is an effective social move in this community.David Hornbein, comment of 17 December 2020, LessWrong. Wei Dai asked for a concrete case and, given Alexander's "eargreyish" (mice and elephants grouped as grey animals with large ears), pressed that a thin subspace is sometimes exactly the right one, as when a biologist hypothesizes that grey skin and big ears are what let prey escape a new predator; Davis conceded that and added the mimicry examples that reappear in 2021.Exchange of 21 April 2019 beginning at commentId 9SeYXBqX8H2zKGNBs. The deepest objection came later and I hold it for section 5: the moderator Raemon's complaint that the Musashi peroration is motte-and-bailey-ing between "These are the rules of thinking rationally, for forming accurate beliefs, and for communicating about that in particular contexts" and "these are the rules of communicating, in whatever circumstances you might find yourself."Raemon, comment of 21 January 2021, LessWrong.

High dimensions and simple tests

"The Univariate Fallacy" (June 2019) is a short demonstration, with Python, that two distributions can overlap on every single coordinate and still be separable with supreme, godlike confidence when forty coordinates are taken together."The Univariate Fallacy" (AAL, 15 June 2019), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Jun/the-univariate-fallacy/. Davis credits a tweet by Steven Pinker for the name and notes Yudkowsky's suggested alternatives. The mathematics is elementary (independent coordinates, likelihood ratios multiplied), and the point it makes is correct and important: "the distributions overlap on this variable" does not entail "the groups are not distinguishable." The post says nothing about sex. It did not need to; it was the mathematical core of the 2018 scatterplot, and in "Sexual Dimorphism in Yudkowsky's Sequences" (2021) Davis applies it explicitly, noting that most of the probability mass of a high-dimensional distribution sits in a shell away from the mode, so that The typical point in a high-dimensional distribution is not typical along each dimension individually."Sexual Dimorphism in Yudkowsky's Sequences, in Relation to My Gender Problems", section anchored "typical-point". I assess the empirical use of this in section 5.

"Schelling Categories, and Simple Membership Tests" (August 2019) is the post in which the program first qualifies itself, and it is the most philosophically careful thing in the sequence."Schelling Categories, and Simple Membership Tests" (AAL, 25 August 2019), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Aug/schelling-categories-and-simple-membership-tests/. Take the forty-dimensional example again, but suppose two agents must make the same decision about each point while each observes only one coordinate. Add a forty-first binary coordinate that is more informative than any single one of the others. Then the agents may rationally define their shared category by that coordinate alone, even if x₄₁ itself has no effect on the quality of your decisions, because it is the best single observation and, being salient, a Schelling point. The conclusion: the right categories to use given constraints on communication and observation, might be different from the category boundaries you would draw from a "God's eye view", in part because consideration of which categories are easy for different agents to coordinate on is relevant, not just raw information-theoretic expressive power. The applications are the age of majority (replacing a complicated, illegible category with a simple membership test (an age cutoff that everyone knows how to compute)), commodity grading (delegation to an authority), and tribal rallying flags, including the option of delegating the classification problem to the person themselves as the authority whose discernment is to be trusted. The post warns that simple tests invite regressional Goodhart and delegation invites adversarial Goodhart, the gaming of the test, and ends: coordination is costly.

This is the apparatus that Davis then turned against his own side. "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point" (October 2019), on the gender blog, announces an apparently novel argument in favor of the self-identity criterion: if social reality requires that everyone agree on one answer to what sex a person is, and passing is too blurry to serve, then the natural Schelling point is, "Just ask the person what gender they are, and that's what they are." Not because people know themselves best, but because people are still the focal point for reasonable mutual expectations about knowledge about themselves. He then observes that the two extreme conventions are both stable Schelling points, chromosomes-only (It would be cruel to trans people and people with complete androgen insensitivity syndrome, but it would be a Schelling point.) and self-report-only, while any of a number of "compromise" systems, while potentially performing better on edge cases, suffer from ambiguity and are on that account less game-theoretically stable."Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point" (UUT, 6 October 2019), http://unremediatedgender.space/2019/Oct/self-identity-is-a-schelling-point/. The follow-up "More Schelling" (UUT, 22 December 2019) uses Thomas Schelling's daylight-saving-time example to explain why the fight is over language rather than any particular policy. I want to flag how much this concedes, because the memoirs never do. The post locates the dispute over "woman" inside the theory of coordination, where Davis's own framework says the answer is value-dependent and where the self-identity convention has a structural advantage. It does not say the convention is false. It says it is a Schelling point with costs. That is the correct description of the conflict, and it is Alexander's description with the metaphysical dressing removed.

Lying, meaning, and deception

The autumn 1919 posts on lying belong to this chapter because Davis used them to test the categorization theory on a word other than "woman." "Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist" (October 2019) answers Alexander's "Against Lie Inflation," which had argued that stretching "lie" to cover motivated reasoning would make the word useless and everyone angrier."Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist" (AAL, 14 October 2019), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Oct/maybe-lying-doesnt-exist/; Scott Alexander, "Against Lie Inflation", Slate Star Codex, 16 July 2019 (read in the archived copy listed in sources.md). Davis's objection is that this is an appeal to consequences, and that appeal-to-consequences is an invalid form of reasoning for optimal-categorization questions for essentially the same reason as it is for questions of simple fact. If saying that Elijah is lying will make Elijah angry, that's not an argument for changing the definition of lying; that's an argument that I have an incentive to lie about whether I think Elijah is lying! Then a second, more speculative point: the folk intension of "lie" (conscious intent to deceive) may fail to match the extension we most need the word for, people motivatedly saying convenient things without bothering to check whether they're true. The post is frank that Davis himself frowns on the expanded sense; his quarrel is with the reasons for frowning.

Alexander replied in the comments two months later, and the exchange is the clearest statement of the standing disagreement between them. "Appeal to consequences" is only a fallacy in reasoning about factual states of the world. In most cases, appealing to consequences is the right action. Language, he wrote, draws on the structure of reality, but has many free variables, so that the structure of reality doesn't constrain it completely, and for the free variables we have nothing to make those decisions on except consequences.Yvain (Scott Alexander), comment of 22 December 2019, LessWrong. Davis's first reply was that he could not muster the willpower to engage if you're going to motivatedly play dumb like this; Alexander answered I absolutely deny I am "motivatedly playing dumb"; a conversation in person followed, and Alexander's editorial note on the 2014 essay was one result.Comments of 24–26 December 2019 in the same thread; the in-person meeting is narrated in "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them". The exchange matters because Alexander's position is not stupid. If a word's extension is underdetermined by the territory, something other than the territory has to fix it. Davis's eventual answer, in "Unnatural Categories," is that the something must still be a prediction-shaped criterion, a metric on the variables you care about, and that any other criterion is suspect. Whether that answer works is the question of section 5.

Two companion posts push the theory of deception toward mechanism. "Algorithms of Deception!" (October 2019) gives four Python "Reporters" that pass observations to an "Audience": one faithful, one that always reports a 4, one that selectively omits, one that collapses 1 and 4 into "oneorfour," and shows the last two distorting the audience's posterior while each Reporter, personified, insists it never said anything false."Algorithms of Deception!" (AAL, 19 October 2019), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Oct/algorithms-of-deception/. "Firming Up Not-Lying Around Its Edge-Cases Is Less Broadly Useful Than One Might Initially Think" (December 2019) argues against Yudkowsky's "meta-honesty" that a rule of never uttering a literally false sentence is weak protection, since a motivated speaker will end up accidentally training yourself to get really good at misleading people with a variety of not-technically-lying rhetorical tactics, among which Davis lists gerrymandered category boundaries."Firming Up Not-Lying…" (AAL, 26 December 2019), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2019/Dec/firming-up-not-lying-around-its-edge-cases-is-less-broadly-useful-than-one-might-initially-think/. The structural claim that connects these to the categories program is that a category system and a lying policy can be input–output equivalent, so that the ethics of one is the ethics of the other.

Before that claim could be made precise, Davis needed an account of meaning that did not already presuppose it, and he found one in Brian Skyrms's Signals. "Philosophy in the Darkest Timeline: Basics of the Evolution of Meaning" (June 2020) wraps a reinforcement-learning simulation of the Lewis–Skyrms sender–receiver game in a bleak near-future frame story: two agents with no shared language, rewarded when the receiver acts correctly, converge on one of two conventions, and The meaning of a word "lives", not in the word/symbol/signal itself, but in the self-reinforcing network of correlations between the signal, the agents who use it, and the world."Philosophy in the Darkest Timeline: Basics of the Evolution of Meaning" (AAL, 7 June 2020), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2020/Jun/philosophy-in-the-darkest-timeline-basics-of-the-evolution-of-meaning/. The frame story's neighbor, who hands over a paper reading "HOT" when it is cold and then insists it was abstract art, is the gaslighting trope of "The Categories…" made literal. "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!" (August 2020) then notices the problem this creates: if the meaning of a signal is simply how it makes a receiver update its probabilities, which is determined by the conditions under which the signal is sent, then a predator firefly that mimics the mating flash is not lying once prey have priced the mimicry in. Davis's answer, from Skyrms and Barrett, is to define propositional content in the common-interest subgame, so that deception becomes "ontologically parasitic" in sense that holes are: you can't have a lie without some shared map for it to be a lie in. And a sufficiently deceptive map, like a sufficiently holey material, collapses into noise and dust."Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!" (AAL, 22 August 2020), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2020/Aug/maybe-lying-cant-exist/, citing Skyrms, Signals ch. 6; Skyrms and Barrett, "Propositional Content in Signals"; Fallis and Lewis, "Toward a Formal Analysis of Deceptive Signaling"; and Cailin O'Connor, Games in the Philosophy of Biology. These are cited from Davis's bibliography; I did not read them for this chapter. This is a real philosophical move and, so far as I can tell from Davis's own bibliography, a faithful report of the literature. Its cost is that "deception" is now defined relative to a cooperative baseline, so that whether a usage is deceptive depends on which subgame one takes to be the real one. That dependence is exactly what the gender dispute is about.

"Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception"

The sequel to "Boundaries?" took twenty months and came out at ten thousand words in January 2021."Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception" (AAL, 8 January 2021), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2021/Jan/unnatural-categories-are-optimized-for-deception/. Davis's 2025 self-review calls it excellent work that no one (rounding down) appreciated at the time because I sacrificed readability by optimizing for comprehensiveness, LessWrong. The interlocutor now makes the strongest version of Alexander's case: categories are like national borders, a human-made project with free variables, and I don't see why I shouldn't be willing to trade off one unit of categorizational nonawkwardness for X units of morality. Davis's reply has four stages.

First, the metaphor is dismantled by exhibiting the mathematics it hides. A 128-cell joint distribution over blueness, eggness, and vanadium content factorizes into a naive-Bayes model with a three-valued category node; the model makes specific predictions (an eggness score of 7 implies blegg with probability 0.96); and because category "boundaries" are merely a visualization for a probabilistic model that makes predictions about the real world, you can't "redraw the boundaries" associated with a communication signal without messing with the model that generates them, which means messing with your predictions about the real world. Countries aren't Bayesian networks. They just aren't. Second, "compactness" is given a score. Mutual information will not do, because a bit is a bit regardless of shape; what distinguishes "1–4 versus 5–8" from "even versus odd" is that the former gets you close. With a Euclidean metric on the feature space, the natural categories reduce expected squared error from about 13.63 to 1.38, the gerrymandered "blegg*" categories only to 4.12. Third, and this is the part that separates Davis's position from a naive essentialism, the dependence on a metric is acknowledged as a dependence on values, but of a particular kind: a specific kind of lawful dependence between your values and the distribution of data in the world, not an atomic preference for using a particular encoding for its own sake. Poison is an unnatural category to a chemist and a natural one to a creature with a human metabolism; How you should define words depends on what you want, but that's not the same as defining words any way you want. This is Yudkowsky's "Unnatural Categories" absorbed and narrowed: value-ladenness enters through the choice of space and metric, after which the clustering is a matter of fact.

The fourth stage is the one the title announces. Suppose the interlocutor simply insists on an intrinsic preference for the squiggly boundary. Okay, but where would that kind of exotic utility function come from? How would it arise naturally in an intelligent system? Davis's answer is that it would not. A system that sends "BLEGG" on the gerrymandered criterion is optimizing for something, and the something, in his story, is a moonlighting engineer's supplier revenue. Under a scoring rule of squared error minus revenue, the gerrymandered categories win; and such a scoring rule is a compromise between map–territory correspondence and saying whatever pays, a point on a continuum whose far end is pure deception. The receipt argument makes the equivalence explicit: there's no functional difference between saying "I reserve the right to lie p% of the time about whether something belongs to category C", and adopting a new, less-accurate category system that misclassifies p% of instances with respect to the old system. The post is careful to say that Depending on your goals, deception can be rational!, and that in order to help you decide whether deceiving people is the right thing to do, it helps to notice that what you're doing is deceiving people. It then treats self-directed redefinition (deciding to be "funny" by redefining "funny") as wireheading, works through mimicry (Batesian butterflies, robot ducks, squirt guns) to establish that mimics are non-deceptive only in contexts where the unmatched features are irrelevant, and closes on meat substitutes: the mathematics doesn't say which subspace to look for clusters in, so it has nothing to say to someone who thinks taste is what matters; but to someone who wants "meat" extended because animal suffering is important, it says that the hidden Bayesian structure of language and cognition makes no reference to the stakes, and departing from the structure extracts a price that isn't up to us.

The post's last movement drops the pretense of innocence. A "Great Teacher" who lives in an area where claiming that the sky is blue would be political suicide is quoted saying that people now seem to be talking as if color references are necessarily a factual statement about frequencies of light and that using language in a way you dislike, is not lying. The words are Yudkowsky's November 2018 tweets with "pronouns" and "chromosomes" swapped for colors and light. Anyone on LessWrong who knew the thread knew the target.

The technical reception was the most useful Davis received anywhere. John Wentworth's comment reframed the whole apparatus: "clusters in thingspace" is a metaphor. There isn't really a canonical "thingspace" with pre-defined features along the axes; figuring out what features to use is most of the problem from the start. The underlying concept, he said, is conditional independence given the cluster's summary statistics, plus the hypothesis that natural concepts are discrete rather than arbitrarily close together, which is what lets people coordinate on them; and socially constructed categories are moved by changing the territory so that a different concept becomes the Schelling point.johnswentworth, comment of 8 January 2021, LessWrong. Davis worked through the relation between his squared-error clustering and Wentworth's framing in a later comment and in "Feature Selection" (AAL, November 2021), which this chapter does not treat. Davis absorbed this. By "Blood Is Thicker Than Water" he was saying that "There's conditional independence structure in the causal graph" is a meaningfully deeper explanation than "There's a cluster in configuration space", and that configuration space itself is a convenience whose construction the deeper account has to explain.

The dolphin war

In June 2021 Nate Soares, then executive director of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, tweeted that the definitional gymnastics required to believe dolphins are not fish are staggering, and Yudkowsky retweeted it. Davis's "Reply to Nate Soares on Dolphins" is a short post with a long tail. Its argument is the "root of the causal graph" idea that he credits to tailcalled and to Wentworth's conditional independence: genetics are at the root of the causal graph underlying all other features of an organism, such that creatures that are genetically close to each other are more similar in general, which is why Unambiguous discrete features make for better word definitions than high-dimensional statistical regularities, even if most of the everyday inferential utility of using the word comes from fuzzy high-dimensional statistical correlates. A definition is a pointer to a natural abstraction, not a summary of it. And "fish" is polysemous: Wiktionary's sense 2 is the swimmy animals, and nobody is trying to take that away; Soares is the one proposing to abolish sense 1. The post ends with a wink that no one could miss: Could there, perhaps, be some additional context to this conversation that Soares neglected to make explicit? That seems unlikely, however."Reply to Nate Soares on Dolphins" (AAL, 9 June 2021), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2021/Jun/reply-to-nate-soares-on-dolphins/. Soares's tweets are login-walled; they are quoted in the post and restated by Soares in the thread.

Soares answered at length and with good grace. His epistemic status had been "i been shitpostin since april"; the fragment he endorsed was "be wary of those who say they are here to refine your concepts, for sometimes they are here to steal them"; that thread was not a veiled attempt to discuss gender issues; and what had changed since 2008 was that I noticed the subtle background forces that whisper (at least to blue tribe members in their youth) "phylogenetic classification is the one true way to organize life forms", and rejected its claim.So8res (Nate Soares), comments of 10–11 June 2021, LessWrong. Vaniver added the best object-level point in the thread: by divergence times, sharks are farther from guppies than dolphins are, so the 2008 fish list was bad phylogenetics to begin with.Vaniver, comment of 16 June 2021, LessWrong. Davis's own conduct in the thread was poor by his admission: a comment asking Am I the only one creeped out by this? and a later one at −26 karma, followed by an apology; and an unpublished reply, preserved as a hidden page on his blog, in which he tells Soares that If you clowns can't get your own philosophy of language right, why should we trust you to save the world?"Dolphin War!! 🐬 (draft reply to Nate Soares, 16 June 2021)", a page marked Hidden, UUT_content/pages/ancillary/dolphin-war.md, live at http://unremediatedgender.space/ancillary/dolphin-war/. Davis's apology to Soares is at commentId FEihAQ6QMacoaFDhx.

The calmer sequel, "Blood Is Thicker Than Water 🐬" (September 2021), is the best statement of the mature position on natural kinds. Phylogeny matters not as an æsthetic choice, but for structural reasons that can be understood mechanistically: conditioning on the genome d-separates the phenotype nodes, which is the conditional-independence structure that makes a category node worth having, and it is why one can expect to learn new things about dolphins (the number of bones, say) that were never among the dimensions in which the cluster was first noticed. Blood is thicker than water (that is, the similarities induced by shared blood are a thicker subspace of configuration space than the similarities induced by living in the water). Against the charge that phylogeny demands monophyly, Davis replies that there is no principle that it would be hypocritical to betray, that definitions need to be monophyletic clades; paraphyletic groups like fish, reptiles, and monkeys are fine when the excluded branch has acquired salient differences, and the continuity of evolution only asks that good categories be connected in phylogenetic space. Against "fish used to mean the swimmy animals," he produces Aristotle distinguishing cetaceans from fish in the fourth century BCE and the Old High German fledarmūs."Blood Is Thicker Than Water 🐬" (AAL, 27 September 2021), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2021/Sep/blood-is-thicker-than-water/. The linkpost on the gender blog adds that the argument is equivalent to the case that sex chromosomes are a good way to operationalize sex in humans. This is, in effect, Boyd's homeostatic-property-cluster account of species arrived at from the other direction, and the "root of the causal graph" is a better name for the mechanism than "homeostasis." It is Davis's most durable contribution to the subject, and it owes its two key ideas to commenters.

The post's reception shows the pattern that recurs through the program: the top comment, by aphyer, says that cooks and botanists both have legitimate uses of "fruit" and that a demand for a single sense is denying cooks access to a useful regularity that they care about; Davis replies I actually agree with this. Alexander, appearing as Yvain, asks what happens after a billion more years of convergent evolution make whales 99 percent fish-like, and whether there is not a long period in between when you're allowed to categorize them however you want based on which is more useful for you? If the latter, what makes you think we're not in that period right now?, adding I think "crabs" are in this situation right now. Davis: No, of course not: we want categories that give useful information.Comments of 28 September and 8–12 October 2021 on "Blood Is Thicker Than Water", LessWrong. Each time, the critic says "there are several legitimate categories and use picks among them," and each time Davis agrees, and insists that the agreement was already in the post. It usually was. The posts are written as though they are contradicting someone who denies that dolphins are mammals, and nobody does.

One last piece belongs here. "Comment on 'Deception as Cooperation'" (November 2021) reports Manolo Martínez's rate–distortion reframing of signaling games, in which a sender and receiver with partly divergent interests jointly construct a code, and in which calling an individual codeword "deceptive" can seem to miss that the code is a cooperative achievement. Davis's response is a sentence the whole program needed: I am, ultimately, willing to bite this bullet. Agents with different interests will fight about where to draw the boundaries in state-space, and we can still strive to notice the difference between maps optimized to reflect decision-relevant aspects of territory, and maps optimized to control other agents' decisions."Comment on 'Deception as Cooperation'" (AAL, 26 November 2021), https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2021/Nov/comment-on-deception-as-cooperation/, on Manolo Martínez, "Deception as Cooperation", Studies in History and Philosophy of Science Part C (2019). Not read for this chapter. "Strive to notice the difference" is a much weaker claim than "unnatural categories are optimized for deception," and it is the claim the evidence supports.
