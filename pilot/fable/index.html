1. The problem and the thesis

Between February 2018 and September 2025 Zack M. Davis wrote something like forty posts, across two blogs and the LessWrong forum, on a single philosophical question: what makes one way of sorting things into categories better than another? The question is old. Plato's butcher who carves nature at its joints, Locke's nominal essences, Quine's remark that the notion of a kind and the notion of similarity are substantially one notion, Goodman's grue, Richard Boyd's homeostatic property clusters, John Dupré's "promiscuous realism" about biological taxa: there is a literature, and Davis engages almost none of it. His sources are instead a sequence of blog posts that Eliezer Yudkowsky wrote in early 2008 under the title "A Human's Guide to Words," a 2014 essay by Scott Alexander called "The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories," a handful of textbooks in probability and information theory, and Brian Skyrms's work on signaling games. The occasion was narrower still. Davis took up the question because people kept citing Alexander's essay at him when he tried to argue, in the Bay Area rationalist community of 2016 and 2017, that a sexual-orientation-based typology of male-to-female transsexualism was correct, and that the men in that typology's second type were not women in any sense that a scientifically literate person should be obliged to honor.1

A reader who knows only that much would be entitled to expect a motivated hack: an epistemology constructed to deliver a verdict on one contested noun. That expectation is wrong, and showing why it is wrong is the first task of this chapter. The categorization posts are the most sustained, most technically serious, and most successful philosophical work in the corpus. They identify a real equivocation in Alexander's essay, one that Alexander himself eventually conceded in an editorial note; they extend Yudkowsky's informal account of categories with genuine content (the subspace resolution of the dolphin problem, the mutual-information analysis of title inflation, the squared-error formalization of "compactness," the observation that a variable at the root of a causal graph buys conditional independence across every downstream feature); and they were produced under conditions, social and psychological, that would have excused far worse work. Several of the people Davis was arguing with said as much. Said Achmiz called the central post an excellent post with that rare quality, like much of the Sequences, of the ideas it describes being utterly obvious—in retrospect; the LessWrong administrator Oliver Habryka said of the 2022 pronoun post that basically everything in this post strikes me as "obviously true".2

The second task is to say where the work fails, and here the chapter's thesis has two parts. First, Davis's positive theory, that the cognitive function of categories is to support probabilistic inference and that any other reason for drawing a boundary reduces to coordination, self-deception, or deception, is overextended. It handles natural kinds well and conventional kinds badly. Money, marriage, crime, adulthood, and (on the view of most of the philosophers who have written about it) gender are categories whose boundaries partly constitute the facts they describe, and a theory that classifies every non-predictive reason for a boundary as "wireheading" or "war" has stipulated its conclusion rather than earned it. Davis half-knows this: the "Schelling categories" posts of 2019 are an admission that coordination is a legitimate second-order criterion, and in a 2020 comment he lists the self-fulfilling prophecy as a complication whose theory he does not yet understand. But the admission is never integrated into the polemic. Second, and more consequentially, the application of the theory to the word "woman" trades on an unresolved choice between two categories that Davis's own framework licenses as natural: perceived sex, the category that everyday cognition actually uses and that successfully transitioned people can enter, and developmental sex, the category rooted in gametes and chromosomes that no current intervention changes. Davis's 2018 essay grants the first; his 2021–2024 writing insists on the second; the framework says both are clusters in different subspaces, and that which one gets the short word is a coordination problem whose solution depends on what speakers want to talk about. By his own lights, then, the dispute he fought as a battle between "Truth" and "Feelings" was a conflict over which Schelling point a speech community should adopt, with real costs on both sides. The framework is better than the war fought in its name.

There is an irony in how the war ended, and the chapter will come to it. The two principals, Alexander and Yudkowsky, each eventually conceded the narrow philosophical point in public: Alexander by appending a note to his 2014 essay in December 2019 saying that one must deal with the consequences of your definitions, which can include confusion, muddling communication, and leaving openings for deceptive rhetorical strategies, and Yudkowsky in a September 2020 Facebook post that, as Davis quotes it, said you are being the bad guy if you try to shut down that conversation by saying that "I can define the word 'woman' any way I want".3 Neither changed his policy on how to talk about trans people. That outcome is exactly what Davis's own theory predicts if the underlying disagreement was about coordination and values rather than about the laws of probability. He experienced it as a defeat.

A note on method. Davis's posts on this subject are unusually interlinked, and later ones restate earlier ones; the memoir posts of 2023–2024 then restate the whole sequence as a narrative. I read them in order of composition and treat the memoirs as a second source rather than as the authoritative one, because the memoirs are written to prosecute a case. Where a claim about what someone else said rests only on the memoirs (Yudkowsky's tweets, Facebook posts, and private correspondence), I say so.

2. The inheritance: Yudkowsky's "Human's Guide to Words" and Alexander's essay

Davis's position cannot be understood without the two texts it is built on and against, and both deserve to be read on their own terms rather than through his summaries of them.

Yudkowsky, 2008

"A Human's Guide to Words" is a run of about thirty posts that Yudkowsky published on Overcoming Bias in February and March of 2008 and later collected under that title. Its governing move is a reduction. A word is not a label with a meaning attached; it is a communication signal whose function is to let one mind cause another mind to reconstruct a concept, and a concept is a piece of inferential machinery. "Disguised Queries" tells the parable of a factory worker sorting blue egg-shaped "bleggs" from red cubical "rubes" and discovering that the question "Is this object a blegg?" has no content beyond the specific predictions it stands in for: which bin it goes in, whether it glows in the dark, whether it contains vanadium. The question "Is this object a blegg?" may stand in for different queries on different occasions. If it weren't standing in for some query, you'd have no reason to care.4 "How An Algorithm Feels From Inside" explains why the question nevertheless feels as though it has leftover content: the brain's categorizer is a central node with an activation of its own, so that even after you know every characteristic of the object, you still find yourself wondering: "But is it a blegg, or not?"5 "The Cluster Structure of Thingspace" supplies the geometry: objects are points in a very high-dimensional configuration space, categories are clusters, and because the empirical clustered structure of the real universe is not so crystalline, a natural category may have no set of necessary and sufficient properties—no set of characteristics that all group members have, and no non-members have.6

So far this is anti-essentialism of a familiar kind, and a reader who stopped here could take the sequence as licensing conventionalism: if categories are human inferential conveniences with fuzzy edges, then surely one draws them as one likes. The sequence's second strand exists to block that reading. "Where to Draw the Boundary?" (the singular title matters later) says that the challenge worthy of a rationalist is figuring out which things are similar to each other—which things are clustered together—and sometimes, which things have a common cause, calls a boundary that loops around and swerves through the clusters a wrong word, and delivers the line that Davis would later quote against its author more than once: Or you could stop playing nitwit games and admit that dolphins don't belong on the fish list.7 "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace" gives the strand its only piece of mathematics: a word for a conjunction of properties earns a shorter code than the conjunction itself only when the properties co-occur more often than their marginal probabilities predict, so that the way to carve reality at its joints, is to draw your boundaries around concentrations of unusually high probability density in Thingspace, and One may even consider the act of defining a word as a promise to this effect.8 "Conditional Independence, and Naive Bayes" explains why a category node is worth having at all: when observing a central variable screens the surrounding features off from one another, the inferential mess becomes tractable. "Categorizing Has Consequences" adds the psychological point that drawing a boundary in thingspace is not a neutral act, because a human brain, once given a label, begins harvesting similarities whether or not they exist.9 The summary post, "37 Ways That Words Can Be Wrong," opens by saying that saying "There's no way my choice of X can be 'wrong'" is nearly always an error in practice, whatever the theory and closes on item 37: You think that definitions can't be "wrong", or that "I can define a word any way I like!" This kind of attitude teaches you to indignantly defend your past actions, instead of paying attention to their consequences, or fessing up to your mistakes.10

Two further posts from the same year complicate the picture in ways Davis would have to deal with. In "Unnatural Categories" Yudkowsky coins that phrase for a category whose boundary you draw in a way that sensitively depends on the exact values built into your utility function, gives "poison" and "person" and "true happiness" as examples, and adds: I don't mean to imply that unnatural categories are worthless or relative or whatever. That's what moral arguments are for—for drawing and redrawing the boundaries.11 That is a concession that some category boundaries are legitimately fixed by what we care about and not by the statistics of the territory; Davis's 2021 post with the near-identical title would have to narrow it. And in "Changing Emotions" (January 2009) Yudkowsky took sex change as the archetype of a transformation that is easy to name and enormously hard to do: it's not like people have a binary attribute reading "M" or "F" that can be flipped as a primitive action; remapping body and pleasure circuitry would produce a normal, masculine man wearing a female body like a suit of clothing; and If I fell asleep and woke up as a true woman—not in body, but in brain—I don't think I'd call her "me".12 Davis would read this post as a statement that sex is a high-dimensional cluster that cannot be entered by fiat, and he is not wrong to; but the post's own subject is the difficulty of mind-editing, and it does not say anything about what the word "woman" should mean.

Where does this sit in the philosophical literature that Yudkowsky does not cite? Closest to Quine's "Natural Kinds" (1969), which ties kinds to similarity and similarity to what confirms an induction, and to the cluster-kind realism that grew out of it. The Stanford Encyclopedia's summary of the latter could serve as a gloss on "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace": kinds are families of such properties that are contingently clustered in nature, and A natural kind is any such family of co-occurring properties that may be employed in inductive inferences or for the purposes of scientific explanation.13 Boyd's version adds that the clustering is sustained by a homeostatic mechanism, which will matter when Davis arrives, in 2021, at the idea that a variable at the root of a causal graph is what makes a cluster robust. What Yudkowsky adds to that tradition is not metaphysics but engineering: the claim that the same criterion governs a Bayesian classifier and a human vocabulary, and the pedagogical insistence that it be stated in the objectivist register ("words can be wrong") rather than the relativist one. Davis later reports Alexander wondering, on a train in 2019, why the moral had not been phrased as "you can define a word any way you want, but then you have to deal with the consequences," and records his own answer: If you write your summary slogan in relativist language, people predictably take that as license to believe whatever they want without having to defend it.14 The whole of the subsequent dispute can be read as a quarrel over that pedagogical choice.

Alexander, 2014

"The Categories Were Made for Man, Not Man for the Categories" (21 November 2014) is a six-part essay, and its argument has a distinct shape in each part that the later controversy tended to blur.15 Part I is about King Solomon and whales. The Hebrew dag picks out finned sea creatures; a modern speaker's "fish" picks out a phylogenetic group; the two words are different words, and there is no fact of the matter on whether a whale is a fish. The argument is entirely semantic. Part II takes over Yudkowsky's own account of Pluto and the planets (it cites "How An Algorithm Feels From Inside") and concludes that when an object satisfies half the criteria for one category and half for another, it's a judgment call which you have to decide by fiat. Part III shifts to the metaphor of national borders: borders can be stupid, but in no case can an agreed-upon set of borders or a category boundary be factually incorrect. Part IV applies this to gender. The anti-transgender argument Alexander says he takes seriously is that rationalists must call a man who thinks he is a woman a man and take the consequences; his reply is An alternative categorization system is not an error, and borders are not objectively true or false. He then describes the transgender movement's project as a switch from using chromosomes as a tiebreaker to using self-identification as a tiebreaker, and ends the part with the sentence everything turned on:

If I'm willing to accept an unexpected chunk of Turkey deep inside Syrian territory to honor some random dead guy – and I better, or else a platoon of Turkish special forces will want to have a word with me – then I ought to accept an unexpected man or two deep inside the conceptual boundaries of what would normally be considered female if it'll save someone's life. There's no rule of rationality saying that I shouldn't, and there are plenty of rules of human decency saying that I should.

Part V is the hair-dryer story: a patient whose obsessive fear of having left her hair dryer on was cured by carrying the hair dryer in her car, and the moral that the primary thing in psychiatry is to help the patient, whatever the means. Part VI answers the "trans-Napoleon" objection, partly by gesturing at the various heavily researched apparent biological correlates of transgender, and ends with Emperor Norton of San Francisco, humored by his fellow citizens, as one of the most heartwarming episodes in the history of one of my favorite places in the world.

The essay is doing two things that it does not distinguish. The first is a thesis about which dimensions matter: whether to sort sea creatures by habitat or ancestry depends on whether you run a fishing fleet or a genetics lab, and no fact decides it. That thesis is true, is Yudkowsky's, and is the content of Parts I through III. The second is a thesis about where to draw the line once the dimensions are fixed: that one may accept "an unexpected man or two" inside the boundary of "female" because doing so will save a life. That thesis is not supported by the first, because the reason offered for it is not a claim about similarity along any dimension at all. The Turkish exclave is a weird border, but it is a border on the same map; the question is whether the analogy carries over to a category whose job is inference. Part II, which Alexander took from Yudkowsky, already says what categories are for: to sweep all common correlations into one big category in the middle. A boundary that does not track the correlations fails at that job however decent the motive. Ozy Brennan, no ally of Davis on the object level, saw the equivocation at once in 2018: Taylor points out that there are totally shenanigans going on here. There are two different senses of the word "useful."16 So, eventually, did Alexander. The note he appended in December 2019 reads in part: While nothing is stopping you from defining a word any way you want, you should definitely worry about it. He added that plausible definitions will be limited to a few possibilities suggested by the territory, with worsening penalties as you deviate from these further. That is a long way from "not an error," and the distance was travelled under pressure from Davis.

Two things should be said in Alexander's defense before Davis's reply is examined. The first is that Part VI does gesture at an empirical argument, the biological correlates, which is the argument Davis himself says a non-question-begging case would have to make. The second is that Alexander's conclusion is a claim about a policy, what to call people, and the essay's confusion lies in dressing a policy argument as a point about the metaphysics of categories. Strip the dressing and the position is: there is no uniquely correct boundary; this boundary, which costs little, helps someone a lot; so adopt it. That is not a philosophical error. It is a utilitarian bid in a coordination problem. Whether it is a good bid depends on how little it costs, and the cost is exactly what Davis set out to compute.

3. The first reply (2018)

Davis's prescriptivist instincts predate the controversy. In August 2016, in a short dialogue called "Prescription," one speaker complains that other people are really bizarrely cavalier about using words to mean whatever they feel like at the moment and that I wasn't expecting people to redefine a top-20 noun out from under my feet.17 Four months later a one-paragraph aside regrets having missed the chance to be for civil unions but against redefining existing words that already mean something else.18 And a March 2017 note on the "Bayes-structural justification for free speech norms" already contains the key figure of the later work: a coalition that bans hate speech probably won't notice when members of the coalition begin to slowly gerrymander the hate speech category boundary in the service of their own values.19 "Gerrymandered" would become his standard adjective for a boundary drawn for its effects.

"Blegg Mode"

The first published piece of the program is "Blegg Mode" (1 February 2018), eight hundred words of what Davis's tag calls "deniably allegorical" fan fiction for Yudkowsky's factory. Hard, blue, furry objects start arriving; on inspection they are rubes with sanded corners and glued-on fur. The narrator, invoking Alexander, insists on calling them "adapted bleggs," but notices that they occupy a third category in his mind anyway, and extracts a criterion: a population earns a category when it has cheap-to-detect features that correlate with more-expensive-to-detect features that are decision-relevant with respect to the agent's goals; such a category is a distinction that makes a difference, something your brain wants to have an efficient representation so that you can attend to it.20 The criterion is sound as far as it goes, and its weakness was identified on the day the piece reached LessWrong in March 2019: the commenter gjm called it a principle with the look, to me, of a principle that's constructed in order to reach a particular conclusion, and Jessica Taylor pointed out that a Bayesian classifier isn't going to assign special importance to observed features, as it treats the observations as causally downstream of the ontological reality rather than causally upstream of it, to which Davis replied that he might have been on the wrong track there.21 Taylor's point is the better one. Observability is a constraint on which category you can use, not on which categories exist; "Blegg Mode" runs the two together, and the later work separates them under the heading of Schelling categories.

"The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions"

The main reply, "The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions" (23 February 2018, about 6,500 words), is the essay Davis describes as the capstone of a year's sabbatical, and it is the hinge of the whole program.22 It opens by granting Alexander everything in Parts I–III and then turning the grant into an accusation: all of it is entirely correct—and thus, an excellent motte for the less honest sort of Slate Star Codex reader to appeal to when they want to obfuscate and disrupt discussions about empirical reality by insisting on gerrymandered redefinitions of everyday concepts. The central paragraph against "not an error" is worth quoting because it states the position Davis would spend six years elaborating:

But this is just giving up way too easily. The map is not the territory, and many very different kinds of maps can correspond to the territory in different ways—we have geographical maps, political maps, road maps, globes, &c.—but that doesn't mean no map is in error. Rationalists can't insist on using the one true categorization system, because it turns out that—in all philosophical strictness—no such thing exists. But that doesn't release us from our sacred duty to describe what's actually true. It just leaves us faced with the slightly more complicated task of describing the costs and benefits of different categorization systems with respect to different criteria.

Whales are mammals and whales are dagim; both categories do cognitive work, because each predicts unobserved features; and in configuration space the whale sits in two clusters at once, in two different subspaces. The essay's most valuable sentence is a methodological one. Where political factions fight over a common word, rationalists may not be able to say that one side is simply right and the other is simply wrong, but we can at least strive for objectivity in describing the conflict. And: That there exists an element of subjectivity in what you choose to pay attention to, doesn't negate the fact that there is a structured empirical reality to be described—and not all descriptions of it are equally compact. On Lincoln's riddle: you can call a tail a leg, but you can't stop people from noticing that out of a dog's five legs, one of them is different from the others.

Then comes the diagnosis of Alexander's Part IV. The claim that one may redraw the boundary to save a life is true in a tautological sense: if you deliberately gerrymander your category boundaries in order to get the answer you want, you can get the answer you want. The hair-dryer analogy fails because carrying a hair dryer affects one person, while collectively agreeing that gender should be defined on the basis of self-identity affects everyone who wants to use language to categorize humans by sex: The words man and woman are top-20 nouns! People need those nouns to describe their experiences! The cost of the redefinition may be small per speaker but needs to be aggregated across everyone subject to it, like so many dust specks in their eyes. Note what this argument concedes: that the question is one of costs and benefits, aggregated across parties. That is a utilitarian frame, not a "words can be wrong" frame. The essay never resolves which it is making, and the ambivalence persists through the whole program.

What the essay does next is more interesting than the diagnosis, and it is the part the later polemic tended to forget. Davis says that the right kind of argument for calling trans women women would be an empirical one: that successfully transitioned people are categorized as their target sex by the pre-verbal perceptual machinery everyone actually uses, so that they fit into existing concepts of gender, and It works because and to the extent that transitioning actually works. A natal female who presents as a man and is modeled as a man might be said to be a man in the sense of social gender (but not in the sense of "biologically male adult human"), because that's the mental category that people are actually using for him. This is a concession of real size. It says that there are at least two legitimate senses of the gender words, perceived and developmental, that the perceived sense is a natural category in Davis's own terms (it is what the classifier in people's heads computes), and that trans people can belong to it. The rest of the essay then argues that most trans women, conditional on the two-type taxonomy and the non-intersex etiology of late-onset dysphoria, do not pass well and are drawn from the male distribution along most psychological dimensions, so that most trans women aren't just not part of the female cluster in configuration space; they're specifically part of male cluster along most dimensions, which people already have a concept for. The scatterplot that accompanies this (fictitious data, three dimensions, a purple cluster that matches the blue one on two axes and the pink one on the third) is honest about its dependence: People who care more about predicting x₁ and x₂ have reason to prefer categories and corresponding language that group by natal sex; people who care more about predicting x₃ have reason to prefer categories and language that group by gender identity. The figure is a picture of two natural categories and a value-dependent choice between them. Davis drew it himself, in 2018, and the rest of this chapter will keep returning to it.23

The essay's last third leaves philosophy for policy and testimony: two anonymous women on the changing character of "all women" events in a community where, by the 2018 Slate Star Codex survey, trans women were a large share of the women; the author's refusal to say what the right norms are (People should get what they want. We should have social norms that help people get what they want. I don't know what the optimal social norms about transitioning would be.); a photograph of pronoun stickers at an American Philosophical Association meeting; and the lines that gave the essay its reputation: If you need a sticker to get people to gender you correctly, your transition has failed. All you can do is incentivize them to lie. This is not rationality. This isn't even kindness. We're smarter than this. The coda rewrites Alexander's Emperor Norton as a man who one day suspects he is not emperor and asks his advisor for the truth; the advisor, reaching for the categories-are-made-for-man line, finds that you don't believe your own bullshit, tells him, and offers to help him plan what to do now. Davis discloses here that he had himself been delusional the previous year and had been helped by friends who didn't automatically dismiss everything I said as wrong, but who also told me when I wasn't making sense. The parable is the essay's moral core and also its weakest inference: it assumes that the trans person's self-understanding stands to reality as Norton's did, which is the thing in dispute.

"Reply to The Unit of Caring on Adult Human Females"

Two months later Davis answered Kelsey Piper, then writing on Tumblr as The Unit of Caring, who had said she could find no one willing to say what good properties the definition "adult human female" has.24 The post is shorter and better organized than its predecessor, and its first point is the best single argument in the 2018 material: the definition has non-circularity. One can list membership tests that do not mention the category; a self-identification criterion cannot, because without a base case, the infinite recursion of (people who identify as (people who identify as (people who identify as ...))) never terminates. Davis then does something the memoirs do not advertise: he constructs a non-circular trans-inclusive sense of the word, adult human females, and also adult human males who have undergone interventions to resemble adult human females sufficiently closely so that they can be taken as such socially, and calls it a mere broadening of the "adult human female" definition that tacks on extra complexity. His complaint is not that the broad sense exists but that the narrow one is being made unsayable: the point is that it should be socially acceptable to describe reality using words. Three dialogues dramatize what is lost when a speaker cannot say That's not what I meant by woman in this context and you know it: a girls' track meet, a meetup whose women were all trans, the Dhejne cohort study's violent-crime rates. The epigraph is a maxim from Alicorn, Thou shalt not strike terms from others' expressive vocabulary without suitable replacement. On Piper's thought experiment of perfect transition technology, Davis gives a conditional answer and ends it with a sentence that should be set beside the APA stickers passage: if psychological sex differences exist and most trans women transition for reasons other than having female minds, then under perfect body-changing technology Honestly, I lean towards Yes, but I can at least see the argument of someone who preferred not to use the word that way.

Put the two 2018 essays together and the position is this. Words have narrower and broader senses, which can be distinguished from context. "Woman" in its sex sense picks out a robust cluster; "woman" in a perceived-sex sense picks out another; a trans-inclusive sense can be defined non-circularly as an extension of the first by the second. None of these is false. What is objectionable is (a) the argument that one may extend a category on grounds of kindness alone, because that is not a reason that bears on the category's function; and (b) the social norm that forbids the narrow sense. Both objections are correct. But notice how much of Alexander's practical conclusion survives them. A contextualist about "woman" who insists only that the sex sense remain available has not shown that "trans women are women" is false; he has shown that it is true in one sense, false in another, and that a speech community choosing which sense to make default is choosing, in Davis's word, a convention. Ozy Brennan's reply, which I turn to in section 5, pressed exactly here.

4. The Less Wrong program (2019–2021)

Between April 2019 and November 2021 Davis moved the argument from his pseudonymous gender blog to LessWrong, the forum built around Yudkowsky's Sequences, and recast it without its gender content. He later described the first of these posts as a "stealth reply" to Alexander and to a November 2018 Twitter thread of Yudkowsky's, quoting/paraphrasing without attribution in order to reply to the philosophical substance while eliding the political context, and in December 2020 he put up a "containment thread" acknowledging that the reason I've been doing so much philosophy-of-language blogging lately is because I was frustrated because I thought a lot of people were motivatedly getting the philosophy of language wrong for political convenience, specifically around transgender issues.25 The stealth was not very stealthy, and I return to its cost in section 7. Here I take the posts as the philosophy they claim to be, because they are also that.

"Where to Draw the Boundaries?"

The plural in the title answers Yudkowsky's singular. "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" (13 April 2019) is a dialogue with "the one," an interlocutor whose lines are unattributed quotations from Alexander and Yudkowsky, and its first move is the reconciliation of their two dolphin verdicts. Yudkowsky in 2008 had said dolphins don't belong on the fish list; Alexander in 2014 had said that was a judgment call. Davis's answer: both are right, in different subspaces.26

When rationalists say that definitions can be wrong, we don't mean that there's a unique category boundary that is the True floating essence of a word, and that all other possible boundaries are wrong. We mean that in order for a proposed category boundary to not be wrong, it needs to capture some statistical structure in reality, even if reality is surprisingly detailed and there can be more than one such structure.

Thingspace is not well defined, he concedes, but one can form a space from whatever properties one cares about, and Different choices of properties correspond to different cross sections of the grander Thingspace. Dolphins cluster with fish in the subspace of swimming-related dimensions and with mammals in another; the sailors' word was not wrong within its domain, and a modern biologist's word is better because the biologist's model has more dimensions in it. Then the interlocutor makes Alexander's move in its purest form: Everything we identify as a joint is only a joint because we care about it. Davis's reply is the thesis of the whole program in one sentence: No. Everything we identify as a joint is a joint not "because we care about it", but because it helps us think about the things we care about. Values choose the subspace; the territory supplies the clusters within it. The distinction he wants is between having a utility function defined over a statistical model's performance against specific real-world data (even if another mind with different values would be interested in different data), and having a utility function defined over features of the model itself.

Two numerical examples carry the weight. In the first, "Foo" names a cluster centered at [1, 2, 3] in three dimensions and "Quux" one at [2, 4, 6]; someone who redefines Foo to cover both and then, on seeing a first coordinate of 2, still predicts 4 and 6 for the others is not using the new concept but only mouthing it, because a genuine Foo-with-the-new-definition would predict 3 and 4.5. The point is that You can't change the categories your mind actually uses and still perform as well on prediction tasks—although you can change your verbally reported categories. In the second, a worker is "promoted" to Vice President of Sorting with no change in pay, tasks, or authority. Davis models a four-property Jobspace, computes that the mutual information between title and pay falls from about 0.918 bits to about 0.2516 bits once half the low-pay jobs get high titles, and concludes that all that's actually been accomplished is making language less useful. The post ends by proposing a thirty-eighth way that words can be wrong, 38. Your definition draws a boundary around a cluster in an inappropriately 'thin' subspace of Thingspace that excludes relevant variables, resulting in fallacies of compression., and with a pastiche of Yudkowsky's Musashi: Do not ask whether there's a rule of rationality saying that you shouldn't call dolphins fish. Ask whether dolphins are fish. One parenthesis deserves notice because the 2021 sequel grows out of it: if you're actually trying to optimize other people's models of the world, rather than the world itself—you could just lie, rather than playing clever category-gerrymandering mind games. It would be a lot simpler!

The post is good, and the reception on LessWrong was substantive on both sides. Said Achmiz's praise has been quoted. David Hornbein, in the 2019 Review, called it a crushingly well-argued piece of philosophy of language and its relation to reasoning and observed, double-edgedly, that "write a nigh-irrefutable argument about philosophy of language, in order to strike at the heart of the substantive disagreement which provoked the social conflict" is an effective social move in this community.27 Wei Dai asked for a concrete case and, given Alexander's "eargreyish" (mice and elephants grouped as grey animals with large ears), pressed that a thin subspace is sometimes exactly the right one, as when a biologist hypothesizes that grey skin and big ears are what let prey escape a new predator; Davis conceded that and added the mimicry examples that reappear in 2021.28 The deepest objection came later and I hold it for section 5: the moderator Raemon's complaint that the Musashi peroration is motte-and-bailey-ing between "These are the rules of thinking rationally, for forming accurate beliefs, and for communicating about that in particular contexts" and "these are the rules of communicating, in whatever circumstances you might find yourself."29

High dimensions and simple tests

"The Univariate Fallacy" (June 2019) is a short demonstration, with Python, that two distributions can overlap on every single coordinate and still be separable with supreme, godlike confidence when forty coordinates are taken together.30 The mathematics is elementary (independent coordinates, likelihood ratios multiplied), and the point it makes is correct and important: "the distributions overlap on this variable" does not entail "the groups are not distinguishable." The post says nothing about sex. It did not need to; it was the mathematical core of the 2018 scatterplot, and in "Sexual Dimorphism in Yudkowsky's Sequences" (2021) Davis applies it explicitly, noting that most of the probability mass of a high-dimensional distribution sits in a shell away from the mode, so that The typical point in a high-dimensional distribution is not typical along each dimension individually.31 I assess the empirical use of this in section 5.

"Schelling Categories, and Simple Membership Tests" (August 2019) is the post in which the program first qualifies itself, and it is the most philosophically careful thing in the sequence.32 Take the forty-dimensional example again, but suppose two agents must make the same decision about each point while each observes only one coordinate. Add a forty-first binary coordinate that is more informative than any single one of the others. Then the agents may rationally define their shared category by that coordinate alone, even if x₄₁ itself has no effect on the quality of your decisions, because it is the best single observation and, being salient, a Schelling point. The conclusion: the right categories to use given constraints on communication and observation, might be different from the category boundaries you would draw from a "God's eye view", in part because consideration of which categories are easy for different agents to coordinate on is relevant, not just raw information-theoretic expressive power. The applications are the age of majority (replacing a complicated, illegible category with a simple membership test (an age cutoff that everyone knows how to compute)), commodity grading (delegation to an authority), and tribal rallying flags, including the option of delegating the classification problem to the person themselves as the authority whose discernment is to be trusted. The post warns that simple tests invite regressional Goodhart and delegation invites adversarial Goodhart, the gaming of the test, and ends: coordination is costly.

This is the apparatus that Davis then turned against his own side. "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point" (October 2019), on the gender blog, announces an apparently novel argument in favor of the self-identity criterion: if social reality requires that everyone agree on one answer to what sex a person is, and passing is too blurry to serve, then the natural Schelling point is, "Just ask the person what gender they are, and that's what they are." Not because people know themselves best, but because people are still the focal point for reasonable mutual expectations about knowledge about themselves. He then observes that the two extreme conventions are both stable Schelling points, chromosomes-only (It would be cruel to trans people and people with complete androgen insensitivity syndrome, but it would be a Schelling point.) and self-report-only, while any of a number of "compromise" systems, while potentially performing better on edge cases, suffer from ambiguity and are on that account less game-theoretically stable.33 I want to flag how much this concedes, because the memoirs never do. The post locates the dispute over "woman" inside the theory of coordination, where Davis's own framework says the answer is value-dependent and where the self-identity convention has a structural advantage. It does not say the convention is false. It says it is a Schelling point with costs. That is the correct description of the conflict, and it is Alexander's description with the metaphysical dressing removed.

Lying, meaning, and deception

The autumn 1919 posts on lying belong to this chapter because Davis used them to test the categorization theory on a word other than "woman." "Maybe Lying Doesn't Exist" (October 2019) answers Alexander's "Against Lie Inflation," which had argued that stretching "lie" to cover motivated reasoning would make the word useless and everyone angrier.34 Davis's objection is that this is an appeal to consequences, and that appeal-to-consequences is an invalid form of reasoning for optimal-categorization questions for essentially the same reason as it is for questions of simple fact. If saying that Elijah is lying will make Elijah angry, that's not an argument for changing the definition of lying; that's an argument that I have an incentive to lie about whether I think Elijah is lying! Then a second, more speculative point: the folk intension of "lie" (conscious intent to deceive) may fail to match the extension we most need the word for, people motivatedly saying convenient things without bothering to check whether they're true. The post is frank that Davis himself frowns on the expanded sense; his quarrel is with the reasons for frowning.

Alexander replied in the comments two months later, and the exchange is the clearest statement of the standing disagreement between them. "Appeal to consequences" is only a fallacy in reasoning about factual states of the world. In most cases, appealing to consequences is the right action. Language, he wrote, draws on the structure of reality, but has many free variables, so that the structure of reality doesn't constrain it completely, and for the free variables we have nothing to make those decisions on except consequences.35 Davis's first reply was that he could not muster the willpower to engage if you're going to motivatedly play dumb like this; Alexander answered I absolutely deny I am "motivatedly playing dumb"; a conversation in person followed, and Alexander's editorial note on the 2014 essay was one result.36 The exchange matters because Alexander's position is not stupid. If a word's extension is underdetermined by the territory, something other than the territory has to fix it. Davis's eventual answer, in "Unnatural Categories," is that the something must still be a prediction-shaped criterion, a metric on the variables you care about, and that any other criterion is suspect. Whether that answer works is the question of section 5.

Two companion posts push the theory of deception toward mechanism. "Algorithms of Deception!" (October 2019) gives four Python "Reporters" that pass observations to an "Audience": one faithful, one that always reports a 4, one that selectively omits, one that collapses 1 and 4 into "oneorfour," and shows the last two distorting the audience's posterior while each Reporter, personified, insists it never said anything false.37 "Firming Up Not-Lying Around Its Edge-Cases Is Less Broadly Useful Than One Might Initially Think" (December 2019) argues against Yudkowsky's "meta-honesty" that a rule of never uttering a literally false sentence is weak protection, since a motivated speaker will end up accidentally training yourself to get really good at misleading people with a variety of not-technically-lying rhetorical tactics, among which Davis lists gerrymandered category boundaries.38 The structural claim that connects these to the categories program is that a category system and a lying policy can be input–output equivalent, so that the ethics of one is the ethics of the other.

Before that claim could be made precise, Davis needed an account of meaning that did not already presuppose it, and he found one in Brian Skyrms's Signals. "Philosophy in the Darkest Timeline: Basics of the Evolution of Meaning" (June 2020) wraps a reinforcement-learning simulation of the Lewis–Skyrms sender–receiver game in a bleak near-future frame story: two agents with no shared language, rewarded when the receiver acts correctly, converge on one of two conventions, and The meaning of a word "lives", not in the word/symbol/signal itself, but in the self-reinforcing network of correlations between the signal, the agents who use it, and the world.39 "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!" (August 2020) then notices the problem this creates: if the meaning of a signal is simply how it makes a receiver update its probabilities, which is determined by the conditions under which the signal is sent, then a predator firefly that mimics the mating flash is not lying once prey have priced the mimicry in. Davis's answer, from Skyrms and Barrett, is to define propositional content in the common-interest subgame, so that deception becomes "ontologically parasitic" in sense that holes are: you can't have a lie without some shared map for it to be a lie in. And a sufficiently deceptive map, like a sufficiently holey material, collapses into noise and dust.40 This is a real philosophical move and, so far as I can tell from Davis's own bibliography, a faithful report of the literature. Its cost is that "deception" is now defined relative to a cooperative baseline, so that whether a usage is deceptive depends on which subgame one takes to be the real one. That dependence is exactly what the gender dispute is about.

"Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception"

The sequel to "Boundaries?" took twenty months and came out at ten thousand words in January 2021.41 The interlocutor now makes the strongest version of Alexander's case: categories are like national borders, a human-made project with free variables, and I don't see why I shouldn't be willing to trade off one unit of categorizational nonawkwardness for X units of morality. Davis's reply has four stages.

First, the metaphor is dismantled by exhibiting the mathematics it hides. A 128-cell joint distribution over blueness, eggness, and vanadium content factorizes into a naive-Bayes model with a three-valued category node; the model makes specific predictions (an eggness score of 7 implies blegg with probability 0.96); and because category "boundaries" are merely a visualization for a probabilistic model that makes predictions about the real world, you can't "redraw the boundaries" associated with a communication signal without messing with the model that generates them, which means messing with your predictions about the real world. Countries aren't Bayesian networks. They just aren't. Second, "compactness" is given a score. Mutual information will not do, because a bit is a bit regardless of shape; what distinguishes "1–4 versus 5–8" from "even versus odd" is that the former gets you close. With a Euclidean metric on the feature space, the natural categories reduce expected squared error from about 13.63 to 1.38, the gerrymandered "blegg*" categories only to 4.12. Third, and this is the part that separates Davis's position from a naive essentialism, the dependence on a metric is acknowledged as a dependence on values, but of a particular kind: a specific kind of lawful dependence between your values and the distribution of data in the world, not an atomic preference for using a particular encoding for its own sake. Poison is an unnatural category to a chemist and a natural one to a creature with a human metabolism; How you should define words depends on what you want, but that's not the same as defining words any way you want. This is Yudkowsky's "Unnatural Categories" absorbed and narrowed: value-ladenness enters through the choice of space and metric, after which the clustering is a matter of fact.

The fourth stage is the one the title announces. Suppose the interlocutor simply insists on an intrinsic preference for the squiggly boundary. Okay, but where would that kind of exotic utility function come from? How would it arise naturally in an intelligent system? Davis's answer is that it would not. A system that sends "BLEGG" on the gerrymandered criterion is optimizing for something, and the something, in his story, is a moonlighting engineer's supplier revenue. Under a scoring rule of squared error minus revenue, the gerrymandered categories win; and such a scoring rule is a compromise between map–territory correspondence and saying whatever pays, a point on a continuum whose far end is pure deception. The receipt argument makes the equivalence explicit: there's no functional difference between saying "I reserve the right to lie p% of the time about whether something belongs to category C", and adopting a new, less-accurate category system that misclassifies p% of instances with respect to the old system. The post is careful to say that Depending on your goals, deception can be rational!, and that in order to help you decide whether deceiving people is the right thing to do, it helps to notice that what you're doing is deceiving people. It then treats self-directed redefinition (deciding to be "funny" by redefining "funny") as wireheading, works through mimicry (Batesian butterflies, robot ducks, squirt guns) to establish that mimics are non-deceptive only in contexts where the unmatched features are irrelevant, and closes on meat substitutes: the mathematics doesn't say which subspace to look for clusters in, so it has nothing to say to someone who thinks taste is what matters; but to someone who wants "meat" extended because animal suffering is important, it says that the hidden Bayesian structure of language and cognition makes no reference to the stakes, and departing from the structure extracts a price that isn't up to us.

The post's last movement drops the pretense of innocence. A "Great Teacher" who lives in an area where claiming that the sky is blue would be political suicide is quoted saying that people now seem to be talking as if color references are necessarily a factual statement about frequencies of light and that using language in a way you dislike, is not lying. The words are Yudkowsky's November 2018 tweets with "pronouns" and "chromosomes" swapped for colors and light. Anyone on LessWrong who knew the thread knew the target.

The technical reception was the most useful Davis received anywhere. John Wentworth's comment reframed the whole apparatus: "clusters in thingspace" is a metaphor. There isn't really a canonical "thingspace" with pre-defined features along the axes; figuring out what features to use is most of the problem from the start. The underlying concept, he said, is conditional independence given the cluster's summary statistics, plus the hypothesis that natural concepts are discrete rather than arbitrarily close together, which is what lets people coordinate on them; and socially constructed categories are moved by changing the territory so that a different concept becomes the Schelling point.42 Davis absorbed this. By "Blood Is Thicker Than Water" he was saying that "There's conditional independence structure in the causal graph" is a meaningfully deeper explanation than "There's a cluster in configuration space", and that configuration space itself is a convenience whose construction the deeper account has to explain.

The dolphin war

In June 2021 Nate Soares, then executive director of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, tweeted that the definitional gymnastics required to believe dolphins are not fish are staggering, and Yudkowsky retweeted it. Davis's "Reply to Nate Soares on Dolphins" is a short post with a long tail. Its argument is the "root of the causal graph" idea that he credits to tailcalled and to Wentworth's conditional independence: genetics are at the root of the causal graph underlying all other features of an organism, such that creatures that are genetically close to each other are more similar in general, which is why Unambiguous discrete features make for better word definitions than high-dimensional statistical regularities, even if most of the everyday inferential utility of using the word comes from fuzzy high-dimensional statistical correlates. A definition is a pointer to a natural abstraction, not a summary of it. And "fish" is polysemous: Wiktionary's sense 2 is the swimmy animals, and nobody is trying to take that away; Soares is the one proposing to abolish sense 1. The post ends with a wink that no one could miss: Could there, perhaps, be some additional context to this conversation that Soares neglected to make explicit? That seems unlikely, however.43

Soares answered at length and with good grace. His epistemic status had been "i been shitpostin since april"; the fragment he endorsed was "be wary of those who say they are here to refine your concepts, for sometimes they are here to steal them"; that thread was not a veiled attempt to discuss gender issues; and what had changed since 2008 was that I noticed the subtle background forces that whisper (at least to blue tribe members in their youth) "phylogenetic classification is the one true way to organize life forms", and rejected its claim.44 Vaniver added the best object-level point in the thread: by divergence times, sharks are farther from guppies than dolphins are, so the 2008 fish list was bad phylogenetics to begin with.45 Davis's own conduct in the thread was poor by his admission: a comment asking Am I the only one creeped out by this? and a later one at −26 karma, followed by an apology; and an unpublished reply, preserved as a hidden page on his blog, in which he tells Soares that If you clowns can't get your own philosophy of language right, why should we trust you to save the world?46

The calmer sequel, "Blood Is Thicker Than Water 🐬" (September 2021), is the best statement of the mature position on natural kinds. Phylogeny matters not as an æsthetic choice, but for structural reasons that can be understood mechanistically: conditioning on the genome d-separates the phenotype nodes, which is the conditional-independence structure that makes a category node worth having, and it is why one can expect to learn new things about dolphins (the number of bones, say) that were never among the dimensions in which the cluster was first noticed. Blood is thicker than water (that is, the similarities induced by shared blood are a thicker subspace of configuration space than the similarities induced by living in the water). Against the charge that phylogeny demands monophyly, Davis replies that there is no principle that it would be hypocritical to betray, that definitions need to be monophyletic clades; paraphyletic groups like fish, reptiles, and monkeys are fine when the excluded branch has acquired salient differences, and the continuity of evolution only asks that good categories be connected in phylogenetic space. Against "fish used to mean the swimmy animals," he produces Aristotle distinguishing cetaceans from fish in the fourth century BCE and the Old High German fledarmūs.47 This is, in effect, Boyd's homeostatic-property-cluster account of species arrived at from the other direction, and the "root of the causal graph" is a better name for the mechanism than "homeostasis." It is Davis's most durable contribution to the subject, and it owes its two key ideas to commenters.

The post's reception shows the pattern that recurs through the program: the top comment, by aphyer, says that cooks and botanists both have legitimate uses of "fruit" and that a demand for a single sense is denying cooks access to a useful regularity that they care about; Davis replies I actually agree with this. Alexander, appearing as Yvain, asks what happens after a billion more years of convergent evolution make whales 99 percent fish-like, and whether there is not a long period in between when you're allowed to categorize them however you want based on which is more useful for you? If the latter, what makes you think we're not in that period right now?, adding I think "crabs" are in this situation right now. Davis: No, of course not: we want categories that give useful information.48 Each time, the critic says "there are several legitimate categories and use picks among them," and each time Davis agrees, and insists that the agreement was already in the post. It usually was. The posts are written as though they are contradicting someone who denies that dolphins are mammals, and nobody does.

One last piece belongs here. "Comment on 'Deception as Cooperation'" (November 2021) reports Manolo Martínez's rate–distortion reframing of signaling games, in which a sender and receiver with partly divergent interests jointly construct a code, and in which calling an individual codeword "deceptive" can seem to miss that the code is a cooperative achievement. Davis's response is a sentence the whole program needed: I am, ultimately, willing to bite this bullet. Agents with different interests will fight about where to draw the boundaries in state-space, and we can still strive to notice the difference between maps optimized to reflect decision-relevant aspects of territory, and maps optimized to control other agents' decisions.49 "Strive to notice the difference" is a much weaker claim than "unnatural categories are optimized for deception," and it is the claim the evidence supports.

5. Objections

I take five objections in turn, in roughly increasing order of how much of Davis's program they touch. The first two were pressed by his critics and he answered them, partly. The third and fourth are my own, built from his concessions. The fifth concerns the empirical premise and is only sketched, because the full treatment belongs to the sexology chapter.

(a) Conventional kinds

Ozy Brennan's 2018 reply made the point with money. Bitcoin, gold, dollar bills, and prison cigarettes are all money, and what makes them money is that everyone agrees; this is not circular, since the definition is "any item or verifiable record that is generally accepted as payment for goods and services and repayment of debts in a particular country or socio-economic context." Social gender is like money. It predicts pronouns, gendered terms, holidays, who gets to hold the baby, who gets harassed on the street, and so forth; and "you're a woman if you identify as a woman" is not a definition but a criterion for who should be put in the category, so that the decision of who should be socially gendered male or female, and how many social genders we should have is not an epistemic decision. This decision can and should be made on purely utilitarian grounds. On the women's-events problem Ozy had a sharper reply still: The actual category they should be using is not "cis women." The actual category they should be using is "people who would be contribute to the atmosphere you made this a woman-only event for." Allocate more categories; do not fight over the old one.

Davis's answer came eighteen months later in "Reply to Ozymandias on Fully Consensual Gender," and it is better than his critics usually allow. He accepts the analogy and turns it: not any form of money will serve, because perishable goods cannot store value and non-fungible ones cannot be a unit of account; by Gresham's law, in a market that is required to treat defaced Canadian five-dollar notes as American, smart buyers will prefer to buy with defaced Canadian dollars, and smart sellers will try to find plausibly-deniable excuses to not accept them; and so with gender: even if everyone says the words, people following their own self-interest are likely to systematically make decisions that treat non-well-passing trans women as if they were something more like men, even if no one would dream of being so rude as to admit out loud that that's what they're doing. "Consent" is the wrong word, because it names an asymmetry of interests where there is none, and resolving a conflict between a person's wish to be modeled one way and another's perception in the direction of "consent of the modeled" would amount to mind control, or at least compelled speech. Fully consensual gender, he concludes, is a coherent position. That doesn't make it feasible.50

Notice what this argument is and is not. It is an argument that a convention must be backstopped by enforcement and that a convention which contradicts what people perceive will leak. It is not an argument that conventional kinds are illegitimate or that their boundaries are "deceptive." On the contrary, Davis grants that money is a convention whose feasibility depends on many facts of economics and sociology, and that fiat currency, which would once have provoked "how dumb do you think I am," now circulates. The money analogy therefore cuts both ways. If a convention can carry fiat paper from absurdity to universality, the question about a gender convention is a question about feasibility, cost, and enforcement, which is a utilitarian question of just the kind Ozy said it was. Davis's 2020 LessWrong comment on this point concedes the structure while refusing the conclusion: coordination, deception, and self-fulfilling prophecy are three "complications" to the prediction-only theory, and of the third he writes, I'm not sure I understand the theory of this yet. His conclusion in the same comment, that it's not the map's job to provide compensatory usefulness; the map's job is to reflect the territory, is true of maps and false of money. The commenter TAG put it in one line: You can make predictions about money, but that is not the central purpose of money.51 So the first objection stands, with a qualification. Davis is right that a conventional category whose convention outruns perception will be unstable and will be privately defected from; "fully consensual gender" is not a free lunch. He is wrong that this makes the convention a kind of lie rather than a kind of institution with costs.

There is a philosophical literature exactly here, and Davis does not engage it. Sally Haslanger's "ameliorative" project defines gender terms by what we want them to do for us, and defines "woman" as a position of systematic subordination marked by presumed female reproductive role; the Stanford Encyclopedia's summary notes that on this definition the Queen of England may not count as a woman, and that Haslanger accepts the revisionary result because the definitions are not meant to capture our intuitive gender terms.52 That is the position Davis is attacking, held explicitly and with its reasons given; it is also, as Robin Dembroff points out, a project that ties prescription to description rather than abandoning the latter.53 Dembroff's own suggestion, that in political contexts "woman" and "man" function as floating signifiers that are doing normative, political work more so than descriptive work, is the same observation as Wei Dai's 2024 comment that "trans women are women" may be a signal of local values, trying to imply something like "people around here do not distinguish between trans women and cis women in their values, at least in most circumstances".54 Davis's reply to Wei Dai was that the value should then be argued for on the merits rather than smuggled into a noun. That is fair. But a theory on which every non-predictive use of a category is deception has no room for the ordinary fact that calling someone a woman can be a way of saying how one intends to treat her, and that fact does not go away because one disapproves of it.

(b) Deception overreach

The second objection is to the title of the 2021 post. Several commenters noticed that an openly declared category system is not functionally a lie. tslarm: If I'm honest about the boundaries of my new category system, how is this deceptive? Daniel Filan: redefining "book" to include vegan chocolate bars and redefining it to include a small number of random other things are not equivalent, and only the second resembles reserving the right to lie.55 Davis's answer, in both cases, is to point to "Maybe Lying Can't Exist?!": deception is defined relative to the cooperative subgame, and a usage that is priced in has not thereby stopped being optimized against the receiver's interest. But that reply proves too much. It makes "deceptive" a property of a usage's history (what it was optimized for) rather than of its current effect on anyone's beliefs, which is the sense in which the firefly mimic remains "deceptive" after the prey have adjusted. Under that definition every convention that was once contested is deceptive forever, including the ones Davis likes.

Davis himself has the materials for a more modest claim. In "Comment on 'Deception as Cooperation'" he bites the bullet and retreats to "strive to notice the difference." In the containment thread and in the long correspondence with Abram Demski that he narrates in the memoir, he goes further. Demski had asked whether, ideally, all conceptual boundaries should not be drawn with appeal to consequences, since the reason Bayesianism is an attractor is that probabilistic reasoning is useful; a design with a separate epistemic module answerable to nothing but accuracy is, in Demski's words as Davis reports them, a split-brain in which an instrumental task-monkey does all the important stuff while an epistemic overseer supervises. Davis's reply, fourteen months later: on further thought, he was right, and it made sense to regard "have accurate beliefs" as merely a convergent instrumental subgoal, rather than what rationality is about—as sacrilegious as that felt to type.56 If accuracy is instrumental, then Alexander's "nothing to make those decisions on except consequences" is not a fallacy but the correct meta-level description, and the real disagreement is about which consequences count and over what horizon. Davis's practical answer, that consequences for the model's accuracy count and consequences for third parties' feelings do not, is a defensible rule for a truth-seeking institution. It is not a theorem about intelligent systems, and the 2021 post presents it as one.

(c) Polysemy, and the dilemma that goes both ways

The third objection is that Davis is a contextualist about "woman" who argues as if he were not. The evidence is in his own texts: "it's not uncommon for words to have both narrower and broader definitions which can be distinguished from context" (2018); water(1) includes ice and water(2) does not (2021); fish(1) and fish(2); berry(1) and berry(2); and in the containment thread, when TekhneMakre proposed that a word for "the cluster of people who want to be treated the way women are treated" is legitimate, Davis agreed that it is fine where unambiguous and said the problem is only that a dominant faction in my Society really doesn't want it to be socially permissible to disambiguate.57 This is the view that Jennifer Saul, Talia Bettcher, Elizabeth Barnes, and Dembroff defend in the philosophical literature under the names contextualism and polysemy, and that Alex Byrne, the philosopher whose conclusion ("women are adult human females") Davis's most resembles, treats as the main obstacle to his thesis. Byrne's "polysemy puzzle" is Davis's dilemma from the other side: if "woman" has several meanings, shouldn't one of these meanings correspond to the category adult human female? Why wouldn't it?58 Davis would say yes, obviously, and that is all he has ever asked.

But once the view is stated that way, the thing Davis spent years calling a philosophy-of-language error shrinks to a dispute about which sense should be default and whether the other sense may be spoken. Those are questions about social norms, not about whether categories can be wrong. Davis's genuine dilemma for the other side is this: either "woman" is polysemous, in which case "trans women are women" is true in the broad sense, uninformative about the narrow one, and no reason to forbid the narrow one; or it is univocal, in which case the sentence is a substantive claim that needs an argument. That dilemma is sound and it is his best weapon. The matching dilemma for Davis is that if "woman" is polysemous, then insisting that the narrow sense is "the" natural category, the one that "carves reality at the joints," is itself a choice among joints, and the choice is governed, by his own theory, by what speakers want to talk about and can coordinate on. He concedes as much whenever a critic says it. What he does not concede is that the concession dissolves the frame of "Truth versus Feelings."

(d) Two natural categories

Which brings me to the objection I think is decisive against the polemic, if not against the philosophy. Davis's framework licenses at least two natural categories in the neighborhood of "woman," and his writing moves between them without ever settling which one the short word belongs to.

The first is perceived sex: the output of the pre-verbal, subconscious, System 1 process that notices breasts, facial structure, voice, gait, and so on, which, as he says, is not directly focused on genitalia or chromosomes. This category is natural by every criterion in the program: it is what the classifier in people's heads computes, it supports a vast range of inferences, and passing trans people are inside it. Davis affirms this in 2018, affirms in the dolphin-war draft that she is obviously the correct pronoun for people who look like women, and in "Challenges" affirms that he does not misgender people in public. The second is developmental sex: the cluster rooted at the SRY gene, which, as "Blood Is Thicker Than Water" and "Beyond the Binary" argue, buys conditional independence across every downstream feature including ones not yet discovered, and which no current intervention changes. This category is natural too, and more robust. Davis's later writing treats it as the referent: in 2024 he says the thing one must be able to say in public is that trans women are male and trans men are female with respect to a concept of binary sex that encompasses the many traits that aren't affected by contemporary surgical and hormonal interventions.59

Both claims are true "with respect to" their categories, as Davis's phrasing acknowledges. The question that his framework poses and that he never squarely answers is: which of these should the top-20 noun be reserved for, and why? The program's own answer, from "Blood Is Thicker Than Water," is that the short word tends to go to the thicker subspace and to the category one needs most often (You'll probably end up talking about {u, w} more often than {u, v} because the former cluster is in a "thicker", higher-dimensional subspace). But that is a claim about usage frequency and decision relevance, and in everyday social life the decisions people make with gender words (pronouns, forms of address, who to flirt with, which bathroom) are overwhelmingly driven by perceived sex, not karyotype. Davis knows this; it is why he grants that passing works and why "Self-Identity Is a Schelling Point" treats the self-ID convention as a solution to disagreements about perceived sex. The cases where developmental sex is decision-relevant (medicine, reproduction, sport, sex-segregated spaces) are real and he lists them, but they are the cases for which, as Ozy said and he conceded, more specific vocabulary is available and in use. So the dispute over the default sense of "woman" is, on Davis's own analysis, a conflict between two natural categories, in which one side wants the word's default to track perception and self-report (a Schelling point with costs) and the other wants it to track development (a Schelling point with costs). The memoir's framing, a battle between Feelings and Truth, between Politics and Truth, is unavailable to someone who holds the theory of the 2019 posts. The battle is between two truths and the policy question of which to make default. That is what Alexander said in 2014, badly.

I do not think Davis would accept this restatement, and I can state his best reply. It is that the two categories are not symmetric: perceived sex is a proxy for developmental sex, an evolved detector whose whole function is to estimate the thing that matters for mating, so that when the proxy and the target come apart the proxy has misfired, and a convention that enshrines the misfire enshrines an error. That is the point of the mimicry section of "Unnatural Categories" and of the sentence about the trans woman who looks like a male celebrity: passing as a woman isn't the same thing as actually being female. The reply has force, and it is why the program is not simply refuted. But it depends on a further premise: that the thing people mostly want gender words for is the target rather than the proxy. For a sexologist, a physician, or a man deciding whom to date, that is true. For most of the rest of social life it is the proxy that does the work, as Davis's own account of "social gender" in 2018 and of Julia Serano's transition in "Crossing the Line" (2020) describes.60 Which premise is right is an empirical question about how a speech community uses its words, and it is not settled by information theory.

(e) The empirical premise

Finally, the empirical premise that most trans women are, along most psychological dimensions, drawn from the male distribution. The chapter on sexology examines the two-type taxonomy; here I note only how the categorization argument depends on it and what the mathematics does and does not establish.

The univariate fallacy is a fallacy, and Davis is right to deploy it against the inference from overlap-per-trait to indistinguishability. The brain-mosaic dispute is the cleanest test case. Daphna Joel and colleagues reported in 2015 that across more than 1,400 MRI scans most brains are comprised of unique "mosaics" of features, some more common in females compared with males, some more common in males compared with females, and some common in both females and males, and concluded that human brains do not belong to one of two distinct categories: male brain/female brain.61 Marco Del Giudice and colleagues replied that the method systematically fails to detect large, consistent sex differences.62 Joel's own online response concedes the point that matters for Davis: predicting sex from brain structure can clearly be done, and I am sure with much better accuracy than Del Guidice and colleagues achieved; what her group denies is the reverse inference, from sex category to an individual's mosaic.63 Davis's summary in 2021, that lack of "internal consistency" is exactly the behavior we expect from multivariate normal-ish distributions with different-but-not-vastly-different means, is therefore a fair reading of a dispute in which both sides agree on the classification accuracy and disagree about which direction of inference the word "dimorphic" should track. Which is to say: the mosaic dispute is itself a dispute about what the categories are for, and Davis's side of it is the side that cares about classifying individuals by sex.

What the mathematics does not establish is where trans women fall. The 2018 scatterplot is fictitious by its own caption. The claim that the purple cluster sits with the blue one on most axes rests on the etiological thesis that late-onset gender dysphoria in males is mostly not an intersex condition, and that thesis is contested in the literature and, in Davis's own later work, softened: by 2022 he describes the two-type taxonomy as a useful approximation for this causal model, a model in which transition is a threshold on the sum of femininity, autogynephilia, and cultural factors, and in which one can buy decent predictive accuracy by casually speaking as if there were two discrete "types", and in 2021 he published a post titled "You Are Right and I Was Wrong" conceding to tailcalled that Different causal theories can generate the same correlations in a particular set of observations, while still making meaningfully and drastically different claims about the world.64 The categorization argument is explicitly conditional on the sexology, and Davis has always said so. The reader should hold the two apart: the laws of categorization that the LessWrong posts articulate do not depend on any claim about trans women; the application to trans women depends on a claim that the laws cannot supply.

6. Pronouns, compression, and the late synthesis (2022–2025)

After 2021 the categorization writing changes register. The LessWrong posts had argued in the abstract; the four pieces treated here apply the finished theory to Yudkowsky directly, and two of them are the clearest statements of the mature view.

"Challenges to Yudkowsky's Pronoun Reform Proposal"

In February 2021 Yudkowsky posted on Facebook that the lack of a gender-neutral singular pronoun is a design flaw in English and that the simplest and best protocol is, "'He' refers to the set of people who have asked us to use 'he', with a default for those-who-haven't-asked that goes by gamete size" and to say that this just is the normative definition.65 Davis's twelve-thousand-word reply, the longest thing in the categorization corpus after the memoirs, makes one argument from several angles. The proposal is self-undermining, because if it were actually true that he simply referred to those who take the pronoun he, then there would be no reason for trans people to care which pronoun people used for them. People ask for "she" because, in English, those are the pronouns for males and females respectively; misgendering is received as an attack, not as a parsing error. "Pronouns shouldn't convey sex-category information" is a fine motte for a bailey that does not follow from it. The claim that Yudkowsky cannot feel a pronoun attached to anything in his head "more firmly than 'doesn't look like an Oliver'" is met with a joke about a man named Oliver whose face is shown; if Yudkowsky gets the joke, Davis argues, his brain has done the thing he says it does not do.

The post's most important move for this chapter is a distinction it draws against both Yudkowsky and the crude version of its own side: pronouns can have meaningful semantics without being denotative statements that can be straightforwardly "false", and the people Yudkowsky is arguing with are largely not claiming that "pronouns are lies". That is right, and it should be read back onto "Unnatural Categories": if a pronoun is not a proposition, then the equivalence between a category system and a lying policy, which was proved for declarative signals, does not apply to it directly. What applies is the weaker claim that a pronoun convention conveys sex-category information and that a convention which detaches the pronoun from the category imposes a cost on everyone whose head still has them attached. Davis's own case is the example: asking people to call him "she" would be an obviously incredibly bad decision, because anyone who looks at me can see at a glance that I'm male, and the cost would be this tremendous awkwardness and fakeness suffusing all future social interactions involving me. The conclusion is not that Yudkowsky's policy is wrong but that it is not neutral: sometimes there are cases where there is no "neutral" policy, because all available policies impose costs on someone and there's no solution that everyone is happy with.

This is the coordination theory of section 4 again, now applied with care, and it is a strong post. The LessWrong reception is instructive. Oliver Habryka, who runs the site, wrote that basically everything in this post strikes me as "obviously true" and that Yudkowsky's original post had shifted his sense of the community from trusting its leadership to maintain epistemic coherence under pressure to expecting that depending on the circumstances community leaders might make up sophisticated stories for why pretty obviously true things are false in order to not have to deal with complicated political issues.66 gjm, who had criticized "Blegg Mode" in 2019, wrote that Zack is clever and insightful and I agree with most of the specific things he says, but that pretty much all he ever does here is to point out errors made in support of one particular side of a live sociopolitical debate, and that it's possible for one's thinking to be made worse overall by a process of correcting errors, if the choice of which errors has enough bias in it.67 Both are fair. The post is right about what it says and selective in what it chooses to say it about, and Davis, in the 2025 piece discussed below, would concede the selection and defend it.

"Beyond the Binary"

"Beyond the Binary" (December 2023) is the mature theory stated in three thousand words, and it is the post a reader with limited time should read.68 It begins with an invented puzzle (a human whose gonads produce eggs but who appears male in every other respect: female or male?) and refuses it: such a person does not exist, and the cognitive technology of "words" having "definitions" doesn't work in the world of the imagination. Then the bitstring model. In a world where every twenty-bit string is equally likely, no five-bit codeword can compress anything; language is possible only because the distribution is non-uniform, because almost all the strings one sees are near all-zeros or all-ones. A definition is a pointer: Discrete features make for better word definitions than high-dimensional statistical regularities, even if most of the everyday inferential utility of using the word comes from the high-dimensional statistical correlates. Gametes, genitals, and chromosomes are the never-flipped first bit: It's not that people directly care about gametes or chromosomes or even genitals in most everyday situations. It is that they are discrete features that are causally entangled with everything else that differs between females and males, including differences that haven't even been discovered yet. And then the admission that the previous section has been building toward: because our brains are good at using sex-category words to simultaneously encode predictions about both absolute discrete differences and high-dimensional statistical regularities of various effect sizes, without our being consciously aware of the cognitive work being done, it's easy to get confused by verbal gymnastics if you don't know the theory.

The post is even-handed in a way the LessWrong material rarely was. It says that the gender-critical slogan "sex is just gametes" isn't going to fly either, because people who defend single-sex spaces too want mere statistical tendencies to be part of the concept; it grants that the stereotype complaint against trans ideology has something to it because removing the discrete anchors leaves nothing but statistical tendencies; and it bites the bullet it has been avoiding since 2018, that on this view masculine women and feminine men are in some sense less "real" women and men, respectively. It even offers the olive branch Ozy had offered in 2018, in Davis's own idiom: maybe we should talk about categories like "masculine people" (including both typical men, and butch lesbians) more often! The one sentence I would press on is the hinge: the typical trans advocate shell game of just replacing "sex" with "gender" and letting people choose their "gender" isn't going to fly, because sex actually exists and we have a need for language to talk about it. Both halves of the "because" are true. Neither entails that the short word must be the one reserved for sex. The post's own theory, that definitions are pointers to natural abstractions and that the everyday utility comes from the correlates, would be equally satisfied by a convention in which "woman" points to the perceived-sex abstraction and "female" to the developmental one, which is roughly the convention Davis's interlocutors have been proposing. He has shown that there must be a word for sex. He has not shown which word.

"Prediction Markets Are Not a Drop-In Replacement for Concepts"

The 2024 post takes its text from Yudkowsky's fiction, in which a character suggests that a military with prediction markets would not need to consult sex, since If there's more information to be gained from measuring biological sex than by just measuring height, it'll show up in the military prediction markets.69 Davis's reply is a short, clean piece of decision theory. A market can only match a decision procedure if its traders are free to run that procedure, so the market's existence is no argument against the procedure; It would be lying to claim that Society doesn't need to compute P because it has prediction markets, if, "under the hood", the traders in the market are in fact computing P all the time. A restaurant-choice market does not abolish the concept of Italian food, because the first thing a trader will do is search for Italian restaurants, not because they're fools who think that "Italian food" is somehow ontologically fundamental and eternal, but because there contingently do happen to be approximate conditional independence relationships between the features of meals served by different restaurants. And the real question is institutional: whether institutions should be able to take category membership into account as a matter of explicit policy (rather than only implicitly via the black box of a prediction market, whose traders are allowed to notice things that the policy isn't). The argument is correct and the point generalizes: "we have a better predictor, so we don't need the concept" is a fallacy whenever the predictor uses the concept. The post is also a good example of the deniable-allegory method working as intended, since it can be read in full without any reference to gender and is, in that reading, simply right. I note, for the record of reception, that the post ends by reading the fiction's text closely to decide whether its author endorses the character's view, and concludes that he does; this is literary criticism of a kind the chapter cannot assess, since I have not read the novel.

"A Generic Ontological Finickiness"

The last post in the sequence (2025) answers a Yudkowsky thread arguing that outrage at transition is tied to what a culture thinks is being stolen, not to generic ontological finickiness, and is therefore not a philosophical position at all.70 Davis's answer is the best short defense of the whole program's motive. People who point out that pyrite is not gold are still telling the truth even if their motivation for speaking up is that they think gold is valuable and pyrite is not, rather than a "generic ontological finickiness." He concedes the selectivity gjm had complained of (I selfishly focus on the topics that are important to me.) and claims the generality at the level of method: there are general laws of cognition governing language and thought, which I seek to understand and apply in my work in the hopes of improving the accuracy of shared maps. The methodology is general even if the choice of topic is idiosyncratic and personal. He also, in a footnote, states the inferential claim in the form the sexology chapter will have to assess: what it means to say that 6'2" women "are still women", is that we can confidently predict they'll be more female-typical on average among the innumerable other measurable properties of a person.

Set the 2025 post beside the 2018 one and the shape of the development is clear. The empirical criterion for category membership (cheap features that predict decision-relevant ones) has been replaced by a causal one (a discrete root variable that buys conditional independence across everything downstream). The claim that redefinition is "deception" has been restated as the claim that one can tell the truth selectively. The hostility to "social construction" has been replaced by a theory of Schelling categories on which social constructions are real coordination equilibria with costs. What has not changed is the insistence that a particular word belongs to a particular cluster, and that is the one claim the theory never supported.

7. The Category War as history

The three memoir posts of 2023–24 ("A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning," "If Clarity Seems Like Death to Them," "Agreeing With Stalin in Ways That Exhibit Generally Rationalist Principles") narrate the dispute as Davis experienced it, and they are the fullest account of the Category War there is. They are also one side's account, written five years later with the correspondence to hand; the other parties have not published theirs, and most of the primary documents (Yudkowsky's tweets, his Facebook posts, the emails) are either login-walled or private. What follows takes the memoirs as evidence of Davis's reasoning and conduct, which they are, and treats their claims about other people's motives as claims.71

What happened

On 28 November 2018 Yudkowsky posted a Twitter thread which, as Davis quotes it, began: Some people I usually respect for their willingness to publicly die on a hill of facts, now seem to be talking as if pronouns are facts, or as if who uses what bathroom is necessarily a factual statement about chromosomes. Come on, you know the distinction better than that! Later tweets in the thread said that "Using language in a way you dislike, openly and explicitly and with public focus on the language and its meaning, is not lying" and that "you're not standing in defense of truth if you insist on a word, brought explicitly into question, being used with some particular meaning." Davis read this as a reversal of "37 Ways That Words Can Be Wrong," and the memoir is candid about the magnitude of his reaction: if the rationalists were going to get the philosophy of language wrong and Eliezer Yudkowsky was in on it (!!!), that was intolerable, inexplicable, incomprehensible—like there wasn't a real world anymore. He emailed Yudkowsky with an offer to pay a "cheerful price" to have his post read; he and a small group of allies wrote again, and again, and a fourth time after "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" was published in April 2019. He explains the dolphin post's indirection with a line from Schelling about helping one's counterparty make a concession: By making the post be about dolphins, I was trying to deny myself too great of a reward on the gender-politics front. In December 2019 he wrote to Alexander asking for an explicit and loud clarification or partial retraction of the 2014 essay; Alexander's editorial note followed within the month. In September 2020, after a further angry email that the memoir reproduces at length, Yudkowsky published a Facebook post that, as Davis reads it, restricted his 2018 criticism to naive essentialism and contained the line you are being the bad guy if you try to shut down that conversation by saying that "I can define the word 'woman' any way I want". Davis: There it is! A clear ex cathedra statement that gender categories are not an exception to the general rule that categories aren't arbitrary. He emailed his allies a thank-you note with the subject line "the end of the Category War," and wrote, in retrospect, At the cost of two years of my life, we finally got a clarification from Yudkowsky that you can't define the word woman any way you like.

Yudkowsky's February 2021 pronoun post reopened it, and the dolphin tweets of June 2021 reopened it again. By 2024 the memoir's judgment had hardened into a conditional charge: if Yudkowsky can't unambiguously choose Truth over Feelings, then he's a fraud. Davis insists this is meant in Merriam-Webster's sense, "a person who is not what he or she pretends to be", applies Yudkowsky's own words about Stephen Jay Gould ("[c]onsistently self-serving scientific 'error', in the face of repeated correction and without informing others of the criticism, blends over into scientific fraud."), and reserves evaluation as a separate step. Of Alexander he says only: Scott Alexander chose Feelings, but I can't hold that against him, because Scott is explicit about only speaking in the capacity of some guy with a blog.

What it shows

Three things stand out when the story is set beside the philosophy.

First, the narrow point was conceded, and the memoirs say so. Alexander's note says that one should "definitely worry about" definitions and that plausible ones are limited to a few possibilities suggested by the territory. Yudkowsky's clarification says that "I can define 'woman' any way I want" is the bad guy's move. Those are the propositions "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" was written to establish, and by Davis's own reading they were established. What did not change was anyone's policy: Alexander still used trans people's preferred pronouns, Yudkowsky still proposed self-chosen pronouns with a gamete-size default, and so, Davis says more than once, does Davis. On the account of the dispute given in section 5(d), that is the expected outcome. If the question of the word's default sense is a coordination problem between two natural categories, then establishing that categories are not arbitrary leaves the coordination problem exactly where it was. The war was won and nothing moved because the thing won was not the thing contested.

Second, the memoir shows the apparatus of the program being applied with a strictness that the program itself does not support. The "fraud" charge rests on the equivalence of "Unnatural Categories": a speaker who adopts a boundary for its effects is, functionally, lying, and a speaker who does so while claiming epistemic heroism is a fraud. But section 5(b) argued, from Davis's own concessions to Demski and Martínez, that the equivalence holds only relative to a cooperative baseline and that an openly declared convention is not deception in the sense that matters. Yudkowsky's pronoun proposal is openly declared. One can think it wrong, as Davis does and as I do in part, without its being a lie; and the move from "wrong" to "fraud" is the move that the philosophy, read carefully, forbids. Davis's allies pushed him toward the stronger word, and the memoir records his own resistance to it: I still sympathized with the pushback from Caliphate supporters against using "scam"/"fraud"/"lie"/&c. language to include motivated elephant-in-the-brain-like distortions. By 2024 the resistance had gone.

Third, the method. The cheerful prices, the group emails, the stealth posts, and the memoir's reconstruction of private correspondence are all defended in the text on decision-theoretic grounds, and the defenses are not frivolous. But the record also contains the costs. A commenter told Davis's collaborator that by copying the group on the emails he had "sacrificed all hope of success in favor of maintaining his own sanity by CC'ing you guys." Anna Salamon asked him to stop sending cheerful-price offers. The dolphin thread ended in an apology. And Michael Vassar, the ally who pushed hardest, eventually told Davis that the philosophy articulated in "A Human's Guide to Words" was inadequate for politicized environments where our choice of ontology is constrained: that without a third gender or a public fluent in cluster-talk, the available ways to think about trans women were creepy men (the TERF narrative), crazy men (the medical model), or a protected class of actual woman. That is the coordination objection of section 5(a), from inside the posse, and the memoir reports it without answering it.

Reception of the memoirs

The LessWrong reception of the memoirs was large and mostly serious. The moderator Raemon, responding to Davis's summary of what he had wanted, wrote that he agreed Alexander's sentence was false and Scott should retract it and we should all agree he should retract it, and that "it's important to preserve a category for 'actual women' that you can still talk about." But he was still unsure whether you think this court ruling is about "on LessWrong / in truth-focused contexts/communities", "worldwide in all contexts", and whether the demand was that rationalists refuse a social category of women that includes unsuccessfully transitioned trans women because of reasoning like "there are rules of rationality suggesting this has epistemic costs, but the social benefits outweigh those costs."72 That is the question this chapter has been asking, and the fact that a sympathetic reader could not find the answer in a fifty-thousand-word memoir is itself a finding.

Vaniver's summary for a baffled commenter is the most balanced account of the affair by a third party that I found. The community's implicit promises were about core epistemic virtues and discussion practices; Davis saw Eliezer as breaking a lot of these core expectations, not being open about it or acknowledging it when being called out, and also others not reacting to Eliezer breaking those expectations. But the promises were (I think) clearly aspirational / mediated by the pressures of having to actually exist in the world. I do think it makes sense to have a heresy budget, and I think Zack got unlucky with the obsession lottery. And the sentence that best states what went wrong: Instead they made philosophical arguments that, as far as I can tell, were not correct, and this was crazy-making.73 I think Vaniver is right on every point, including the one that cuts against Davis. A community that had said "we are being greengrocers on this issue, sorry" would have been conceding a coordination constraint, which is what Davis's own theory says the constraint is. What it did instead was produce bad philosophy to avoid saying that, and Davis was right to object to the bad philosophy, and wrong to conclude that the thing being protected was a falsehood rather than a convention.

Hostile reception exists and should be recorded. On the subreddit SneerClub in 2023, a commenter who read "The Categories Were Made for Man to Make Predictions" wrote that an analysis which questions whether trans women form a homogeneous category without questioning whether 'cis women' makes sense as a category is biased, and that this makes the idea of taking this writer seriously as a thinker on gender a nonstarter.74 That objection is answered by the 2021 and 2023 posts (the cis category is "questioned" at length, and found to be rooted at a discrete variable), but it was made against the 2018 essay, which does take the female cluster for granted. The rest of the thread is abuse and does not engage the arguments.

8. Verdict

The philosophy of categorization in Davis's two blogs is a single sustained argument, begun in 2018 against an essay by Scott Alexander, developed on LessWrong between 2019 and 2021 as a sequel to Yudkowsky's "A Human's Guide to Words," and restated in mature form in 2023. Its core is correct. Alexander's 2014 essay equivocated between "useful for inference" and "useful for someone's welfare," and inferred from the first that a boundary may be drawn for the second; Davis saw this, said it, and was eventually conceded the point by both of the people whose words he was contesting. Along the way he added to Yudkowsky's framework several things that were not in it: the subspace reconciliation of the two dolphin verdicts; the mutual-information analysis of title inflation; squared-error "compactness" as the thing a good category buys; the Schelling-category theory of coordination under limited observation; the root-of-the-causal-graph account of why phylogeny is a thick subspace and why discrete markers make good definitions; the univariate fallacy; and the observation that a predictor which uses a concept cannot replace it. Any of these would be a respectable contribution to a philosophy-of-language seminar, and together they constitute the most rigorous thing anyone in the rationalist community has written on the subject.

The program's reach exceeds its grasp in two places, and both were visible to its author. The theory of categories as prediction devices, with every non-predictive boundary treated as deception or wireheading, cannot accommodate conventional kinds: money, marriage, adulthood, and the social sense of gender are categories whose function is partly to be agreed on, and the Schelling-category post of 2019 already says so. Davis's critics pressed this (Ozy, TAG, Raemon, Wei Dai, Demski, aphyer), he conceded it each time, and the concession never made its way back into the thesis. Second, applied to "woman," the framework licenses two natural categories, perceived sex and developmental sex, and Davis's own 2018 scatterplot is a picture of the value-dependent choice between them. His later insistence that the short word belongs to the second is a claim about which cluster speakers most need, which is a sociolinguistic fact rather than an information-theoretic one, and he does not argue it. The "Truth versus Feelings" framing of the memoirs is thus unavailable to him. On his own theory the Category War was a coordination conflict between two truths, and the fact that it ended with the narrow point conceded and no policy changed is what that theory predicts.

Where I land on the object-level dispute, since the brief asks: Davis is right that it must remain sayable, in plain words, that trans women are male in the developmental sense and that this sense is causally thick; the norm that forbids the sentence is a real cost, and the philosophical arguments offered for the norm in 2014 and 2018 were bad. He is wrong that the broad, socially-anchored sense of "woman" is a lie, a shell game, or a category error, and his best work (Schelling categories, "Reply to Ozymandias," "Challenges," "Beyond the Binary") says so in all but the summary sentences. The sentence he most wanted conceded, Alexander's "there's no rule of rationality saying that I shouldn't," is false as stated, since there are such rules and they have costs; the sentence Alexander should have written, that the costs are worth paying, is the one Davis never engaged, because his theory had defined it as deception.

The empirical premise on which the application rests, that most trans women are drawn from the male distribution along most psychological dimensions, is held with a confidence that the categorization work cannot supply and that Davis's own 2021–22 revisions of the taxonomy partly withdraw. That question is deferred to the chapter on the sexology, where it belongs; the reader should not let the elegance of the categorization mathematics stand in for an answer to it.

Finally, the method. The deniable-allegory posts are a literary achievement and a philosophical one: "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" is good on dolphins whether or not one knows what it is about, and the comment thread shows how much a reader can learn from arguing about dolphins. But the stealth was a strategy for extracting a concession, and it cost its author the thing the posts were supposed to buy: a reception on the merits. gjm's complaint that the error-correction is one-sided is answered by the 2025 post, honestly, as a confession of motive, and that is the right answer. What the memoirs do with the concession, converting a philosophical disagreement that was eventually resolved into a charge of fraud, is the part of the corpus that the philosophy least supports and that a future reader is most likely to remember. The posts deserve to be remembered for the other thing.
