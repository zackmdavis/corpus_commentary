The Relationship of Male Transsexual Typology
to Psychosocial Adjustment
Sandra L. J o h n s o n , Ph.D. 1 and D . D a n i e l H u n t , M.D.2. 3

In a search to uncover variables predictive o f psychosocial adjustment o f
male transsexuals, this study examined features which have been empirically
linked to characteristics o f male transsexual typology. Data from 25 male
transsexual volunteers were examined by means o f multiple regression analysis to determine whether symptoms o f introversion, depression, or tension,
as well as adjustment to work and gender reorientation couM be linked to
the typological variables o f androphilia, gynephilia, cross-gender fetishism,
feminine gender identity in childhood, and age o f onset o f transsexualism.
Valid and reliable scales measuring the typological variables o f erotic partner
preference (androphilia and gynephilia), cross-gender fantasy in association
with sexual arousal (cross-gender fetishism), and degree o f feminine gender
identity in childhood were used. Results indicate a significant relationship
between social gender reorientation and the feature o f androphilia and between work adjustment and gynephilia. This differential adaptation is explained in terms o f the different course that transsexualism takes within each
typological subgroup. These findings offer evidence as to why some transsexuals may more readily adopt a female gender identity than others. There was
no relationship f o u n d between the typological features and those variables
measuring psychological disturbance.
KEY WORDS: transsexualism; cross-gender fetishism; cross-gender identity; erotic partner preference; gender reorientation.

INTRODUCTION
One of the earliest debates in the literature on transsexualism concerns
whether transsexuals are basically mentally sound or whether they suffer from
psychopathological conflict. Research into this controversy began to accumulate in the early 1970s. The early results (Stinson, 1972; Finney et al., 1975)
suggested that, as a group, transsexuals showed clear signs of significant ego
defect, or at least some degree of psychopathology. Other investigators
(Rosen, 1974; Roback et al., 1976; Tsushima and Wedding, 1979) indicated,
however, that though some transsexuals may show a vulnerability to mental
health adjustment, no real signs of psychopathology have been evidenced,
especially when transsexuals are compared as a group to other psychiatric
populations.
These results led some investigators to suspect that the research question was more complex and that the entire transsexual population could not
be usefully defined in such dichotomous terms as psychologically sick or healthy. Langevin et al. (1977) then suggested that some factor within the syndrome may discriminate between transsexuals who are psychologicallyhealthy
or unhealthy, and they began to look at the amount of time each of their
subjects had spent in the feminine role. They found that this factor seemed
to discriminate clearly between adjusted and unadjusted groups. It was Greenberg and Laurence (1981), however, who actually clarified the relationship
of living-in-the-role of a woman and level of disturbance° They tested their
hypothesis on four patient groups and concluded that this sample characteristic accounted for the discrepancies in the levels of psychopathology demonstrated by the various groups in the studies previously cited.
As is typical in many types of research, the issue of debate evolved into
a more complex question than it began with and the search for an answer
to the question of whether transsexuals are inherently psychologically disturbed
or well has shifted to an investigation of the variables that may predict or
assist their mental adjustment.
One study which attempted to systematically identify variables which
may moderate psychological adjustment in male transsexuals was conducted
by Blanchard et al. (1983). They formalized the idea of living as a woman
as a set of steps they call "gender reorientation" (p. 504). Basically, this variable has two components: a social component reflecting whether the transsexual is living as a woman at home, socially, and in the identification cards
carried; and a physical component reflecting whether they have begun hormone treatment or have had genital surgery. These factors were compared
by means of multiple regression analyses to two measures of psychological
adjustment, the MMPI Tension and Depression Cluster Scales (Stein, 1968)
and two measures for social adjustment, involvement and cohabitation. "Involvement" was defined as having been involved with a steady partner in the

past year, and "cohabitation" meant currently living with a lover or spouse.
Results indicated a significant negative correlation with the measures of social
feminization and the tension and depression scales and a significant positive
correlation between genital surgery and cohabitation with a partner. Thus
it was shown that social and physical gender reorientation appear to be highly
correlated to several indices of mental health adjustment. However, making
significant progress in undertaking the gender reorientation process only offers us another criterion to measure the psychological adjustment of transsexuals and offers no information about those who are poorly adjusted or those
who may be having trouble undertaking the feminine reorientation process.
For the proposed study, it seemed reasonable to assume that a more explanatory source for variables moderating mental health might come from the factors that describe consistent differences in the male transsexual populations;
that is, the male transsexual typology.
The investigation of typological subgroups in the area of transsexualism dates from the early 1900s (Hirschfeld, 1910, 1922). Since then many
others have added to this literature (American Psychiatric Association, 1980;
Benjamin, 1966; Bentler, 1976; Buhrich and McConaghy, 1978, 1979; Meyer,
1974; Person and Ovesey, 1974a, 1974b; Stoller, 1968). Fortunately, there
is widespread agreement on the categories of behavior that differentiate types.
In general, the clinical features that reoccur consistently are those relating
to erotic partner preference, presence or absence of cross-gender fetishism,
and to a lesser degree, recalled feminine gender identity in childhood.
Further empirical support for this hierarchy of descriptors has been revealed by Freund et al. (1982). The principle component analysis of these
and several other variables indicated that the dimension of heterosexuality
versus homosexuality, cross-gender fetishism, and feminine gender identity
in childhood accounted for 47% of the variance in subjects' scores on scales
measuring these factors. These data not only support the clinically observed
descriptions of transsexual types, but increase the evidence that there may
be two separate etiological tracks that develop into transsexualism: one track
proceeding from effeminate homosexuality and another from heterosexual
cross-gender fetishism (Freund et al., 1982; Buhrich and McConaghy, 1977,
1978, 1979).
In addition to the four established typological variables, age of onset
was also selected as a variable that may be important in relation to both typology and etiological theory. Though often recorded for the purposes of clinical
diagnosis, age of onset does not appear to have been systematically studied.
For the purposes of this study, this variable was defined as the age at which
evidence of cross-gender behavior or fantasy begins to appear. Evidence that
this variable may he related to typology is implied from research on cross-gender-identified children (Green, 1987). These data suggest that overt feminine
behavior in boyhood is more strongly associated with homosexuality and con-

sequently homosexual transsexuals. If this is true, then the age at which the
earliest memory of these behaviors is recalled should be younger for the homosexual transsexual than for the heterosexual fetishistic group, and this factor
should be related negatively to homosexual transsexualism. That late onset
may be related to psychopathology is suggested from an epidemiological study
by O'Gorman (1982). He found that transsexuals who showed a later onset
experienced enuresis in childhood and had a history of psychiatric illness.
This study, then, proposed to examine the variables that have been established as most descriptive of the transsexual syndrome in relation to indices
of psychoscial adjustment and disturbance. A significant relationship between
any of these variables and mental health may suggest, at least, whether there
are any psychosexual or developmental factors that can predict more consistently the psychological adjustment or disturbance of male transsexuals.

METHOD
Subjects

The subjects were 25 volunteer males recruited from the Seattle community. The principal source for the participants was the Ingersoll Gender
Center, a nonprofit agency organized for the purpose of providing a support
group and counseling referral services to individuals with gender conflicts.
The main recruiting strategy was an advertisement given out through support
groups, therapists, and an electrologist serving the transsexual community.
Participants were screened on the basis of their feminine gender stability.
This was assessed through the reponse to the question,"Have you ever felt
like a woman?" Those responding "at all times for at least one year" were
included in the sample. This question has been used by others (Freund et
al., 1982; Blanchard, 1985) and found to be reliable when compared to other
clinical screening methods. In addition, the majority of the respondents had
been in the transsexual community for at least 4 to 5 months and were known
to group leaders and others to be serious about pursuing gender change. All
participants indicated they either planned to have genital surgery, or were
postsurgical. No one was judged to be psychotic. All volunteers were paid
$20 for completed questionnaires.
Materials

The instruments are grouped into two categories, predictor variables
designed to measure the various typological features of male transsexualism
and criterion variables chosen to measure specific dimensions of psychological, social, and psychogenderal functioning. The predictor variables erotic

Male Transsexual Typology and Psychosocial Adjustment

partner preference were measured by the Modified Androphilia Scale (Andro)
and the Modified Gynephilia Scale (Gyne) (Freund et al., 1982; Blanchard,
1985). Androphilia (or androphile) is a term that refers specifically to a
homosexual male who prefers a physically mature partner as opposed to a
younger, immature sex partner, and a gynephile is the opposite or a heterosexual male who prefers a physically mature sex partner. Cross-gender fetishism was measured by the Cross-Gender Fetishism Scale (Blanchard, 1985).
Cross-gender identity in childhood was measured by a version of the Feminine
Gender Identity Scale (A) Revised (FGIS) (Freund et al., 1974, 1977). Blanchard (1985) recommended several changes in this scale to increase its validity
as a measure of femininity in childhood. Incorporating these changes resulted
in a 16-item scale with all items referenced to childhood (ages 6-12) and a
few to adolescence. Age of onset (Onset) was measured by two questions:
"About what age were you when you first recall wishing you were a girl or
woman?," and "About what age were you when you first dressed yourself
in girls' or women's clothing?" The age of onset was determined by the earliest
of the two reported ages, if there was a 4 or more year range between the
two, or if the ages were within a 4-year period, they were averaged.
The criterion variables measuring psychological disturbance were three
M M P I Cluster Scales, which are part of a group of seven scales developed
by Stein (1968) to overcome the problem of overlapping items in the MMPI.
The three scales chosen are TSC I, social introversion (Introv); TSC IV, depression and apathy (Depr); and TSC VII, tension, worry, and fear (Tens).
As described in the literature review, a form of social adjustment demonstrated specifically by transsexuals is gender reorientation. In this study, the
instruments used to measure this criterion variable are the same as the social
feminization and physical feminization measures used by Blanchard et al.
(1983) with the following changes: The third item of the social feminization
variable (GR Soc) substitutes "in your name on your driver's license" for "in
your name on your documents"; and physical feminization (GR Phy) combines "hormones" and "vaginoplasty" into one variable.
To broaden the scope of the behavioral criterion, a variable measuring
work adjustment (Work Adj) was also used. This single item variable asks,
"Which of the following describes your current source of income or support
for your living expenses?" The response, "full-time work" was scored 2 points;
"part-time work," "savings," "retirement pension," and "student loans, grants,
or scholarships" were scored 1 point. All other sources, "welfare," "parent,"
"spouse" were scored 0.
Procedure

Participants were asked to complete a 154-item questionnaire containing
the questions designed to measure the predictor and criterion variables. They

DISCUSSION
The major finding of the present investigation was that the typological
variables androphilia and gynephilia, or erotic partner preference, do appear
to have a significant relationship to the social adjustment variables social
feminization and work adjustment. Thus, those who scored higher on the
Androphilia Scale manifest better adjustment in relation to gender conflict
by their comparative readiness to take on a feminine presentation or role.
With a larger sample, it is also likely that the tendency for androphiles to
be more physically feminized would become a significant association. On
the other hand, high scores on the Gynephilia Scale showed positive adjustment by their ability to generate their own income through some stable income
resource(s).
These systematic adjustment differences between the heterosexual and
homosexual types appear to have been noted over the years in various clinical
data (Person and Ovesey, 1974a, 1974b; Meyer, 1974; Roback et al., 1984).
The data suggest that homosexual transsexuals tend to have, or take on, more
femininity earlier in life and that the heterosexual types tend to be older and
have developed a more masculine career and life-style before they undertake
gender change. In their national survey, Roback et al. (1984) found these
same differences existed particularly between younger and older groups of
transsexuals. To explain these differences, they theorized that gender conflict
for the older, more heterosexual group, is less intensely experienced early
in life and becomes more intense only after traditional solutions have failed.
To amplify this hypothesis further, it is suggested that for the heterosexual transsexuals, the erotic component of cross-gender fetishism may provide an early outlet for the "intensity" of the cross-gender conflict. This
"outlet" of cross-dressing and experiencing erotic release may delay the need
for an early gender resolution by providing intermittent relief from gender
distress until this erotic component fades later in adult life. Without such

an outlet, the homosexual transsexual experiences an increasing need for conflict resolution and is motivated to take on feminization at an earlier age.
These behavioral differences in erotic genderal functioning between the two
transsexual types may also reflect differences in the causal mechanism underlying the syndrome.
One study, of course, cannot verify that the behavioral typology within
the two subgroups is unrelated to psychopathological vulnerability. However,
all the multiple regression analyses showed a low association between the
typological features and psychological disturbance variables, even though
clearly high and low profiles (high and low psychopathology) made up most
of the sample. In addition to the low association, 8 of the possible 12 regression coefficients (betas) between the typological variables and the psychological variables were also negative. The exceptions were the association of
feminine gender identity in childhood (FGIS) with all the psychological variables and the relation of cross-gender fetishism with introversion. These associations were low and positive. Given the evidence on differential adjustment
between the heterosexual and homosexual subgroup presented above, one
might suspect that these differences might leave one group more vulnerable
than the other. However, results in this study suggest little association between typology and psychopathology or that differences in adjustment style
necessarily create psychological vulnerability.
Age of onset, proposed as a fifth variable for the typological study,
did not perform exactly as theorized. Contrary to expectation, the homosexual
subgroup (androphiles) did not report significantly lower ages for transsexual
onset. Instead, lower reported age of onset appears to vary as a function
of the number of recalled feminine behaviors in childhood. Since the heterosexual subgroup (gynephiles) were most likely to recall fewer of these childhood behaviors, it might be expected that they would also report older ages
for onset. This logic, however, was not supported by this study. Though it
was found that gynephiles are most likely to score lower on the FGIS variable,
they do not systematically report older ages of onset, nor do the androphiles
always report younger ones. This finding is also supported by Roback et al.'s
(1984) survey which found no differences between their groups in reported
age of onset. These data suggest, then, that it is likely that the syndrome
manifests itself in both groups around the same time, though subsequently
a different course is taken due to differences in erotic-genderal functioning.

L i m i t a t i o n s and R e c o m m e n d a t i o n s

Clearly, the most limiting factor in this study is the sample size. Though
most of the intercorrelations discussed are significant, they are of a lower
magnitude than would optimally be preferred and thus are unstable. Results,

Male Transsexual Typology and Psychosocial Adjustment

359

then, must be accepted cautiously and validated by further studies with a
larger sample. Since this is also one of the few systematic investigations undertaken with a population sample it is also possible that some variables may
not perform in the same way that they might in a more restricted one.
Finally, since the typological variables under study account, at most,
for less than 50% of the variance in any one of the dependent variables, it
is suggested that further studies include variables outside the typological
domain as predictors. Variables that measures the quality of family life and
interactions among family members and partners should be of particular importance to include, as the object relations or experiential domain is proposed
as one area that may causally contribute to transsexualism.
